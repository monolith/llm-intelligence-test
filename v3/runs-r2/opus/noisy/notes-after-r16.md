## 0. THE CASE IN ONE PARAGRAPH (established by r04/r07/r11, disputed by r01/r05/r08/r12/r14/r15/r16)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association ran a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up
the valley from its creamery at Larrow Green. For about a quarter century the milk delivered at that station
tested lower in butterfat than milk delivered elsewhere, and the patrons were paid less accordingly. Three
explanations circulated: the land (wet/bog hay thinning the cream), the patrons (watering their cans), and
the tester (Ansel Keddie stealing cream). All three were wrong. The station's milk-test pipettes delivered
16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 c.c. mark — 95 percent, one part in twenty short — so every test read 95 percent
of the truth. The shortfall (76,000 lb of butterfat, ~$19,000) was entered year by year in the Association's
own ledgers as "station gain." The glass was condemned by the state inspector in June 1923; Keddie was
dismissed in 1925; the cause was found and published in 1958, twenty-four years after his death.

## 1. PEOPLE AND RELATIONSHIPS

**The Vose family (Association leadership)**
- **Gideon Vose** — founder; elected first president of the Association at the organizing meeting, 1889;
  held the office until 1911 = twenty-two years, longer than any other man (r02). Drafted the by-laws
  himself; not a lawyer; "they read as a farmer wrote them, which is to their credit" (r02). Died 1917 (r02),
  so could not be asked about Article VII afterward. Called "old Gideon" by Vira Toland (r01), who says the
  family "always thought the valley belonged to them a little." r12 adds: "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who
  had once thought of the law and thought better of it," who wrote the by-laws "at his own kitchen table, in
  a hand that the Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later."
- **Selby Vose** — Gideon's son (r05, r08: "My father, who was your first president"). Vira Toland calls him
  "old Gideon Vose's nephew" (r01); **r12 also says nephew** ("His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager…
  used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last").
  **CONFLICT: son (r05/r08) vs nephew (r01/r12).** Made manager in 1913, served until 1938 (r02); in his 1926
  address says "It is thirteen years this spring, since 1913" (r08). Signed the 1919 circular about water in
  the cans. Signed the annual station sheet every year (r05). Told Teague in 1916 to let the station-gain
  question alone (r05). Kept his father's Jersey herd at the home place (r08). Put the water explanation to
  the 1925 board "without heat and without proof" (r13).
- **Old Mrs. Vose** — Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were **first cousins**; "everybody knew that" (r01). Vira's
  point: the 1919 letter was not just the Association speaking, it was "somebody's cousin" speaking.

**The Keddie family**
- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station. Went up in **1895**, the year before the
  station opened, to get it ready; also tested that first summer (r01). Kept the test book in his own hand
  from 4 May 1896. Twenty-nine seasons, 1896 through 1924; tested not a pound in 1925 (r03). Dismissed
  30 April 1925 (r03); "they put Ansel Keddie out" (r01). Died in the 1930s (r01: "He died in the thirties");
  **died 1934** (r04: "He died in 1934 and had been dead twenty-four years" as of 1958). Described by Strawn
  (r06, 1907) as working "slow and clean," his hand "better than most." Frick (r13) believed him then and
  in 1946: "His denial was accurate and it did him no good at all."
- **Orra Keddie** — Ansel's wife; **Emmet Brant's sister** (r01, r03); first cousin to old Mrs. Vose (r01).
  Outlived Ansel "a good while" and bore the valley's suspicion the whole time (r01). After 1919 she would
  walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane (r01). **r09 (Roy Brant) insists: "Orra
  Keddie was my father's sister. They were full brother and sister, same mother and same father, whatever
  you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it."**
- **Duncan Keddie** — Ansel's son; taught school at Ashlin (r04). r09: Duncan is Ansel and Orra's boy, and
  Roy Brant's **first cousin**.
- **Ivy Keddie** — Duncan's daughter, Ansel's granddaughter; of the State Agricultural College at Farland
  (r07 Item 5). Found the crate February 1958, measured the pipettes 9 March 1958, gave the talk
  "What the Crate Held" to the Ordell County Historical Society at Ashlin in 1959 (r04). Co-wrote the closing
  of Bulletin No. 471 with Professor Vaught (r04). **Second cousin to Hazel Brant** (r09, and r12's 1959
  footnote agrees). Wrote to Rosalie Cudd in 1958 asking, in four lines, what four Ordell County farmers'
  milk tested over the ridge; Rosalie: "here is a young woman from the college who asks one in four lines
  and apologizes for troubling me" (r10). Borrowed Emmet Brant's weigh book from Roy in 1958; Roy: "She is
  careful with paper. It comes back to me when she is through, and after me it is Hazel's" (r09).

**The Brant family (Hessel Bottom)**
- **Emmet Brant** — "the biggest man on the Bottom," most cows, most hay ground, hauled more milk to the
  station than anybody (r01); the station's largest patron in every year of record (r02). Orra Keddie's
  brother; the man the 1919 letter cost most. Before 1919 the two families ate Sunday dinner together; after
  1919 they never spoke again — "not at the store, not at a funeral" (r01, r03: "That is the plainest fact in
  this letter and it is the one I find I have the least to say about"). **r09: born 1854, died 1929.** Kept the
  weigh book from **1 January 1898** until the week before he died (r09) — note r07 Item 4 and r13 Exhibit 3
  both say **1 January 1897**. One of the first men to complain out loud in 1902, and was laughed at (r09).
  Read the 1908 committee report twice, put it in the drawer, and said "that the land had been wet since the
  flood and the tests had not" (r09). Never forgave the 1919 circular — but, per Roy, "not from any quarrel
  over the water, but because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in
  front of that circular and did not."
- **Roy Brant** — Emmet's son; **narrator of r09**; sixty-seven in 1955 (so born ~1888) and still milking;
  kept the Hessel Bottom weigh book up himself after his father died, in the same book until it was filled
  and then in another; lent it to Ivy Keddie (r04, r09). Per r07 Item 4 provenance: book passed "to Roy Brant
  in 1929." Did **not** haul over the ridge: "We had the biggest herd on the meadow, and the biggest herd is
  the one that cannot afford to be talked about, and I have thought since that this was a poor reason and cost
  us money." Their farm: sixty-odd acres of the meadow, thirty-eight cows in a good year.
- **Hazel Brant** — Roy's daughter; the 1958 college researcher was "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed"
  per Vira (r01); per r07 Item 4 the weigh book went "to Miss Hazel Brant in 1962"; r09 (1955): "It goes to
  Hazel when I am done with it. That is all the arrangement I have made and all I intend to make."
  **r09 and r12's footnote both make Hazel and Ivy second cousins** (r01's "first cousin once removed" is the
  odd one out). Roy notes both women "have had to have it explained to them by me more than once."

**Association officers and staff**
- **Effie Loomis** — secretary 1901–1940, kept the minute book in her own hand throughout (r02, r05). Born
  ~1875 ("I was fourteen years old in 1889"). Wrote "Notes toward a history of the Association" in 1941, the
  year after laying down the minute book. Recorded the 1925 award herself on the last day.
- **Wilbur Teague** — bookkeeper. r02 says he "came in as bookkeeper in 1905"; r05 says he kept the books
  "from 1905 to 1945, forty years," and wrote his memoir chapter at Ashlin in the winter of 1948,
  "three years after I gave up the desk." Invented the station-gain ledger line. At the 1925 hearing he put
  the cream-stealing theory obliquely (r13).
- **Hobart Sill** — buttermaker at Larrow Green; kept the churn book and the cream receiving book (r02).
  **Narrator of r14.** Came to the creamery in **1893**; forty-one years at the churn; sixty-four years old
  when he left in **1934**; "they gave him a chair." Lent his own glassware to open the station in 1896.
- **Alonzo Frick** — attorney of Ashlin; chaired the 1925 board of arbitration (r02, r05, r08, r12).
  **Narrator of r13**, writing at age seventy-four in 1946 (so born ~1872); forty years trying titles and
  boundaries, and two homicides. Teague: "as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had." Vose: "an able and
  impartial man." Frick supposed he was named chairman "because I was the one of the three who kept no cow."
- **Nyle Grigg** — of Larrow Green; **chairman of the 1908 investigating committee** (named in r09, r12, r15).
  **Narrator of r15** (the 1919 covering letter). Not a patron of the Ostrey Hollow station: "I haul to Larrow
  Green and always have." Had known the men of the Bottom since he was a young man and had bought hay off two
  of them. "I have been abused for the finding by men on both sides of it, which I take to be a fair sign that
  the finding was an honest one."

**Others**
- **Vira Toland** — narrator of r01. Born 1912 at the top end of Ostrey Hollow, in the house her father's
  father built; a Hessel Bottom patron's daughter. Lived there her whole life except two years at Ashlin when
  first married. Aged 7 in 1919. Taped 1979 at her kitchen table by "a young man from the county who asked
  three questions in an hour." Has a brother who she believes still has the 1919 letter.
- **Vira's father** — hauled milk to the station; one of the nine families who got the 1919 letter; read it at
  the table with his hat still on, which he never did; read it twice; put it in the desk drawer in the front
  room, where it stayed until he died. Said Ansel Keddie was "an honest man who had got into something."
  Also said that if the world kept accounts like Keddie's "there would be no lawyers in Ashlin."
- **Vira's mother** — stood at the stove with her back turned the whole time the letter was read.
- **Merle Strawn** — state dairy inspector, Ordell circuit. Took the circuit in **1907** and laid it down in
  **1936**; twenty-eight pocket books, one to the year, oilcloth covers. Copied extracts in spring 1936 at the
  request of the state dairy office. (Ivy in r04 says his notebooks were "kept... from 1908" — CONFLICT.)
  r09 credits him with leaving his own measure at the station in June 1911 (see §7 conflicts).
- **Halbert Nym** — curator, Ordell County Historical Rooms; wrote the 1972 accession notes (r07), items 1–16.
- **Rosalie Cudd** — runs the Alder Corners Cheese Company over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County.
  **r07 Item 13 calls her Jerome Cudd's niece; r10 (herself) and r12 both make her his DAUGHTER.**
  Copied out that factory's test figures for Ivy in 1958 (Ivy never saw the book itself — r04, r10).
  Was twelve years old when the factory's first vat was set; "drying hoops before I was tall enough to see
  into one"; took the factory over in spring 1941 and had had it seventeen years by 1958; "I have changed the
  boiler and the roof and nothing else."
- **Jerome Cudd** — founder of the Alder Corners factory. **r10: built it in 1913**, "on the flat below the
  church, because there was spring water on the lot and a hard road out to the east"; **died March 1941,
  "between the setting and the cutting of a vat, which he would have arranged that way if he had been asked."**
  r07 Item 13: the book "passed from its founder to his daughter in 1941 and to the Vessey County Historical
  Society in 1970." **r09 and r12 both say the factory opened in 1914** — CONFLICT with r10's 1913.
- **Professor Leland Vaught** — of the State Agricultural College at Farland; professor of dairy husbandry;
  **narrator of r11**; wording of Bulletin No. 471's closing paragraph is "Professor Vaught's wording and mine
  together" (r04). Signs the bulletin "— L. VAUGHT."
- **Dell Rundle** — schoolteacher at Larrow Green; **narrator of r12**; taught the school "a good many years";
  compiled the 1939 pamphlet "from the recollections of old members and two winters of asking questions at the
  store"; too old to rewrite it in 1959 ("I am too old to rewrite a pamphlet and I refer the reader to the
  college. — D.R.").
- **Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S.** — veterinary surgeon of Ashlin, thirty years' practice; **narrator of r16**
  (paper read to the Ordell County Farm Club, 1926). "I have never in my life read a test and would not know
  how."
- **Alder Corners / Vessey County, Redlow Ridge** — the cheese factory over the ridge whose independent tests
  of Hessel Bottom milk ran higher.

## 2. CHRONOLOGY (everything dated across all sixteen retellings)

- **1854** — Emmet Brant born (r09).
- **1889** — Farmers of the Larrow Valley meet at Larrow Green, organize the Larrow Valley Cooperative
  Creamery Association. Gideon Vose elected first president. Effie Loomis is 14. r12: they met in the
  schoolhouse, in cold weather; it went on very late and somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it;
  they were tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking what the merchant allowed them.
- **1889–1892** — Minute book of these years later lost (see 1938).
- **1891** — Main creamery built at Larrow Green, on Larrow Creek (r02: "it is the same building yet," 1941);
  r12: "on the creek below the mill," and the churn set up in it; the valley began to be paid in cash once a
  month. **r12 dates Article VII to 1891** ("Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up") —
  conflicting with r02's 1892 and r15's 1893 (see §7).
- **1892** — Article VII adopted at the annual meeting, the year after the creamery opened (r02).
- **1893** — Hobart Sill comes to the Larrow Green creamery ("Eighteen ninety-three I came here, and there was
  no floor in this room but plank, and we cooled with creek ice out of the pond") (r14). **r15 (Grigg): "Payment
  by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have always understood it."**
- **1895** — Ansel Keddie goes up to the station "the year before they opened it, to get it ready" (r01).
- **1896** — Station opens (May; r14: "that was ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd went up for it").
  Test book begun **4 May 1896**. First-season average test **3.80**. Vose (r08) says the station has been
  "in daily use since 1896." r14: first season (May to the end of the year) took in **about nine hundred
  thousand pounds** of milk — "That is a good deal of milk for a hill station starting from nothing."
- **1896–97** — Glass borrowed until the station's own outfit arrives. **r14 (Sill) claims it was HIS OWN
  glass**, made up at his own bench at Larrow Green and sent up the valley on the wagon, because the station's
  own outfit had been ordered and not come and there was talk of holding off the whole season. Returned to him
  the spring after with one piece chipped, "and I never said a word about that either."
- **14 April 1897** — Ammon & Sons invoice (see §4). Six dozen pipettes at .35, etc., total **$77.93**.
- **1 May 1897** — The station's own new outfit in use (r07 Item 2; Bulletin 471 dates the error's start to
  "the first of May, 1897"). **r11's body text says "the first of May, 1898" — conflicting with its own
  reproduced conclusion.**
- **1897** — Test book records "part year" for 1897; the Brant/Hessel Bottom weigh book begun 1 January 1897
  (r07 Item 4, r13 Exhibit 3); the Association ledgers' station column begins with 1897.
- **1 January 1898** — per r09, the date Emmet Brant began his weigh book (conflicts with 1897 above).
- **1898** — Average test drops to **3.61** and stays there (or within three hundredths) every year after.
  First ledger station-gain entry quoted: 57,760 lb credited / 60,800 lb received / 3,040 lb gain.
- **1901** — Effie Loomis becomes secretary.
- **1902** — "The complaints began in 1902, when it was first said down at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow
  milk did not test with the rest" (r03). r09: the first men to say so out loud said it in 1902 and were
  laughed at, "and one of them was my father." r12: "From 1902 the men of the Hollow complained… They were
  not believed." r14 (Sill): "Nineteen two is the first I remember of it, a man in here shouting that his
  milk tested under his brother-in-law's down the creek and it was the same feed and the same water and what
  did we mean by it." r15: "From about 1902 there had been grumbling out of Ostrey Hollow."
- **1905** — Wilbur Teague becomes bookkeeper; invents the station-gain line.
- **1907** — Merle Strawn takes the Ordell circuit; first-round visit to Ostrey Hollow. Scales true, cold room
  fair, tester's book in his own hand, watched two tests.
- **January 1908** — The investigating committee is appointed ("Your committee, appointed in January last" —
  r15's reprinted report; r15's letter: "By the winter in which we were named…").
- **1908** — Investigating committee (chairman Nyle Grigg) comes up and samples the herds of Hessel Bottom
  **through January, February and March 1908 only** (the report's own words); reports the wet hay of the
  Bottom thins the cream and "the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." **r15's covering letter
  contradicts the report: "We sampled those herds through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year, in
  the barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's, and we did not let a man know which
  day we were coming."**
- **1909** — Strawn at Ostrey Hollow in the fall. Scales true; boiler in order, safety tried; one man cautioned
  about a rusted seam. Notes the station takes from the wet bottom farms and three places on the hill.
- **1910** — Hessel Bottom weigh-book leaf: 200,000 lb even delivered, "by my own scale and theirs agreeing,"
  paid on a test of **3.42**. Selby Vose's Jersey herd at the home place tests **4.56** (r08). r09 adds:
  **the price of butterfat that year was twenty-four cents**, and Emmet "was pleased about the evenness of it;
  he wrote 'even' and underlined it, which for him was carrying on."
- **1911** — Gideon Vose's presidency ends (22 years).
- **8 May 1911** — "Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." Tests immediately rise to 3.80.
- **June 1911** — Strawn visits Ostrey Hollow on his way across the county: "Scales true. Watched a test
  through. Nothing here to note." (He watched a test during the borrowed-measure window and saw nothing odd.)
  **r09 claims: "The state man left his own measure that June and that is what they used."**
- **10 July 1911** — "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."
  Tests fall to 3.61 the same day. Nine weeks total.
- **1911** — State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 published on bog hay (see §4).
- **1912** — Vira Toland born. Strawn at Ostrey Hollow: scales true, lactometer no added water, cream shipped
  down the same day it is separated; creek out over the flat road, he went round by the ridge.
- **1913** — Selby Vose made manager. Strawn spends two days at Larrow Green creamery, churn room and
  receiving books; "New manager at the Association this year and he came out with me to the platform, which
  the last one never did." **r10: Jerome Cudd builds the Alder Corners factory this year.**
- **1914** — **r09 and r12: the Alder Corners Cheese Company opens over Redlow Ridge** (conflicting with r10).
- **1915** — Strawn at Ostrey Hollow and the county-line plants. Wash room floor holding water at the drain;
  he ordered it graded ("A field man who lets a floor go will be shown a floor at every plant on his circuit
  inside of two years"). **From 1915, four Hessel Bottom men haul to Alder Corners in the summer flush**
  (r09, r10) — about 1 June to mid-September, back to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year.
- **1916** — Strawn at Ostrey Hollow: scales true, no added water; "The tester complains that his patrons blame
  him for their figures. I told him that is the trade and he said he knew it." Same year, Teague puts the
  station-gain question to Selby Vose and is told to let it alone.
- **1917** — Gideon Vose dies. Strawn at a cheese factory in the next county, then Larrow Green; "A bad year
  for help... two testers doing the work of three men, which is where mistakes live."
- **1919** — The circular. A printed letter over the manager's (Selby Vose's) signature goes to **nine families**
  of Ostrey Hollow saying there is water in their cans. Vira is 7; it rained that day. Strawn's 1919 entry:
  scales true, lactometer no added water, and "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have
  made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so." **Same year: Nyle Grigg's covering letter (r15)
  reprints the 1908 report because "the Ostrey Hollow question [has been] reopened this season by men who were
  boys when we made it."** Jerome Cudd's book records the four Hessel Bottom men's summer averages: 3.79, 3.81,
  3.78, 3.82 (r10).
- **1920** — Strawn at Ostrey Hollow twice, spring and fall. Scales true both times; cold room short of ice in
  the fall, seen to before he was out of the county.
- **1921** — The state begins issuing **burettes** to field men, graduated, in a wooden case, for checking the
  delivery of measures and pipettes. Strawn's came in the spring with a printed sheet of directions. "It is a
  slow business... most plants have never had it done."
- **1922** — Keddie makes up the summary leaf at the back of his book. Strawn visits: scales true, no added
  water, boiler in order — **but did not run their glass against the burette**, "the day being short and the
  road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened."
- **12 June 1923** — Strawn runs the station's milk pipettes against the burette, finds they will not deliver
  their mark, **condemns the whole crate**, tells the manager to order new. (Vose in 1926 misdates this to
  **12 July 1923**; **r12 repeats "the twelfth of July, 1923."**)
- **June 1923** — The condemned crate is carried up to the loft over the intake room (r04).
- **28 June 1923** — The Association orders replacement pipettes and bottles from Ammon & Sons.
- **2 July 1923** — Ammon & Sons letter acknowledging the order, promising shipment that month, and
  "declining to say anything of the earlier lot."
- **July 1923** — New glass in use.
- **October 1923** — Strawn returns: "New glass in use since July. Ran the new pipettes against the burette.
  They deliver their mark. Scales true."
- **1924** — First full year on true glass. Strawn: "Scales true, no added water, tests improved. Their book
  gives the year at **3.80**." Keddie: "In 1924 the average of this station was 3.80." (Vose claims **3.90**
  and a rise of twenty-one hundredths — CONFLICT.) Test book runs to **31 December 1924** (r13 Exhibit 1).
- **January 1925** — The Ostrey Hollow patrons petition the Association ("in the winter, when a farmer has
  time to be angry" — r05).
- **17 March 1925** — Board of arbitration opens at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick. Keddie gives his nine-sentence
  statement standing. The station test book is impounded by the board this month. (r13: this was the **first**
  of three sessions; the second and third were held at Ostrey Hollow in the station's own weigh room, at
  Frick's request.)
- **30 April 1925** — Keddie put out — **four days before the award** (r13).
- **4 May 1925** — The board's last day; award of $3,800 recorded by Effie Loomis.
- **Spring 1925** — Keddie writes the letter at Ostrey Hollow. Never sent; found among his papers.
- **1925** — Keddie tests not a pound. Strawn in the fall: "New man at the tester's bench this year. He is
  quick, and quick is not the thing I look for."
- **1926** — Selby Vose's annual address to the membership (r08). **Dr. Kepp reads his bog-meadow paper to the
  Ordell County Farm Club (r16).** The state's burette program discontinued; instruments kept by the men who
  have them, no more issued, no returns wanted. Strawn's stays in its case in his grip and "has been used
  twice since."
- **1928** — Strawn: "The station is quieter than it was. The loft over the intake room is full of odds and
  ends and I told them a loft over a milk room is a place for nothing but air, and was told in return that it
  had been full since before the war."
- **1929** — **Emmet Brant dies** (r09; he wrote in the book until the week before). Hessel Bottom weigh book
  passes to Roy Brant.
- **1930** — **Last summer the four Hessel Bottom men hauled to Alder Corners** (r10); sixteen seasons,
  1915–1930 inclusive.
- **1931** — Strawn at Larrow Green and Ostrey Hollow in the same week; both in order; bad ice year, every
  plant in the four counties short, two buying ice from the railroad.
- **1934** — Ansel Keddie dies. Strawn: scales true, no added water; a separator bearing run hot and shut down
  two days, since repaired; "The tester's book is kept up, though not in the hand it used to be kept in."
  **Hobart Sill leaves the creamery after forty-one years; his remarks are taken down that day (r14).**
- **March 1936** — Strawn's last round: Ostrey Hollow, Larrow Green, and two factories over the county line.
  "Scales true at all four. Nothing to note anywhere and nothing left to say about it." Notebooks go to the
  state dairy office on retirement.
- **1938** — Office move; the 1889–1892 minute book is lost. Selby Vose's management ends.
- **1939** — **Dell Rundle's jubilee pamphlet "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years" printed at Larrow
  Green** (r12).
- **1940** — Effie Loomis lays down the minute book.
- **1941** — Effie writes "Notes toward a history." **Jerome Cudd dies in March; Rosalie takes over the factory
  that same spring** (r10). The Alder Corners test book passes from its founder to his daughter (r07 Item 13).

## 3. THE MECHANISM (Ivy Keddie's arithmetic, r04, corroborated r07; explained at length in r11)

- A butterfat test draws a **measured charge** of milk into a bottle whose neck is **ruled to read percent for
  that charge and no other**. A short charge gives a short reading in exact proportion.
- Pipettes blown at **17.6 c.c.**; measured mean delivery **16.72 c.c.**; short **0.88 c.c.**
- 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly**. Every test at that station read **95 percent of the truth**.
- 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Recorded average 3.61; true average 3.80; gap **0.19 of a point**.
- **The gap was proportional, not fixed.** 0.19 is five percent of 3.80. "The station did not lose a set
  number of points. It lost a twentieth of whatever was there."
- Consequence: **the error fell hardest on the best milk.** A herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 of a point;
  a herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18. "The richer the milk, the more was taken from it, and no man had any
  way of knowing that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones."
- 25 full delivery years, 1898–1922: **40,000,000 lb of milk**, averaging **1,600,000 lb/year**.
  - At recorded 3.61%: **1,444,000 lb** of fat credited.
  - At true 3.80%: **1,520,000 lb** of fat delivered.
  - Difference: **76,000 lb of butterfat**.
- Butterfat averaged **25 cents a pound** over the period → **$19,000** unpaid.
- The 1925 award of **$3,800 is exactly one fifth** of $19,000.
- **The two-percentages point (crucial, made in r04 and repeated in r07 Item 6):** 76,000 lb is **5 percent of
  the fat actually delivered** (1,520,000) and **5.25 percent of the fat credited** (1,444,000). "Neither figure
  is an error"; the Association's ledger line uses the second, "because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a
  surplus against the figure he has already entered." But **five percent is the one that is also the glass.**
  Nym's version: "each is right of its own denominator and of no other."

**Vaught's exposition of the instrument (r11 §II) — the clearest statement of why it went unseen:**
- The test has four operations: a measured charge of milk drawn into a pipette and delivered into a test
  bottle; sulfuric acid added, dissolving everything but the fat; the bottle spun in a hand tester so the
  liberated fat rises into the graduated neck; the operator reads the fat off **directly**, in percent.
- "The operator weighs nothing. He reads the length of a column of fat in a narrow neck and calls it a
  percentage, and he is entitled to do so for one reason only: the charge of milk that went into that bottle
  is a fixed and known quantity. The maker rules the neck for that quantity and no other."
- The standard charge in the state and on the glassware examined is **17.6 cubic centimeters**.
- The extension worker's one takeaway: **"the milk pipette is the one instrument in the butterfat test which
  the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the instrument on which every figure in the plant
  depends."**
- **Three consequences "nobody appears to have held all three in mind at once":**
  1. **The error is proportional and not additive** — it removes a fixed *fraction*, not a fixed number of
     points. "Rich milk therefore loses more than poor milk, absolutely, and the ratio between any two tests
     made on the same short glass is undisturbed. A station using such glass will show a perfectly orderly,
     internally consistent set of figures. Its herds will rank correctly against one another. Only against the
     outside world is it wrong."
  2. **The error is precise** — "A leaking pipe or a slipping scale gives a scattered error, and scatter
     attracts attention. A pipette that delivers 16.27 c.c. where it is blown 17.6 delivers 16.27 c.c. every
     time, for a quarter of a century, in January and in July, for a careful operator and a careless one. It
     has the appearance of excellent work."
  3. **The error is invisible from inside the plant** — scales can be checked with test weights, the tester's
     speed counted, the acid checked for strength, the bottle's graduation checked with mercury, "But the
     pipette is not checked against anything, because in the ordinary practice of the trade *the pipette is
     the standard*. It is what the plant measures with. An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be
     caught by measuring with it. It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes
     reached the state's field men very late in this history."
- Vaught's framing of the whole affair (r11 §I): the question "has been answered at various times by reference
  to the land and by reference to the men. It is answered here by reference to a piece of glass… Nobody in that
  valley lacked the intelligence to find it. What they lacked was any occasion to suspect the one article in
  the plant that nobody in the trade is in the habit of suspecting."
- **Vaught's repeatable method (r11 §III):** "The pipette is filled to its mark with water at room temperature,
  the tip is wiped, and the contents are delivered in the ordinary working manner — the same interval of
  drainage, the same touch of the tip to the wall of the receiver — into a burette of certified graduation.
  Five deliveries are made from each pipette and the readings averaged. The whole of it occupies an afternoon
  and requires no apparatus a creamery does not own, save the burette. There is no reason why every station in
  this state should not be checked once in this way, and several reasons in this history why it should be."

**The 1911 natural experiment (r04's decisive evidence; r11 §IV calls it "worth more than the arithmetic"):**
for nine weeks the station's own measure was broken and a substitute used. Same patrons, same herds, same
pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man at the crank — **one object changed** — and the
average went to 3.80 and returned to 3.61 the day the station's own measure came back. Ivy: "No explanation of
this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you."
Vaught: "**One variable changed.**… This is recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who
did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it. I have spent my working life urging
students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed
trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years. It disposes of every explanation that looks
to the herds, to the ground or to the men, for the sufficient reason that none of those was altered in those
nine weeks, and the figures were." (Roy Brant, r09, independently confirms the rise was general: "Through
those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back
into use and the figures went down again to where they had been. My father noticed the rise and he noticed
the fall and he entered both, and he could make nothing of it, and I could make nothing of it either, and
there it sat for near on fifty years.")

**Why the wet-hay theory fails (r04):** Bulletin No. 214 found bog hay as the whole roughage depressed the
test by **not more than 0.05 in winter and by nothing measurable on summer pasture**. Against a gap of 0.19
standing flat in every month of every year: "It is four times too small and it is the wrong shape."
Corroboration: at Alder Corners, over the ridge, the same Hessel Bottom milk ran **about 0.19 higher** than at
home, over fourteen overlapping seasons (per Ivy). Vaught (r11 §VI) puts it: "Five hundredths in winter;
nothing at all in summer. The discrepancy under examination is present in every month of the year and is some
six times the largest effect the trial could produce under conditions harsher than any farmer would impose."
(Note: "six times" fits Vaught's own inflated 0.295 gap, not the 0.19; against 0.19 it is roughly four times.)

**Keddie's own evidence that it was not seasonal (r03):** "They did not come and go with the season. The gap
was there in July as it was there in February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry
hay, and it was the same in a wet year as in a dry one." **Roy Brant says the same of his father's book (r09):
"it was seventeen hundredths in July and it was seventeen hundredths in February. It did not come and go with
the season. It did not care whether the cows were on the meadow or on hay in the barn. Nobody would take
account of that, then or since, though it was in front of them."**

**Hobart Sill's unrecognized corroboration (r14) — arguably the strongest contemporaneous evidence anybody
had, and nobody used it:** the cream coming down from Ostrey Hollow **always ran heavier in fat than the
office sheet said had been bought at the station**. "The office would send down its sheet and the sheet would
say so many pounds of fat bought at the station, and the fat that came into this room off that wagon was more
than the sheet said. Always more. Never less." He weighed it himself: "The platform man weighed it and I
weighed it over again when it came into the cold room, because I have never in my life taken another man's
figure for what was going into my churn." He dismissed it as an artifact of cream's nature — "Cream is not
milk and it will not stand still and be counted… So it ran heavy. So does everybody's. I never made anything
of it, and I will say plain, nobody ever asked me to." This is exactly the station-gain phenomenon seen from
the receiving end. Dell Rundle (r12) reasons from the same fact to the right conclusion about theft:
"**Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and
I cannot make my paragraph stand.**"

> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of

clause, because the valley has had fifty years to learn what it means… Gideon Vose put that in for convenience

## 5. PLACES AND PHYSICAL DETAIL

- **Ostrey Hollow** — the hamlet and valley head; the station stood "at the very top of the Hollow where the
  road bends and then goes to nothing but the ridge" (r01), seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green
  (r02, r05, r08, r12, r13, r14). Low building, whitewashed, "cold as a spring house inside even in August,"
  smelling of milk and steam and hot iron; you could hear the engine before the last turn (r01).
  r12 adds: "a plain one, boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and a loft over it that
  I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up; and behind it the creek, which in a wet April
  comes up to the sill and has twice come over it." r12 on why it was built: "in the nineties the pull down
  was three hours in mud and could not be done at all in March."
- **Hessel Bottom** — the wet meadow below the hamlet; "the best of that milk came off Hessel Bottom" (r02).
  **Seven farms had rights on Hessel Bottom** per r02; **six farms** per r09, r15 (the 1908 report), and r16 —
  see §7. The largest was Emmet Brant's (r02, r09). Strawn (1909): the station takes from the farms of the wet
  bottom and from **three places on the hill**. **r09: "a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under
  the hill at Ostrey Hollow"**; the Brants had sixty-odd acres of it and thirty-eight cows in a good year;
  "The water stands on the low end into May, and there is a soft place by the alders that has taken a horse to
  the hock in my time and my father's. The hay off it is coarse, and it cuts late, and a cow will eat it and
  do well enough, whatever has been said about that hay by men who never fed it… it never failed to grow
  something." r16 (Kepp): "the completest example of wet meadow in this county… the springs come out at the
  foot of the slope and run in it the year round; a part of it is under water every spring and is not fit to
  be walked on until well into May. Its hay is sedge hay, and on those farms it is the winter dependence and
  very nearly the whole of it."
- **Larrow Green** — creamery site on Larrow Creek, built 1891 (r12: "on the creek below the mill").
- **Ashlin** — the Association office (one room over a harness shop, cold at the north end, never cold enough
  at the south; ledgers on a shelf built by a taller predecessor; Teague's sloped desk with a brass rail
  across the foot to keep sheets from sliding). Also the seat of the 1925 arbitration's first session and of
  the Ordell County Historical Rooms. Vira lived there two years when first married. Duncan Keddie taught
  school there; Dr. Kepp practiced there. Before 1889 the valley hauled its butter to Ashlin to sell.
- **Ordell County** — the county; Vessey County is over Redlow Ridge (Alder Corners). **Tarnet** — where
  Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply is. **Farland** — the State Agricultural College.
- **Redlow Ridge** (r10, from the Vessey side): "Four miles of switchback, cut for a lumber road and never much
  improved since. It shuts in November and it does not open before April, some years May, and there is no
  arguing with it — the drifts sit in the cut below the top and stay there after everything else is bare. So
  no man from your valley ever hauled to me in winter, whatever the price was. It was not possible."
- **Alder Corners Cheese Company** — on the flat below the church, built where there was spring water on the
  lot and a hard road out to the east; Jerome Cudd tested himself, on his own bench, in the little room off
  the intake, with his own glassware bought new, washed in the evening, kept in a rack he had made, and wrote
  his own book (r10).
- **The station loft** — over the intake room, reached by a ladder; the crate was under a tarpaulin at the
  back of it (r04). Strawn complained about the loft's clutter in 1928; r12 says it "has never been cleared
  out since it was put up."
- **The crate** — deal, stenciled on the end **AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET**, much stained; the pipettes
  packed in straw; nine survived unbroken, two chipped at the tip (r04, r07 Item 5). Nym's note: "Item 5
  wants a case with a lock."
- **Keddie's slope desk by the window** at the station, where he wrote standing up in the light (r01).
- **The old station building** was shut by the late 1950s and used for storage — "they kept fence wire and salt
  in it" (r01); "used for storage since it closed" (r04).
- **The Larrow Green churn room in 1893** — "there was no floor in this room but plank, and we cooled with
  creek ice out of the pond" (r14).
- **Emmet Brant's book as an object (r09)** — "a ledger, a plain one, ruled by hand. Two columns for each day
  of delivery… The cover is gone off the front, the back is held by a strip of harness leather my father
  tacked on himself, and the first forty leaves are stained brown at one corner where it lay in a leaky window
  a winter he was sick." He wrote at the kitchen table after the second milking, in the lamp, with his hat
  still on, and Roy's mother would not have the table cleared till he was done.

## 6. HOW THE WORK WAS DONE

**Keddie's unsent letter, r03 — the fullest procedural account:**
- Day began at **half past five**. Wagons came up the Hollow in the dark most of the year; intake room open,
  lamps lit, scale swinging free before the first wagon reached the door.
- Every can weighed, the weight called and written before the milk went anywhere else. The scale was tested by
  the state's man on his rounds and was true. "I have never in twenty-nine seasons had a patron dispute a
  weight with me and be right."
- A sample taken from each patron's milk as it was weighed; samples into **his own jar, numbered to him and
  to nobody else**; the composite jar **made up over ten days** before testing.
- Testing morning: the jar warmed in water until **blood-warm right through**, then poured vessel to vessel
  and poured again until the risen cream was carried down through the whole of it. "A sample that is not
  warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy could tell you so."
- **The charge:** milk drawn up in the measure to the mark on the stem, the tip wiped, the charge blown out
  into the bottle carrying the patron's number.
- Acid poured down the side; the bottle turned until the milk was dark.
- **Four bottles in the hand tester**, weighted against each other, cover down, crank turned at the speed he
  was taught and no faster. "No man ever stood beside me and made me hurry it."
- The fat rises in the neck of the bottle as a clear column, read off in percent against the rulings **with a
  pair of dividers**, and written down.
- "It is not a hard trade. It is only a trade that must be done the same way every time, which is a harder
  thing than it sounds."
- **The book kept in five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat.** Errors crossed through with his initials
  against the crossing, never written over — "a figure written over another figure is a figure no man can
  swear to afterward." (Nym's Item 3 lists **four** columns: date, patron, pounds, test — a small conflict.)
- **Thirty-one tests a week for twenty-nine seasons** (from the statement).
- What the station did: took in the milk of the upper farms, separated it, sent the cream down to Larrow Green,
  gave the **skim back to the patron** for his hogs and calves (r02, r05, r12, r13).

**Vaught's four operations (r11 §II)** — see §3 above: measured charge into the bottle; acid; spin; direct
reading in percent off the ruled neck.

**Strawn's inspection routine, always the same four things in the same order** (r06): (1) the scales, against
his sealed weights — both intake scale and cream scale, loaded light and loaded heavy; (2) the **lactometer**
on the day's milk, patron by patron where there was any question, **for added water**; (3) the boiler, water
supply and cold room — pressure, gauge, the cased well, and whether the room would hold overnight what they
put in it; (4) standing at the tester's elbow to watch a test through from sample to reading, "and I did not
let him hurry it" — "the one most men grudged me." His circuit: **fifty-odd plants in four counties**, each to
be seen at least once a year, the doubtful ones oftener; by train where there was a train, by livery where
there was not, and in spring by neither.

**The 1908 committee's method (r15's report):** visited each of the six farms, some three and four times; drew
and sealed their own samples; watched the herds milked, the cans filled, the hauling and the weighing in at the
intake; stood by while the test was made; tried the station scales against a known weight and the patrons' own
scales against the station's; inquired into each barn's water supply. **They checked everything except the
pipette** — and had no burette with which to check it (the state issued none until 1921).

**The Hessel Bottom haul to Alder Corners (r10):** "They started before daylight to have the cans on my
platform by seven, because milk that has been four miles down a switchback in a wagon on a July morning is not
improving while it stands, and they came in with the horses steaming and the tarps wet with the cloud that
sits on the top of the ridge till the sun is well up. One of them brought his boy, who used to sit on my scale
platform and be weighed for a joke. That boy is a man in the state road department now and does not remember
me." Reasons for going, per Rosalie: "cheese paid better than butter in a flush month, I was buying hard in
flush because a cheese factory can use everything you can carry it, and while the road was open my gate was a
shorter pull for those four farms than the run down the valley." Per Roy (r09): "It was for the price and the
flush market and a shorter haul while the road was open, and it was talked about here as though they had gone
over to the enemy, which shows you what a valley is."

**The cream wagon (r14):** came down from Ostrey Hollow every day of the week but Sunday, seven miles of hill
road, "and in July it came down warm no matter how early they started. It was set off at the platform and it
was weighed and it was sampled and it went into the cold room, and I took it from there." Sill's own routine:
"Cans off, cans on the scale, cans into the ice, and the book ruled and signed before I put my coat on."
His hand is on every page of the receiving book from 1893.

## 7. CONFLICTS, CONTRADICTIONS AND ODDITIES (the heart of the exercise)

**A. The award figure**
- r01 (Vira): **$3,500**, "split up nine ways over all those years — my father's share of it did not buy a hay
  rake."
- r02, r04, r05, r07, r08, r13: **$3,800**. The documentary figure is $3,800; Vira's memory is off by $300.
- r12 does not give a number ("it made an award to the patrons without admitting anything").

**B. The date and occasion of the hearing**
- r01 (Vira): the hearing came "the winter and spring after" the 1919 letter, i.e. 1919–20, and Keddie was put
  out then; the tests rose "the summer after."
- r02/r03/r05/r07/r08/r12/r13: the petition was **January 1925**, the board sat **17 March – 4 May 1925**,
  Keddie was put out **30 April 1925**, and the tests rose in **1924** (before the hearing, after the new
  glass). Vira has telescoped 1919 and 1925 into one event and inverted the order of the rise and the hearing.

**C. Selby Vose's relation to Gideon Vose**
- r01 and **r12**: **nephew** (r12: "His nephew Selby Vose… his uncle wrote by-laws…").
- r05/r08: **son** ("son of the late president"; "My father, who was your first president").
- Two independent sources on each side; unresolved.

**D. When the station's glass error began, and where the first glass came from — now a THREE-way conflict**
- Keddie's book, first leaf: "Glass **borrowed of Larrow Green** until our own comes."
- Keddie's letter body (r03): "The glass I began on in ninety-six was borrowed off the **Ashlin creamery**, and
  we used it until our own outfit came up the valley in a crate the spring following." — **internal conflict in
  r03**; also note there is no Ashlin creamery elsewhere in the record (Ashlin is the office).
- **r14 (Sill): it was his own personal glassware, made up at his own bench at Larrow Green out of glass "that
  I had used and knew," sent up the valley on the wagon; returned to him the spring after with one piece
  chipped.** Sill's account is consistent with the test book's "borrowed of Larrow Green" and supplies the
  human detail behind it; it also means the borrowed 1896 glass was known-good working glass — which is why
  1896 read 3.80.
- The invoice is dated 14 April 1897 and the outfit was in use from 1 May 1897, which fits "the spring
  following" and explains why 1896 read 3.80 (borrowed, true glass) and 1898 onward read 3.61.
- **r11's body text says the station's own glass went into use on "the first of May, 1898"** — one year later
  than every other source and than its own reproduced conclusion.

**E. The date of the condemnation**
- Strawn's book and Bulletin 471: **12 June 1923**. Nym Item 5: 12 June 1923.
- Selby Vose (r08): "**the twelfth of July, 1923**." **r12 (Rundle) repeats "the twelfth of July, 1923."**
  A month's slip that conveniently puts the condemnation after, not before, the tests began to move; Rundle
  probably took it from Vose's address or from the valley's talk.

**F. The 1924 average and the size of the rise**
- Keddie (r03) and Strawn (r06): **3.80**; rise of **0.19** from 3.61.
- Vose (r08): the tests "rose by **twenty-one hundredths** and the average for 1924 stood at **3.90**."
  Vose's figures are both inflated, and they are the figures on which he rests his watering theory.
- **Kepp (r16) uses "twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21" as the gap "between the milk of that station and
  the milk of this valley at large," saying it was "given to me and… I have satisfied myself."** He has almost
  certainly picked up Vose's inflated number and repurposed it as the size of the gap.

**G. The station's total intake and its average test**
- r02 (Effie, from Teague's own annual sheets), r04 (Ivy), r07 (ledgers), **r11 (Vaught)**: **40,000,000 lb**
  over **25 years, 1898–1922**, averaging 1,600,000 lb/yr; average test **3.61**.
- r05 (Teague himself, writing in 1948 from memory): "that station took in **forty-four million** pounds of
  milk," and "the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow... was **3.16**."
  **3.16 is 3.61 with the digits transposed** — almost certainly a slip, but it is the bookkeeper making it.
- **r12 (Rundle) repeats "forty-four million pounds."**
- **r13 (Frick) repeats the transposed test: "The patrons of that station had been paid, year upon year, on an
  average test of 3.16… Three and sixteen is not a scandalous figure standing by itself."** Frick, like
  Rundle, is downstream of Teague. Teague's two errors have propagated into the county's bar-association
  yearbook and its jubilee pamphlet, i.e. into what look like independent records.

**H. The length of the 1911 interruption**
- r03 (the book itself: 8 May – 10 July), r04 and **r09** and **r11**: **nine weeks**.
- r05 (Teague): "**six weeks** before their own came up from town." **r16 (Kepp) also says six weeks**, twice,
  and builds his dismissal on it ("Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen").
- Also **where the substitute came from**: r04 says "The measure they borrowed in 1911 came up from **Larrow
  Green** with the cream wagon"; r05 says their own "came up from **town**"; **r09 (Roy Brant) says "The state
  man left his own measure that June"** — but Strawn's own 1911 entry mentions no such loan, and the substitute
  was already in use from 8 May, a month before Strawn's June visit, so Roy's version is chronologically
  impossible as stated. Keddie says only "I made shift with one borrowed."

**I. Where the crate was found**
- r04 (Ivy, who found it): in the **loft over the intake room at the station building**, under a tarpaulin,
  February 1958.
- r07 Item 5 (Nym): "found in the **Association office at Ashlin**, in February 1958, by Miss Ivy Keddie."
  Nym's provenance contradicts the finder's own account; Strawn's 1928 note about the cluttered loft and r12's
  remark that the loft "has never been cleared out" both support Ivy.

**J. Nym's swapped attributions (r07)**
- Item 3, the **station test book**, is headed "**kept by E. Brant**" — but its first leaf is signed A. Keddie,
  it is in one hand for twenty-nine seasons, its provenance is the station, and **Frick's 1925 exhibit list
  independently calls it "in the hand of Ansel Keddie."**
- Item 4, the **farm weigh book** from Hessel Bottom, is headed "**kept by A. Keddie**" — but it was kept by
  one household at Hessel Bottom and passed Emmet → Roy Brant → Hazel Brant. Ivy calls it "the Brant weigh
  book"; **r09 is the keeper's own son describing writing in it; Frick's Exhibit 3 calls it "the private weigh
  book of Emmet Brant."** **The curator has transposed the two keepers' names.**

**K. The Alder Corners evidence — how many seasons?**
- r04 (Ivy): "where the two books overlap, **fourteen seasons** of them, that same milk off that same wet
  ground ran about nineteen hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at home."
- r07 Item 13 (Nym): the correspondence file holds figures copied in 1958 "from the test book of the Alder
  Corners Cheese Company **for the season of 1919**" — one season, not fourteen.
- **r10 (Rosalie, the copier): SIXTEEN seasons, 1915–1930 inclusive, tabulated in full.**
- Reconciliation: sixteen seasons were sent; the station's affected years end with the new glass in 1923, so
  the **overlap of short-glass years is 1915–1922 = eight**, or 1915–1923 = nine. Ivy's "fourteen" fits
  neither; Nym's "one" reflects only what happens to be filed. The one quoted leaf in r10 is the 1919 season,
  which is exactly the season Nym's file names — Nym has probably mistaken the specimen quotation for the
  whole of the file's content.
- Also: the book "passed from its **founder to his daughter** in 1941" (r07 Item 13), while Nym describes
  Rosalie Cudd as Jerome Cudd's **niece** — **r10 and r12 both establish she is his DAUGHTER**, so the
  provenance line is right and Nym's relationship label is wrong.

**L. Strawn's circuit dates and his notebooks**
- r06: took the circuit in **1907**, laid it down 1936, twenty-eight books, which went to the state dairy
  office on retirement.
- r04 (Ivy): "Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks, kept by the state's dairy inspector for this county from
  **1908**, have been in my own college's library at Farland since 1947."
- r07 Item 9 reconciles the custody: to the state office in 1936, to Farland in 1947, the 1923 volume copied
  1958. The **1907 vs 1908** start date remains unreconciled.

**M. Teague's station-gain line and the 1898 entry**
- r05: "The station-gain line was **my own device; I began it when I came in 1905**." But the entry he quotes
  in full — with the settled phrase "**as usual**" already in it — is "the entry for **1898**," seven years
  before he arrived. Either the line predates him (contradicting his claim to have invented it) or he
  reconstructed the earlier years in his own form. Nym (Item 6) says the station column runs from 1897 and
  "each year carries an entry in one form, of which this is typical." Rundle (r12) confirms independently that
  "The station account carries, every year, an entry of this form."

**N. The two percentages, and who used which**
- 5.00% is of fat delivered (and is the glass ratio); 5.25% is of fat credited. Teague's ledger says
  "five and a quarter per cent" but his prose in r05 attaches it to "the fat that was actually in the cream,"
  which would be 5.00%. **Vaught (r11 §V) makes exactly the same slip: "The gain is five and a quarter percent
  of the butterfat actually delivered."** Ivy and Nym both insist the denominators are not interchangeable.

**O. Vose's Jersey herd at 4.56 (r08) vs Ivy's example herd at 4.56 (r04)**
- Vose offers "In 1910 that herd tested 4.56" as proof the valley's land is good. Ivy uses **4.56** as her
  worked example of a rich herd shorted 0.24 of a point. Vose's herd was at the **home place** (delivering to
  Larrow Green, not the station), so it would not have been shorted — but the coincidence of the figure is
  conspicuous, and it is the number that most sharply illustrates the proportional error he is denying.
  Compare the Hessel Bottom weigh book's 1910 test of **3.42**, Ivy's other worked example (short 0.18).

**P. When Keddie went up, and how long the station ran**
- r01: Keddie "went up there in **ninety-five**, the year before they opened it, to get it ready, and he tested
  that first summer too."
- r03/r07/r13: the book begins 4 May 1896; twenty-nine seasons 1896–1924.
- r08 (Vose): "A station in daily use **since 1896**." r12: "it opened in 1896." r14: "that was ninety-six, in
  May, and there was a crowd went up for it."

**Q. The 1919 circular: what it said, and who defends it**
- r01 (a recipient's daughter): "it said there was **water in the cans** coming down from Ostrey Hollow."
- r06 (Strawn): "The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the patrons of this station about
  water in the cans. I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never
  found any, **and I told him so**." — the manager was told, in 1919, by the state's own inspector, that his
  premise was false.
- r08 (Vose, defending it in 1926): "It warned the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, **in temperate language**, that
  added water would be looked for and would be dealt with. It named nobody. It accused nobody... and I would
  write every word of it again tomorrow." He does not mention Strawn's contradiction.
- r09 (Roy Brant): "nine families at the head of the valley got that paper and nine families read it for what
  it was." His father never forgave it — **and specifically resented Keddie's silence, not the accusation
  itself**: "because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of
  that circular and did not." This is a distinct motive for the Brant–Keddie rupture from anything in r01/r03.
- r12 (Rundle): after the circular "the two ends of this valley did not speak civilly to one another for six
  years"; the schoolteacher saw it "come down to them entire, without a word of it being explained, the way a
  debt does," in one schoolroom.
- **r14 (Sill) applauds it: "I thought well of him for it. It was the only plain thing that office ever did,
  and of course they hated him for it up the Hollow, and of course it changed nothing, because a paper never
  emptied a can."** The only source that praises the circular besides its author.

**R. The condemned crate was never mentioned at the hearing — but the 1923 notebook WAS an exhibit**
- r02 (Effie, who took down every word): "The crate was **never mentioned in the hearing at all**. I took every
  word down and it is not there... Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the patrons,
  by the officers, or by the board, and the award's phrase about 'the books and appliances of the station' is
  **a form of words the board used and not a list of anything**." The condemnation was twenty-one months
  before the hearing opened, and the physical evidence sat in the loft throughout.
- **NEW TENSION (r13): Frick's own exhibit list includes "The circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector
  for the year 1923"** — the book with the condemnation entry in it. And Frick says "Every physical appliance
  of that station was before us and we satisfied ourselves upon it." So the board had the document that solved
  the case in its hands and did not read out its most important entry — or read it and did not see it. Effie
  and Frick agree the crate itself was never discussed; Frick's list shows the record of its condemnation was
  nonetheless in evidence. **This is the sharpest new irony in r09–r16.**
- Also: Effie says **three sessions**; Frick confirms three ("We sat three times") but adds that only the first
  was at Ashlin and the other two were held at the station itself, at his request — which no other source
  mentions. If sessions two and three were held in the station's own weigh room, the condemned crate was
  physically in the loft above the board while it sat.

**S. Three explanations argued and none accepted — now with attributions**
- r02: "Three explanations were argued before it. None of the three was accepted and I shall not rehearse them
  here; they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe."
- **r13 (Frick) supplies exactly who put each one:**
  1. **Water — Selby Vose, the manager.** "He had circulated a warning to those patrons on the subject some
     years before and he stood by it before us: that the Hollow's milk came in thinned, that a little water in
     a can is the oldest trick in the trade, and that a station cannot test what is not there. He put it
     without heat and without proof."
  2. **Theft by the tester — Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper**, put obliquely: "he was asked about the station's
     account and answered at length about how such an account would look if a man were helping himself, and
     then observed that he made no charge against anybody. There is no cross-examination that reaches a
     witness of that kind, and I did not attempt one."
  3. **The hay** — "Somebody read the report of the 1908 committee into the record… It is an old opinion in
     that valley and it had the advantage of blaming nobody in the room."
- Frick's reflection: "Three were put to us; each of them was urged by a man of standing; each of them would
  have been believed by half the valley; and not one of them was proved. I have wondered since whether the
  answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories, and whether a lawyer who understood
  chemistry might have found it. I did not understand chemistry."

**T. The unexplained self-correction**
- r01 (Vira): "Then the tests went up. All at once, one summer, the figures came up and stayed up, and nobody
  ever said why... That was the queerest part of the whole business to me even as a child, that the thing
  corrected itself and no man stood up and gave a reason for it." She then supplies the wrong reason (Keddie
  was caught taking cream). The true reason is the new glass of July 1923.
- **r14 (Sill) reaches for the same fact and draws the water conclusion: "one summer, along in the twenties,
  those figures came up and they stayed up, and nothing on this earth changed but the season, and men do not
  suddenly start pouring better milk out of the same cows in the same barns. Something got stopped. Something
  got said to somebody. I never knew what and I never asked."** Sill did not know that the glass had been
  replaced in July 1923 — the one thing that changed.

**U. How many farms had rights on Hessel Bottom — SEVEN or SIX**
- r02 (Effie): **seven farms**.
- r09 (Roy Brant, who farmed the largest of them): **six farms** — "Six farms had rights on it and cut hay off
  it and pastured it. Ours was the largest of the six."
- r15 (the 1908 report itself): **six farms** — "Hessel Bottom, where six farms cut hay and turn out stock…
  Your committee visited each of the six farms."
- r16 (Kepp): **six farms**.
- Three-to-one against Effie; six is very probably right.

**V. The size of the gap — the figures do not agree**
- **0.19** — Ivy (r04), Strawn/Keddie's 3.61→3.80, Nym Item 16 ("a little under two tenths of a point better"
  at Larrow Green).
- **0.17** — **Roy Brant (r09): "Our milk at the Hollow ran seventeen hundredths of a point under what the
  same class of milk was making down at Larrow Green."** Note this is measured against Larrow Green's own
  patrons, and **Sill (r14) says Larrow Green ran 3.78** — 3.78 − 3.61 = 0.17 exactly. Roy's number is
  internally consistent with Sill's; the 0.19 is the gap against the station's own *true* value (3.80).
  **Both are right of their own comparison** — the same denominator trap as the 5.00/5.25 percentages.
- **0.21** — Kepp (r16), and Vose's claimed rise (r08).
- **0.20 ("a fifth of a point")** — Kepp again, for wet bottoms generally in the county.
- **0.295** — Vaught's own body arithmetic in r11 §V, from his erroneous 16.27 c.c.

**W. r11 IS INTERNALLY BROKEN — Vaught's method section contradicts his own conclusion**
This is the most conspicuous single-document problem in the set. r11's narrative reasoning is impeccable and
its numbers are wrong:
| quantity | r11 body | every other source (incl. r11's own quoted conclusion) |
|---|---|---|
| mean delivery | **16.27 c.c.** | 16.72 c.c. |
| ratio | **0.9244** | 0.95 exactly (one part in twenty) |
| true average | **3.905** | 3.80 |
| shortfall per point | **0.295** | 0.19 |
| butterfat unpaid | **118,000 lb** | 76,000 lb |
| pipettes bought 1897 | **five dozen** | six dozen (Ammon invoice, r07 Item 2) |
| own glass in use from | **1 May 1898** | 1 May 1897 |
- **16.27 is 16.72 with the digits transposed** — the same class of error as Teague's 3.16 for 3.61, and it
  cascades through every derived figure in §V.
- Vaught himself notices the wreckage without diagnosing it: "**It should be said that this figure is not
  readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the
  glassware.**" That sentence is the tell: 3.905 cannot be right if the station made 3.80 on true glass.
- His §II example — "A pipette that delivers 16.27 c.c. where it is blown 17.6" — repeats the wrong figure, so
  the transposition is in his working, not a single typo.
- Yet §VII reproduces the true conclusion (one part in twenty; 76,000 lb; 1 May 1897), which r04 says was
  worded by Vaught and Ivy Keddie **together**. The likeliest reading: Ivy's arithmetic is in the conclusion,
  Vaught's own recomputation went wrong in the body, and nobody reconciled the two before printing.
- Also note **"five dozen" vs the invoice's six dozen** and Rundle's footnote **"eleven pipettes"** vs the
  measured nine — three different counts of the same lot.

**X. Grigg's covering letter contradicts the report it introduces (r15) — the most flagrant self-refutation**
- **The sampling period.** Grigg's 1919 letter: "We sampled those herds through the **whole of 1908** — every
  month of that year, in the barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's, and we did
  not let a man know which day we were coming." The report printed **immediately below his own signature**:
  "We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through **January, February and March**." Winter only. His whole
  defense of the finding rests on a year-round sample he did not take — and the winter-only window is exactly
  the window in which bog hay could have had any effect at all.
- **The Bulletin 214 figure.** Grigg: the College trial "found the bog hay to depress the butter fat test by
  **two tenths** in the winter feeding. Two tenths, gentlemen." The bulletin's actual words: "**not more than
  five hundredths** of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."
  He has inflated the College's finding fourfold and dropped the summer clause entirely — and then rests his
  case on the agreement between his own finding and the College's.
- **Payment by test.** Grigg: "Payment by test came in with the **1893** meeting, as I have always understood
  it." Effie/Article VII: **1892**; Rundle: **1891**. Grigg hedges his own date ("as I have always understood
  it").
- Grigg's disclaimer of interest is genuine and checkable: he was not a patron of the station, hauled to Larrow
  Green, and had nothing to gain either way — which makes his errors of memory, not of motive.

**Y. Kepp (r16) inherits and compounds Grigg's misquote**
- Kepp: "The College fed the bog hay under control and measured what it did, and it found the butter fat test
  depressed by **two tenths** in the winter feeding. That is the whole case."
- He then celebrates a triple agreement that is entirely manufactured: "Two tenths from the College, laid
  alongside the **fifth of a point** I have found in the field and the **twenty-one hundredths** that station
  has been complaining of, and the three agree so nearly that I do not know what more a reasonable man
  requires." Three numbers, two of them wrong (0.20 for 0.05; 0.21 from Vose's inflated rise) and one of them
  his own unmeasured impression — converging on a false consensus. **This is the best example in the whole
  record of an error propagating because three independent-looking figures agree.**
- Kepp also drops the bulletin's decisive summer clause ("nothing that could be measured on summer pasture"),
  which is what makes the wet-hay theory the wrong shape for a gap that never varies by season.
- **Kepp's dismissal of the 1911 interval is the case's worst piece of reasoning by a competent man:** "It has
  been urged upon me… that in the summer of 1911 the testing at that station was broken off for six weeks,
  their own apparatus having failed them and a borrowed one being used, and that the figures stood higher
  during those six weeks than before or after. Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen. Six weeks of
  June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on
  the aftermath. I am astonished that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures. I could
  produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked." **The refutation is available in the
  station's own book: every other summer of every other year is in it, at 3.61.** If flush season raised the
  figure, 1911 would not be unique. Kepp never asks what the other summers read.
- Kepp on the objection that the trouble might be in the station: "he had no interest in that quarrel and no
  competence in it; that he had **never in his life read a test and would not know how**; but that when a cause
  is found in the soil, in the herbage, in the condition of the stock, in the College's trial, and in every wet
  bottom in the county alike, it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building." He rules
  out the true cause by declaring himself unqualified to consider it.

**Z. Sill's numbers do not fit his own theory (r14)**
- Sill says Larrow Green's own patrons ran **3.78** "taking one year with another right through those years,"
  and that "The Hollow milk was really a **3.85** and no better."
- If the Hollow's true value was 3.85 and it was paid on 3.61, the gap is 0.24 — bigger than anyone else's
  figure. If the Hollow's true value was 3.80 (Ivy, and the 1911 window, and Alder Corners), Sill is 0.05 high.
- His 3.85 is an impression from the churn, not a measurement, and he says as much: "a buttermaker knows what
  his cream is worth before anybody hands him a paper about it."
- **The fatal internal contradiction in r14:** Sill insists the Hollow was watering its cans — and in the same
  breath testifies that the fat arriving at Larrow Green from the Hollow was **always more** than the station's
  sheet said had been bought. Watered milk cannot deliver surplus fat. He has the disproof of his own theory in
  his own handwriting on every page of his receiving book, and says explicitly that nobody ever asked him for
  it. Rundle (r12) — a schoolteacher with no dairy training, looking at the ledger for the first time — sees in
  one afternoon what Sill missed in forty-one years.
- **Butterfat price:** Sill says **twenty-seven cents** the pound "through these years, take one year with
  another"; Ivy (r04) uses **twenty-five cents** for the same period; Roy Brant (r09) gives **twenty-four
  cents** for 1910 specifically and "about twenty-five cents" over the whole stretch. Kepp (r16) also says
  twenty-seven cents. Ivy's 25c is the one used for the $19,000; at 27c the same 76,000 lb would be $20,520.

**AA. The claimed loss — four different figures**
- **$19,000** / 76,000 lb — Ivy (r04), Nym (r07), Bulletin 471.
- **$19,600** — **r12 (Rundle): "The patrons' loss upon it was nineteen thousand six hundred dollars, and their
  petition set the figure out to the dollar."** The only source that says the 1925 petition contained a dollar
  figure at all.
- **67,000 lb of butter fat** — **r13 (Frick): "The patrons' own reckoning of what they had lost was sixty-seven
  thousand pounds of butter fat over the years complained of, and nothing was ever brought forward to disturb
  that figure."** At 25c, 67,000 lb ≈ $16,750; at 27c ≈ $18,090; neither is $19,600. At 3.16 vs the valley
  figure (Frick's own transposed average) the patrons' arithmetic would have differed again.
- **118,000 lb** — Vaught's erroneous body figure (r11).
- The true figure by Ivy's method is 76,000 lb / $19,000. The patrons in 1925, working from pay slips and
  without knowing the mechanism, got within about 12 percent of it (67,000 lb) — remarkably close for men
  guessing at a cause they never identified.

**BB. The date the Alder Corners factory opened**
- **1913** — r10 (Rosalie, the founder's daughter, who was twelve when the first vat was set).
- **1914** — r09 (Roy Brant) and r12 (Rundle).
- Rosalie should be the best authority on her own father's factory; but two Ordell County sources agree on
  1914. Unresolved. (Nothing turns on it: the haul began in 1915 by all accounts.)

**CC. Emmet Brant's weigh book — when it was begun**
- **1 January 1897** — r07 Item 4 (Nym) and r13 Exhibit 3 (Frick's contemporaneous exhibit list).
- **1 January 1898** — r09 (Roy Brant, the son who inherited and continued it).
- Two documentary sources against one recollection; 1897 is likelier. Note 1897 would make the book start
  exactly with the station's own glass (1 May 1897), which is what makes it useful.

**DD. What the 1908 committee's remit and finding were said to be**
- The report says the committee found "**no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter**, and we wish that finding
  to be printed in the same type as the rest" (r15). None of r01–r08 quoted that sentence; it clears both the
  patrons and the tester eleven years before the 1919 circular accused the patrons and seventeen years before
  the 1925 hearing let Teague insinuate against the tester. **The Association's own committee had already
  cleared everybody, in print, and the Association's own manager circularized the patrons anyway.**
- The report also records that they "tried the patrons' own scales against the station's" — corroborating
  Roy Brant's independent point that the two scales always agreed.

**EE. The valley's settled folk verdict, and its retraction in the same pamphlet (r12)**
- Chapter IV as written: "It is the settled opinion of the valley that cream left that station by the back
  door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it did not. I set that down as I have heard it set
  down a hundred times, by men who were there and men who were not, and I have never heard it contradicted at
  the store."
- The note added while in press retracts it on the ledger's arithmetic (quoted in §4 above). **Rundle leaves
  the wrong paragraph standing and corrects it at the back — so the 1939 pamphlet, and its 1959 reprint,
  circulate the accusation and its refutation in the same covers**, and the valley (per r01, r14) went on
  believing the accusation.
- Rundle also concedes his method up front: "What follows is not a record. It is what the valley remembers,
  tidied, and where the valley is certain of a thing I have written it down as certain." That is an accurate
  description of how the wrong story got preserved.

**FF. Strawn's condemnation described as unrelated housekeeping**
- r12: "The state inspector condemned the station's glassware on the twelfth of July, 1923, **for reasons of
  his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel**, and new glass was put in."
- This is the valley's understanding, and it is exactly backwards: the condemnation was the answer to the
  quarrel. Compare Vose (r08): "I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own
  order book with a date and a price against it." **Two independent sources, one hostile to the patrons and one
  neutral, both file the decisive event under routine maintenance.**

**GG. Keddie's private suspicion (r13) — new, and it complicates the martyr reading**
- Frick: "I add one thing which is not in the minutes. In the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told me privately that
  he had always thought **the patrons were at fault** and would not say so in the room. He was not a bitter man
  about it. He had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors, and I record
  it here because it has been said since that he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that he
  suspected nobody, which is not."
- Set against r03, in which Keddie writes that he will not put anything in the place of his question mark "that
  I do not know" — and against Emmet Brant's grievance (r09) that Keddie "ought to have stood up in front of
  that circular and did not." If Keddie privately half-believed the circular, his silence in 1919 looks
  different, and Emmet's twenty-year estrangement from his own brother-in-law looks better founded than r01 or
  r03 lets on.

**HH. The dismissal's timing**
- Keddie was put out **30 April 1925**, four days before the award and while the board was still sitting.
- Frick: "There was no finding against him from us and there never was one; the board had not concluded and did
  not conclude that he had done anything whatever. He was simply let go while the board was still sitting, and
  I have never been able to think well of the Association for it." The chairman of the board says plainly that
  the Association dismissed the man mid-proceeding without any finding to support it.

**II. Sill's first-season intake figure vs Nym's gap**
- r14: the station took in "about nine hundred thousand pounds" in its first part-season (May–December 1896).
- r07 (Nym): "The station's intake for its first season, 4 May to the end of 1896, is **not in the ledgers and
  is nowhere else**; the station column begins with 1897, and inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told
  there is none."
- **Sill supplies, from memory in 1934, the number Nym says does not exist.** It is not documentary and Nym is
  right that the ledger has none — but the curator's "nowhere else" is now falsified by a recorded recollection.

**JJ. Roy Brant's two-scale point — the elimination nobody drew**
- r09: "Two scales, his and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking to one another, and
  the scales never fell out at all. Whatever was wrong at that station — and something was wrong at that
  station for a very long time — **it was not the weighing**."
- Combined with Strawn's twenty-eight years of lactometer tests finding no added water, and the committee's
  1908 finding of "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter," and Sill's cream always arriving heavy, the
  weighing, the watering and the theft were each independently excluded long before 1958 — by four different
  people, none of whom put their exclusions together with anybody else's.

## 8. THE COMPETING VERDICTS, SIDE BY SIDE

- **Vira Toland (1979):** "He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that is why
  the numbers went right the summer after... A man stands in a room by himself with the separator running and
  the door shut and nobody in the valley can add, and after a while he helps himself, and it is not that he is
  a wicked man, it is that nobody ever came up that road and looked." Yet she also records her father's
  verdict — "an honest man who had got into something" — and her own unease: "he had all those years of this
  valley looking at him a certain way in church, and Orra had it too."
- **Effie Loomis (1941):** keeps recollection and record apart, says where the record stops, and closes:
  "Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that the interesting part is generally what none of
  them thought worth saying." Her self-indictment: of the four officers, "not one of us ever went up the
  valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch the work being done. I include
  myself first in that."
- **Ansel Keddie (1925):** "I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what they
  are." And: "a man is allowed to answer and not allowed to explain." (Privately, per Frick: he suspected the
  patrons and would not say so.)
- **Ivy Keddie (1959):** the glass; nothing depends on her opinion of her grandfather — "It depends on nine
  pieces of glass and a burette." Her closing arithmetic of delay: "The glass was condemned in 1923 and the
  cause was found in 1958: thirty-five years. My grandfather was dismissed in 1925: thirty-three years. He
  died in 1934 and had been dead twenty-four years when the sheet was read that clears him. I have no remedy
  to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way out."
- **Wilbur Teague (1948):** a structural verdict, not an accusation. "Cream is portable, and that station was
  in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons... One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man
  made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator.
  There was no second man in that building from the spring to the fall. I do not say what any particular
  person did on any particular day, because I was not there and I do not know... The remedy is two men or a
  lock, and the Association had neither." And on his own blindness: "a figure that comes in at five and a
  quarter every year is **furniture**." And: "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience."
  (Frick, r13, saw him do the same thing to the board's face and called it what it was.)
- **Merle Strawn (1936):** refuses a verdict on principle. "I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book...
  I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man carries out
  to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights." Also: "every time in my life I have seen a
  field man paid for an opinion instead, the opinion has outlived the weighing and done harm after the man was
  dead." (His one procedural failure — skipping the burette check in 1922 — he recorded plainly, and it cost a
  year.)
- **Selby Vose (1926):** watered cans. "Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped
  when the Association began to look. The rise of 1924 is the measure of it... The tests came up when the
  looking began, and any man who wants a different explanation of that is welcome to stand where I am standing
  and give us one." On the condemnation: "I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping that appears
  in our own order book with a date and a price against it." On the payment: "a generous settlement of an
  unproved claim... There is not a business house in Ordell County that would have done it. We did it because
  we are not a business house; we are the farmers of this valley in company together." His charitable framing
  of the imagined culprit: "A little water in a can is not a felony in a man's own mind. It is a way of making
  a hard year come out even, and the man who does it tells himself he will stop when the price improves."
  His closing exhortation: "Bring it as it left the cow. Wash the cans, cool the milk, keep the dog out of the
  milk house, and bring it as it left the cow."
- **Halbert Nym (1972):** curatorial neutrality, with the two-denominator warning and the honest gap note:
  "The station's intake for its first season, 4 May to the end of 1896, is not in the ledgers and is nowhere
  else; the station column begins with 1897, and inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is
  none." His glosses "are mine and are worth what an afternoon is worth."
- **Roy Brant (1955):** no verdict on cause, and an explicit refusal to guess. "Whatever was wrong at that
  station — and something was wrong at that station for a very long time — it was not the weighing." On the
  family fact: "Ansel Keddie, who kept the station's test book, was married into the family that had more to
  lose by that station than any other family on the meadow, and I have never known what to do with that fact
  except set it down." On his father's habit: "It took him no time at all, and it is the only reason there is
  anything left to argue about now." On the constancy of the gap: "Nobody would take account of that, then or
  since, though it was in front of them." On the money: "Seventeen hundredths is nothing to look at on one pay
  slip. Set the slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm."
- **Rosalie Cudd (1958):** an outsider's flat refusal of the valley's whole frame. On watering: "A cheesemaker
  knows watered milk inside a day. He does not need a test to know it, though the test will tell him too. The
  curd comes soft and it will not knit; you get a slack, weak body and your yield is down, and you can stand at
  the vat and see your money going out with the whey. It cannot be hidden from the man who makes the cheese,
  because the cheese tells him, and it tells him the next morning, not next year. Butter is more forgiving that
  way. **Cheese is a witness.** In sixteen summers my father never made one complaint of those four men, never
  docked one of them a cent, and never — not once that I heard, and I was in the room — said a word about their
  milk except that he wished he had more of it in July." On the wet-hay theory: her father "used to say that
  the Ordell people had a theory about that bottom the way some families have a ghost: it explained things, and
  it cost nothing, and nobody was ever going to test it." On her own detachment: "I paid no attention. It was
  not my county, they were not my patrons in September, and I had a make to get out. If I sound short about
  Ordell it is only that I have listened to forty years of your valley explaining that meadow to itself, and
  **I never once heard anybody go up there and look.**" Why her father never compared figures: "it never
  entered his head. He had no business with what your station made of the same milk… He is not keeping a check
  on another man's arithmetic seven miles off over a mountain, and if you had suggested it to my father he
  would have laughed at you."
- **Leland Vaught (1958):** the glass, stated as a matter of instrument design rather than of persons. "It is
  answered here by reference to a piece of glass." His institutional recommendation: every station in the state
  should be burette-checked once, an afternoon's work. His methodological creed: "I have spent my working life
  urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation." (His own calculation is the one thing in the
  bulletin that is wrong.)
- **Dell Rundle (1939/1959):** the valley's memory, faithfully transcribed and then honestly overturned by a
  ledger he happened to see while the type was being set. His frame: "Fifty years is not long for a country,
  but it is a long time for a valley to keep one thing going." His closing: "Set against the quarrel, put the
  rest: fifty Junes of cream coming down that road in the early cool, fifty Januaries of it going down over ice
  with the horses roughshod, a check every month in kitchens that had known none, and a creamery still standing
  that was built by men with nothing but each other and a piece of paper they had written themselves. Whatever
  this valley did wrong at the head of it, it did this right, and it did it for half a century, and the men of
  1889 would think we had done well."
- **Alonzo Frick (1946):** the procedural verdict, and the most self-aware of all of them. "It was not
  litigation at all… with no appeal from it, no reported decision, and nothing whatever to cite it for; and I
  have thought about it oftener than about anything I ever argued in a courthouse." On why Keddie was doomed:
  "**a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author.**"
  On the limits of adjudication: "A board of arbitration can only decide between the accounts that are put to
  it." On what he could and could not do: "I did not understand chemistry. I understood that an honest man was
  ruined by a figure nobody could explain, and that we paid his neighbors money without being able to tell them
  what they were being paid for." On seeing the test made: "I will say plainly that I did not understand the
  test then and do not understand it now; I am a lawyer and the thing is chemistry. But I could see that it was
  done the same way twice, and that the men who did it were not hiding their hands." On moving the sessions to
  the station: "so that the board might see the plant it was to pass upon instead of hearing it described by
  men with an interest in the description. That was my doing and I am still content with it."
- **Hobart Sill (1934):** watered cans, asserted with total confidence by the one man whose own records
  disprove it. "You cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer. There was water in those cans
  and I said so at the time." His contempt for the office is the through-line: "The Association has a paper for
  everything and butter for nothing… I have had men come in here in a collar and tell me my churn was running
  slow when they could not have told you which end of a cow gets up first… not one man in that office ever came
  down here to see how it was done, and every one of them could tell you to the half cent what the fat was
  worth… and there is not a man in the office could tell you what makes it churn." His epistemic rule, stated
  as a virtue: "the day a buttermaker starts asking about other men's cans is the day he stops making butter."
  His last request: "Put in that Hobart Sill made the butter and it was good butter and he stood at the churn
  until the day they gave him a chair."
- **Nyle Grigg (1908/1919):** the land, defended eleven years later with a misremembered sample and a
  misquoted bulletin. "I am content to let the matter rest where the College and this committee have jointly
  put it. The remedy lies with the land and not with the station. Drain that bottom, sweeten it, keep the stock
  off it in the wet of the spring, and feed the coarse hay off it with a mixed ration and not by itself, and
  the Hollow will test with the rest of us. Go on as they are going and abuse the man who reads their figures
  for them, and they will be grumbling in twenty years' time as they were grumbling when we began, and no
  better off." (He was right that they would still be grumbling; wrong about everything else. Note also
  "abuse the man who reads their figures for them" — a defense of Keddie from the wet-hay side.)
- **Roland Kepp (1926):** the land again, dressed in mineral chemistry. "It is my habit, when I am called to a
  herd that is doing badly, to ask first what the cattle are standing on. I have found in thirty years that the
  answer to nine cases in ten is under the animal's feet, and that the tenth is in the water she drinks."
  His model: sour acid soil poor in lime and phosphates → sedge and rush crowd out sweet grasses → hay carries
  bulk and water but no minerals → "A cow in milk is a manufactory working on a narrow margin… If her ration is
  short of the minerals, she does not simply give less; she robs her own frame to make good, and she goes on
  robbing it until she cannot." → "the fat is the first thing to suffer, because the fat is the dearest thing
  the cow makes and it is the first economy she practices when she is pressed." His field claim: "The wet
  bottoms of this county yield a milk poorer in fat by a fifth of a point and every practical man knows it."
  His concession, which he does not follow: "The patrons of the skimming station at Ostrey Hollow have
  complained for something like a quarter of a century that their milk tests below the milk of the rest of the
  valley. **Their complaint is perfectly just and I have never doubted it.**" His remedy: "Drain the bottom;
  open the old ditches and cut new; lime the ground and lime it again; sow it down when it will carry a
  seeding; keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, when a hoof does more harm in a day than a season
  will repair; and never feed that hay alone… Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these
  years, and a fifth of a point taken off every hundredweight a farm ships, year in and year out, will pay for
  a great deal of tile." His closing dismissal of the station theory: "when a cause is found in the soil, in
  the herbage, in the condition of the stock, in the College's trial, and in every wet bottom in the county
  alike, it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building."
  (The discussion: a member asked whether the difference might be the cows rather than the land — Kepp had
  considered and rejected it, "some of the best-bred cattle in the county stand on wet land, and some very
  indifferent cattle on the hills test well; and… a man may improve his herd for twenty years and never get his
  fat up while his ground is sour." A member asked whether the trouble might lie in the station — Kepp
  disclaimed competence, as above. The chairman thanked him and said the paper would be printed for the
  members.)

## 11. SMALL DETAILS WORTH KEEPING

- Nine families got the 1919 letter; "Some of them kept theirs till they died. One woman I knew had hers
  pinned inside the lid of her trunk. You can tell people a thing like that is only business and they will
  still keep the paper forty years to prove what was said of them" (r01).
- It rained the day the letter came; Vira remembers "the wet on my father's shoulders when he came in, and I
  have never in sixty years been able to think about the one without the other."
- Vira's father's joke about lawyers in Ashlin — "There are still lawyers in Ashlin."
- Keddie "tested a thousand cans a week and never a blot in that book" (Vira's account) vs his own "thirty-one
  times a week" (tests, not cans) — different units, not necessarily a conflict.
- The Association was "three things on paper: a creamery at Larrow Green, an office at Ashlin, and a skimming
  station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow. The station was the smallest of the three and it made
  the most noise" (r05).
- Effie on why the Association was founded: before the creamery "there was no common test and no common price,
  and every man's arithmetic was his own... I have never heard anybody in this valley say we should have been
  better without it."

## 12. THE PATTERN THE WHOLE RECORD MAKES (worth holding as a frame)

1. **Every explanation offered before 1958 blamed a person or a piece of land, and each was refuted in
   contemporaneous documents that nobody assembled.** Watering: refuted by Strawn's lactometer on every visit
   (1907–1936), by Rosalie Cudd's "cheese is a witness," and by Sill's own heavy cream. Theft: refuted by the
   cream arriving heavy, which Sill recorded and Rundle finally reasoned from. The land: refuted by Bulletin
   214's actual figures, by the gap's indifference to season, and by the Alder Corners table.
2. **The decisive evidence was in the record within fifteen years of the station opening** — the 1911 nine
   weeks, in Keddie's own hand with a question mark against it — and lay unread for forty-seven years.
3. **Transposed digits corrupt this record twice, in opposite directions, at the two ends of the story:**
   Teague's **3.16** for 3.61 (1948, propagated to Frick and Rundle) and Vaught's **16.27** for 16.72 (1958,
   propagating through the whole magnitude section of the bulletin that solves the case).
4. **A misquotation propagates into a false consensus:** Bulletin 214's "five hundredths" becomes Grigg's
   "two tenths" (1919) becomes Kepp's "two tenths" (1926), which Kepp then triangulates against Vose's
   inflated 0.21 and his own impressionistic 0.20 to declare that three independent figures agree.
5. **Two proportional-error traps recur:** the 5.00%/5.25% denominators (Teague and Vaught both slip) and the
   0.17/0.19 gap (Roy Brant measuring against Larrow Green's 3.78, Ivy against the station's true 3.80 — both
   right of their own comparison).
6. **The people closest to the evidence were the least able to use it,** and each said so in his own way:
   Sill ("nobody ever asked me to"), Teague ("furniture"; "not its conscience"), Effie ("not one of us ever
   went up the valley"), Frick ("I did not understand chemistry"), Kepp ("I have never in my life read a test
   and would not know how"), Rosalie ("I never once heard anybody go up there and look").
