## 0. THE CASE IN ONE PARAGRAPH (the answer the documents converge on)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association ran a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from its creamery at Larrow Green. For roughly a generation the milk tested at that station read low — about 3.61% butterfat where it should have read about 3.80% — and the patrons were paid accordingly. Everyone blamed something: wet bog hay, the tester, the office, watered cans. In 1925 a board of arbitration found "no cause established" and nevertheless paid the patrons $3,800, and the tester, Ansel Keddie, was put out after 29 seasons. In 1958 Keddie's granddaughter Ivy Keddie measured nine surviving pipettes from a crate the state inspector had condemned in 1923 and found they delivered 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 c.c. mark — exactly 95% — so every test made with that glass read 95% of the truth. The shortfall, 76,000 lb of butterfat worth about $19,000, had been sitting in the Association's own ledgers all along, entered year after year by the bookkeeper as "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual." Nobody stole anything. The glass was short.

## 2. PEOPLE AND RELATIONSHIPS

Drafted the by-laws himself; "he was not a lawyer and they read as a farmer wrote them, which is to their credit" (Loomis). **Died 1917** — so he could not be asked afterward what Article VII meant. Kept a herd of **Jerseys** at the home place.
- **Selby Vose** — manager of the Association **1913–1938** (Loomis); by his own 1926 address, "thirteen years this spring, since 1913." Signed the annual station sheet every year (Teague). Sent the 1919 circular. Kept his father's Jerseys after him.
  - **CONFLICT ON KINSHIP.** Vira Toland (r01): Selby was "old Gideon Vose's nephew." Effie Loomis (r02): "Selby Vose, **son** of the late president." Selby himself (r08): "**My father**, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law…" Two sources against one; Toland is the outlier and the latest/most distant.
- **Old Mrs. Vose** — first cousin to **Orra Keddie** (Toland). "So when the letter came it was not only the Association saying it… it was somebody's cousin saying it, and that is a different thing altogether in a place this size."

## 3. THE INSTITUTION

- **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association**, organized **1889** at a meeting at **Larrow Green** by the farmers of the Larrow Valley. Gideon Vose elected first president.
- **Main creamery built at Larrow Green in 1891, on Larrow Creek** — "and it is the same building yet" (1941). Before it, "the members sold their milk where they could get a price for it and there was no common test and no common price, and every man's arithmetic was his own. The creamery ended that."
- Loomis's judgment: "Whatever is said of the Association afterward, it began as a plain remedy for a plain grievance, and I have never heard anybody in this valley say we should have been better without it."
- **Three things on paper** (Teague): the creamery at Larrow Green, the office at Ashlin (one room over a harness shop, cold at the north end and never cold enough at the south), and the skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow. "The station was the smallest of the three and it made the most noise."
- **Hessel Bottom** — the wet meadow below the hamlet, the best milk ground up there. **Seven farms had rights on it**; they cut hay and pastured on it; Emmet Brant's was the largest of the seven.
- **Nine families** hauled to the station (Toland; also the number the 1919 circular went to, per both Toland and Keddie).

### Article VII (quoted identically in r02 and r07)

> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

- **Adopted at the annual meeting of 1892**, the year after the creamery opened (Loomis). The printed pamphlet copies (Nym, Item 1) carry **no year on their face**, only the words "adopted at the annual meeting."
- What it changed: before it, a man was paid on what his cans weighed; after it, on the fat found by test at the place of delivery.
- Loomis's warning about the **last clause**, "which is the one nobody quotes": *The test of the station where he delivers shall govern.* — "Not the test of the creamery, not the average of the valley, not a test made over again anywhere else. The station's test, and no appeal from it. **That clause is the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business** and it was written in 1892 by a man who died in 1917 and could not be asked about it afterward."
- Loomis calls the rule just in itself: "it pays a man for what he actually brings and not for the water that is naturally in it."

### How the station worked
Patrons of the neighborhood hauled milk up to the station instead of driving seven miles down to Larrow Green. Milk weighed in → sample taken from each man's can and tested for butterfat → man paid on his pounds and his test together → station separated the milk → **cream sent down the valley to Larrow Green, skim given back to the farmer for his hogs and calves** (Teague; same account in Loomis).

## 4. THE PHYSICAL STATION

- Stood **at the very top of the Hollow where the road bends and then goes to nothing but the ridge**, seven miles up from Larrow Green.
- "A low building, whitewashed, cold as a spring house inside even in August, and it smelled of milk and steam and hot iron, and you could hear the engine going before you came round the last turn" (Toland).
- Had an **intake room** with a **loft over it, reached by a ladder**. Strawn's 1928 entry: "The loft over the intake room is full of odds and ends and I told them a loft over a milk room is a place for nothing but air, and was told in return that **it had been full since before the war**."
- By 1958 the building was shut and had been for years; "they kept fence wire and salt in it" (Toland). Ivy: "used for storage since it closed."
- Keddie wrote **standing up at a slope desk by the window where the light came in** (Toland). Teague also worked at a sloped desk, with a brass rail across the foot to keep the sheets from sliding.

## 5. THE TESTING WORK, IN KEDDIE'S OWN DESCRIPTION (r03, letter)

- **Day began at half past five.** Wagons came up the Hollow in the dark most of the year; he had the intake room open, the lamps lit, and the scale swinging free before the first was at the door.
- **Every can went on the scale**; the weight was called and written before the milk went anywhere else. "That scale was tested by the state's man on his rounds and it was true, and I have never in twenty-nine seasons had a patron dispute a weight with me and be right."
- **Sample taken from each patron's milk as it was weighed**, into **his own numbered jar**; the jar **made up over ten days** before testing.
- On testing morning: jar set in **warm water until blood-warm right through**, then **poured from vessel to vessel, and poured again**, until the risen cream was carried down through the whole of it. "A sample that is not warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy could tell you so."
- **The charge:** milk drawn up in the measure **to the mark on the stem**, tip wiped, charge blown out into the bottle carrying the patron's number.
- **Then the acid**, poured down the side, and the bottle turned until the milk was dark.
- **Four bottles in the hand tester**, weighted against each other, cover down, **crank turned at the speed he was taught and no faster** — "and no man ever stood beside me and made me hurry it."
- Then the fat stands **up in the neck of the bottle in a clear column**, read off **in per cent against the rulings, with a pair of dividers**, and the figure written down.
- "That is the whole of it. It is not a hard trade. **It is only a trade that must be done the same way every time, which is a harder thing than it sounds.**"

### The book
- Keddie: kept **in five columns — the date, the patron, the pounds, the test, the fat.** (Nym, Item 3, describes "columns for date, patron, pounds and test" — **four**. Minor conflict.)
- "Where I made an error I crossed it and set my initials against the crossing, and **I never wrote over a figure, because a figure written over another figure is a figure no man can swear to afterward.**"
- Toland on the same book: ruled by his own hand with a straightedge, every line the same height as every other, "his figures were like print"; if a man asked what his milk had done, "he did not have to hunt. He turned to the day and he read it off." **"He tested a thousand cans a week and never a blot in that book."** Her father: "if the rest of the world kept its accounts the way Ansel Keddie kept his there would be no lawyers in Ashlin. Which was my father's way of talking. There are still lawyers in Ashlin."
- Keddie's own rate in his statement: **"thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons."** (Toland's "a thousand cans a week" is about volume of cans, not tests; not necessarily a contradiction but the two figures sit oddly together.)
- Nym, Item 3, physical description: half-bound, marbled boards, back mended with cloth; **one hand throughout, twenty-nine seasons of it**.

## 7. THE ARITHMETIC (the core of Ivy Keddie's 1959 demonstration)

- A butterfat test is made by drawing **a measured charge of milk into a bottle whose neck is ruled to read percent for that charge and no other.** Put in a short charge and you get a short reading, **in the same proportion**.
- Pipettes **blown for 17.6 c.c.** delivered a mean of **16.72 c.c.** → **16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly.** Short by **0.88 c.c.**
- **Every test made at that station with that glass read ninety-five percent of the truth.**
- **3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80.** Recorded average 3.61; true average 3.80; **gap 0.19 of a point.**
- Ivy's key insight, which she says everyone before her missed: **"Nineteen hundredths is five percent of three point eight. The station did not lose a set number of points. It lost a twentieth of whatever was there."** It is a **proportion, not a fixed quantity**.
- **Twenty-five full delivery years, 1898 to 1922**: station took in **40,000,000 lb of milk**, average **1,600,000 lb a year**.
  - At the recorded 3.61%: patrons credited with **1,444,000 lb of fat**.
  - At the true 3.80%: they had delivered **1,520,000 lb**.
  - **Difference: 76,000 lb of butterfat.**
- **Butterfat averaged 25 cents a pound** over those years → unpaid fat worth **$19,000**.
- **The 1925 award was $3,800 — "That is a fifth of it."** (Indeed $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20.)

### The two percentages — both right, of different denominators
- **76,000 is five percent of the fat actually delivered** (of 1,520,000).
- **76,000 is five and a quarter percent of the fat credited** (of 1,444,000).
- "The Association's ledger line uses the second, because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered. Neither figure is an error. **But five percent is the one that is also the glass.**"
- Nym's curatorial note on Item 6 says the same: "The two percentages in this file are not interchangeable: five and a quarter percent of the credited fat is five percent of the fat delivered, and each is right of its own denominator and of no other."
- **Check against the 1898 ledger entry:** 57,760 ÷ 60,800 = 0.95 exactly. 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26% (the "five and a quarter" of the entry). 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.0%. The ledger line was a 95% ratio printed every year for a quarter century.

### The regressive effect — it fell hardest on the best milk
- "A herd whose milk was **recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24** of a point. A herd **recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18**. **The richer the milk, the more was taken from it, and no man had any way of knowing that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones.**"
- (Derived: 4.56 ÷ 0.95 = 4.80, short 0.24. 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60, short 0.18. Both check exactly.)
- **Cross-link worth holding:** **3.42** is precisely the test Emmet Brant was paid on in 1910 (weigh-book leaf, §6.12), on 200,000 lb even. Derived loss for Brant in that single year: 200,000 × 0.0018 = **360 lb of fat**, about **$90** at 25 cents. And **4.56** is precisely the test Selby Vose says his father's Jersey herd made in 1910 (§8, r08) — the rich-herd figure in Ivy's example is the manager's own herd's number, though his herd delivered at Larrow Green, not at the station.

### The nine weeks of 1911 — the accidental controlled experiment
- The station's own measure **broke on 8 May 1911**; a substitute was used until **10 July 1911**. (8 May to 10 July = **nine weeks**, matching the summary leaf.)
- **"Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man at the crank — one object changed — and the average went to 3.80 and came back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned."**
- Ivy: **"No explanation of this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you."**
- Ivy says the 1911 substitute **"came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon."** (Keddie only says "one borrowed"; Teague says their own "came up from town." See conflicts, §10.)

### Two more evidentiary props
- **Alder Corners cross-check:** over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, the Alder Corners Cheese Company tested the milk of **four Hessel Bottom men in their summer flush**; where the two books overlap — **fourteen seasons of them** — "that same milk off that same wet ground ran about **nineteen hundredths higher** at Alder Corners than at home."
- **Larrow Green's own books** (Nym, Item 16): over the same years for which the station's 3.61 is recorded, **the Larrow Green average runs a little under two tenths of a point better** — i.e. the ~0.19 gap, seen from the creamery's side.

### The elapsed-time arithmetic Ivy closes on
- "The glass was condemned in 1923 and the cause was found in 1958: **thirty-five years**. My grandfather was dismissed in 1925: **thirty-three years**. He died in 1934 and had been dead **twenty-four years** when the sheet was read that clears him. **I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way out.**"

## 8. THE FOUR RIVAL EXPLANATIONS, AND WHAT KILLS EACH

1. **Wet bog hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream** — the 1908 committee's finding; "the remedy lies with the land and not with the station."
   - Killed by **Bulletin No. 214 (1911)**: bog hay as the *whole* roughage depresses the test by **not more than 0.05 in winter and nothing measurable on summer pasture** — against a gap of **0.19 standing flat in every month of every year**. Ivy: **"It is four times too small and it is the wrong shape."**
   - Killed also by Keddie's own observation: "**They did not come and go with the season.** The gap was there in July as it was there in February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the same in a wet year as in a dry one."
   - Killed also by Alder Corners: the same milk off the same wet ground ran 0.19 higher across the ridge.
   - Note the 1908 committee **sampled only January, February and March** — winter only — and generalized to the whole year.
2. **Water in the cans / the patrons watering their milk** — the Association's 1919 circular; Selby Vose still asserting it in 1926.
   - Killed by **Strawn**, who tested for water with the lactometer on **every visit** across nearly thirty years and **never once found any** — and who told Vose so, to his face, in 1919.
   - Killed by Bulletin No. 471: **"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow."**
   - Killed by shape: watering would not produce a flat 5% proportional shortfall in every month of every year, nor would it stop dead on the day a pipette changed.
3. **The tester took the cream** — Toland's belief; Teague's structural argument; the valley's verdict for a generation.
   - Killed by Bulletin No. 471: **"no cream was stolen there."**
   - Killed by the 1911 nine weeks — the same man at the crank, one object changed, the figures moved.
   - Killed by the ledger: the missing fat was never missing. It **arrived at Larrow Green** in the cream and was **entered as station gain**. Nothing left the building.
4. **The glass was short** — Ivy Keddie's demonstration. Fits every fact: the flat proportion, the season-independence, the 1896 borrowed-glass year at 3.80, the nine weeks of 1911, the 1923 condemnation, the 1924 recovery to 3.80, and the ledger's own 5.25%/5% line.

Loomis notes that **"three explanations were argued before"** the 1925 board and **"none of the three was accepted,"** and that she would not rehearse them — they are in the minutes, and the minutes are in the safe.

## 10. CONFLICTS, DISCREPANCIES AND ODDITIES — the list to carry

### Between sources

1. **Selby Vose: son or nephew of Gideon?** Toland says nephew (r01). Loomis says son (r02). Vose says "my father" (r08). **Two-to-one against Toland.**
2. **The award: $3,500 or $3,800?** Toland: "they paid the patrons **thirty-five hundred dollars** and called it settled… split up nine ways over all those years — my father's share of it did not buy a hay rake." Every documentary source (Loomis's recorded award, Teague's ledger, Nym's Item 7, Vose's address, Ivy's talk): **$3,800**. Toland is the outlier and is recalling at sixty years' distance.
3. **The date of the hearing.** Toland places the hearing "the winter and spring after" the 1919 letter — i.e. 1919/20. Every other source: **the petition was January 1925 and the board sat 17 March – 4 May 1925.** Toland has telescoped six years of grievance into one sequence.
4. **When the station opened.** Toland: Keddie went up in "ninety-five, the year before they opened it, and he tested that first summer too." The book's first leaf: **begun 4 May 1896.** Both can be true if he prepared the building in 1895 and the book began in 1896.
5. **Total intake and average test.** Loomis (from Teague's own annual sheets) and Ivy and Nym: **40,000,000 lb over 1898–1922, average test 3.61.** Teague in his 1948 memoir: **"that station took in forty-four million pounds of milk"** and "the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, taken over the whole period… **was 3.16.**" Teague's 3.16 looks like a **transposition of 3.61**, and his 44 million covers a longer span ("over the years the account ran," from 1897 rather than 1898). Note that Loomis explicitly attributes the 40 million / 1.6 million / 25 cents figures **to Teague's own ledgers**: "These two figures are not mine and I do not vouch for the cows, only for the copying: they are Mr. Teague's, out of the annual sheets, and I have added the columns twice." **The bookkeeper's memoir disagrees with the bookkeeper's ledgers.**
6. **Length of the 1911 borrowed-measure period.** Keddie's book and Ivy: **nine weeks** (8 May – 10 July). Teague: **"they made shift with a borrowed one for six weeks."**
7. **Where the 1911 substitute came from.** Ivy: "came up from **Larrow Green** with the cream wagon." Teague: they made shift "before **their own came up from town**." Keddie: only "one borrowed."
8. **Where the 1896 glass was borrowed from.** Keddie's letter (1925): "The glass I began on in ninety-six was borrowed off the **Ashlin creamery**." His own first leaf (1896): "Glass borrowed of **Larrow Green** until our own comes." Nym's office file agrees with the leaf: "Larrow Green having lent until then." **Keddie misremembers his own book at thirty years' distance** — a useful demonstration that the contemporaneous document beats the later memory.
9. **When the station-gain line began.** Teague: "**The station-gain line was my own device; I began it when I came in 1905.** I have heard it said in the valley that the line was some old dodge of the founders. It was not." But **the entry he quotes complete is the entry for 1898**, seven years before he arrived, and Nym (Item 6) says "the station column runs from **1897**; each year carries an entry in one form." Either Teague's memory of authorship is wrong, or he standardized/reconstructed an existing line and remembers inventing it.
10. **The date of the condemnation.** Strawn's book, Nym's photostat, and Bulletin 471: **12 June 1923.** **Selby Vose says "he condemned the station's testing apparatus on the twelfth of July, 1923."** Vose is refuted by his own Association's paperwork: the replacement order is dated **28 June 1923** and Ammon's acknowledgment **2 July 1923** — both *before* his claimed condemnation date. Vose also says "We ordered new inside the month," which works only for a June condemnation.
11. **The 1924 average.** Keddie: "In 1924 the average of this station was **3.80**." Strawn 1924: "Their book gives the year at **3.80**." **Vose: "the tests at that station rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood at 3.90."** Vose's own two numbers do not reconcile with each other either: 3.61 + 0.21 = **3.82**, not 3.90; and a rise to 3.80 is a rise of 0.19, not 0.21. **Vose has inflated the recovery he is claiming credit for.**
12. **Where the crate was found.** **Ivy Keddie (1959): in the loft over the intake room at the station building up the Hollow, under a tarpaulin at the back, reached by a ladder, in February of last year.** **Nym (1972, Item 5): "The crate was found in the Association office at Ashlin, in February 1958, by Miss Ivy Keddie."** Direct, flat contradiction on the single most important provenance fact in the case. Ivy's account is first-hand and circumstantially rich (the tarpaulin, the ladder, the stencil "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET"); Nym is a curator working from deposit slips thirteen years later. Strawn's 1928 note that the loft "had been full since before the war" supports Ivy.
13. **The keepers of the two books are swapped in Nym's catalogue.** **Item 3 is headed "Station test book, kept by E. Brant"** — but its first leaf is signed **A. Keddie**, it is in one hand for twenty-nine seasons, and every other source says Ansel Keddie kept it. **Item 4 is headed "Farm weigh book, kept by A. Keddie"** — but its provenance is pure Brant: begun at Hessel Bottom, "kept there by one household throughout," to **Roy Brant** in 1929, to **Hazel Brant** in 1962, and Ivy calls it "**the Brant weigh book**… lent to me by Roy Brant, who is Emmet Brant's son." **The curator has transposed the two names.** (Note also that Nym's Item 8, Keddie's statement, is described as being "in the hand of Item 3" — which is right only if Item 3 is Keddie's book, confirming the heading is the error.)
14. **The Alder Corners overlap.** Ivy: "where the two books overlap, **fourteen seasons** of them." Nym, Item 13: the Vessey County correspondence file holds "figures copied in 1958 from the test book of the Alder Corners Cheese Company **for the season of 1919**" — one season. Either Nym holds only a fraction of what Ivy worked from, or one of the two is wrong about the extent of the comparison. **Ivy also never saw the Alder Corners book herself** — the figures were copied out for her by Rosalie Cudd and the book has never left Vessey County. This is the weakest link in her evidentiary chain.
15. **Alder Corners descent.** Ivy: Rosalie Cudd "runs that factory and is **Jerome Cudd's niece**." Nym: the book "passed from its founder **to his daughter** in 1941 and to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970." Niece vs daughter; possibly two different people, possibly a slip.
16. **When Strawn's circuit began.** Strawn: "I took the Ordell circuit in **nineteen hundred and seven**." Ivy: "Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks, kept by the state's dairy inspector for this county **from 1908**." Strawn's own first extract is headed **1907** ("First round of the new circuit").
17. **Twenty-eight books for how many years?** Strawn calls them "twenty-eight of them, **one to the year**, oilcloth covers, pocket size," covering **1907 to 1936 inclusive = thirty years**. Two short. (His extracts also skip 1908, 1910, 1914, 1918, 1927, 1929, 1930, 1932, 1933, 1935 — but he says he copied extracts, not every entry.)
18. **Characterization of the 1919 circular.** **Toland:** "it said there was water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow. That is what it said. **Water in the cans.**" **Keddie:** "a circular went out from the Association to nine families up here **saying there was water in their cans**." **Strawn:** "The manager… has sent a circular round to the patrons of this station **about water in the cans**." **Vose (1926):** "It **warned** the patrons… **in temperate language**, that added water **would be looked for and would be dealt with**. It named nobody. It accused nobody." Three sources describe an accusation; the man who wrote it describes a warning.
19. **Columns in the test book.** Keddie: **five** (date, patron, pounds, test, fat). Nym, Item 3: **four** (date, patron, pounds, test).
20. **Rate of testing.** Keddie: "thirty-one times a week." Toland: "He tested **a thousand cans a week**." Different units, probably not a true contradiction, but hold both.

### Within single sources

21. **Teague's own percentage.** He writes that "**the gain ran at five and a quarter percent of the fat that was actually in the cream**" — but 3,040 lb against the 60,800 lb actually in the cream is **five percent**; 5.25% is the gain against the **credited** figure, 57,760. Teague has attached the ledger's number to the wrong denominator, which is exactly the confusion Ivy and Nym both take pains to unpick.
22. **Vose's arithmetic** (see conflict 11): 0.21 rise and a 3.90 average cannot both be true from a 3.61 base, and neither matches the book's 3.80.
23. **The award itself** — "finds no cause established" and then $3,800: Loomis, Keddie and Vose all remark on the contradiction, from three different angles. Keddie: "both of those things are true at once, and I have given up trying to hold them in my head together." Loomis: "it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once." Vose reframes it as generosity: "a generous settlement of an unproved claim."

### Structural oddities worth flagging

24. **Strawn had the burette from spring 1921 and did not run the station's glass until 12 June 1923** — and explicitly recorded the omission in 1922 ("the day being short and the road home being what it was, **and I have put that down because it is what happened**"). Two seasons of the answer sitting in his grip. He notes it himself in the 1923 entry: "I had carried the burette two seasons before this check."
25. **Strawn's June 1911 visit fell inside the nine-week window** of the borrowed measure — "Watched a test through. Nothing here to note." He was physically present during the one natural experiment that would have settled the case, and had no reason to know it.
26. **The burette program was discontinued in 1926**, five years after it began, "no more to be issued and no returns wanted."
27. **The crate was never mentioned at the 1925 hearing at all.** Loomis, §8: "I took every word down and it is not there… **Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions**, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board, and the award's phrase about '**the books and appliances of the station**' is a form of words the board used and not a list of anything." Her closing line: "**Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying.**"
28. **The condemned crate was in the building (or the office) throughout**, and the award recites having had "the books and **appliances** of the station" before it.
29. **The 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move.** Loomis: "It was not thrown out; it was carried out with the rest and it did not arrive, and I have looked in every place a book can be in this building… **anybody who tells you what was resolved at the first four annual meetings is telling you a story he had from his father.**" Consequence: the founding record, including anything on Article VII's drafting, is gone.
30. **Nym's notes and queries:** the station's intake for its first season (4 May to end of 1896) **is not in the ledgers and is nowhere else**; the station column begins with 1897, and "inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is none." Also: "**Item 5 wants a case with a lock.**" Also: accession numbers still to be cut for Items 13 and 16.
31. **Ammon & Sons "declined to say anything of the earlier lot"** in their 2 July 1923 acknowledgment.
32. **Nobody ever stood at the tester's elbow except the state man.** Both Keddie and Loomis say it independently; Strawn is the only person in the record who ever watched the work done, and his fourth standing task was exactly that: "I stood at the tester's elbow and watched him make a test through from the sample to the reading, and **I did not let him hurry it**."
33. **Ivy's five sets of records, never in one room before 1958:** (1) the station test book; (2) the Brant weigh book; (3) the Alder Corners figures; (4) Strawn's circuit notebooks (at Farland since 1947 — "**eleven years in which nobody put a question to them**"); (5) the Association's annual ledgers.

## 11. VOICES AND JUDGMENTS — what each source thinks happened

- **Vira Toland (1979)** believes **Keddie took the cream**: "He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that is why the numbers went right the summer after… I am not going to dress it up for you. A man stands in a room by himself with the separator running and the door shut and nobody in the valley can add, and after a while he helps himself, and **it is not that he is a wicked man, it is that nobody ever came up that road and looked**." Her father would not say it out loud: "**My father said Ansel Keddie was an honest man who had got into something, which is a nice way of saying it and I never saw the difference.**" But she is uneasy about the cost: "he had all those years of this valley looking at him a certain way in church, and Orra had it too, and she outlived him a good while and had it the whole time." Her closing image: "It rained the day of that letter, I remember the wet on my father's shoulders when he came in, and **I have never in sixty years been able to think about the one without the other.**"
- **Effie Loomis (1941)** is scrupulous about the boundary between record and recollection — the whole document is written "from the minutes wherever the minutes will bear it, from my own recollection where they will not, and the two kept apart here as they ought to be kept." She names the two places the record stops (the lost minute book, the unmentioned crate) and stops there.
- **Ansel Keddie (1925)** never advances a theory: "**I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are**" — the last line of the letter and, in substance, of the statement. On the question mark of 10 July 1911: "**I will not put anything in its place that I do not know.**" On why he wrote the letter: "because I could not say it in that room, **where a man is allowed to answer and not allowed to explain**, and because it seems to me that somebody ought to know how the work was done before everybody who did it is gone." On the 1919 circular: "**I carry no charge against any man who put his name to that paper.** I only say that I stood in that room every morning of those years and I know what came into it, and that nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since."
- **Ivy Keddie (1959)** insists the case rests on measurement, not loyalty, and offers no remedy — "I have only the crate, and it is on the table."
- **Wilbur Teague (1948)** offers a **structural** verdict rather than a personal one: "**Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons.** One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator. **There was no second man in that building from the spring to the fall.** I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because I was not there and I do not know. I say that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it, and that no set of books ever made can prevent it. **The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither.**" This is the argument he gave the 1925 board — and it is exactly the argument that hanged Keddie on structure alone, with no evidence.
  - Teague's confession about evenness: "**A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture.**" And: "a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at audit."
  - Teague on Vose's knowledge: "**Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every year.** I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me."
  - Teague on his own silence: "**I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience**, and a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time after being told once has generally begun to look for another employer."
  - Teague's opening: "In my experience a bookkeeper who wants admiring has generally got something behind him that he would rather you did not turn over." Closing: "anybody who wishes to quarrel with this chapter is at liberty to go and read them, which is more than most of the quarrelers have ever done."
- **Merle Strawn (1936)** refuses to have a view at all, and that refusal is his theme: "I put down what I found. I did not put down what I thought about it, and I have not begun now. **The state pays a field man to weigh and to look, and every time in my life I have seen a field man paid for an opinion instead, the opinion has outlived the weighing and done harm after the man was dead.**" And: "I have always answered that **I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book**, and the book is open to anybody the state sends for it. **I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights.**" His method — the same four things in the same order every time: scales against sealed weights (intake and cream, loaded light and loaded heavy); lactometer on the day's milk for added water; boiler, water supply and cold room; and standing at the tester's elbow through a full test. His plants-by-smell passage: "the sour-sweet of a receiving platform in July, the lime and steam of a wash room, **the flat cold nothing of a well-run cold room, which is the smell I liked best because it meant I would have no letter to write.**"
- **Selby Vose (1926)** is the only source who publicly asserts the watering theory after 1923, and does it in the year *after* the award, from the platform, to a room with Ostrey Hollow men in it: "**Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began to look. The rise of 1924 is the measure of it.**… Nothing was done to those cows. Nothing was done to that bottom land. Nobody changed their feed and nobody changed their pasture. **The tests came up when the looking began**, and any man who wants a different explanation of that is welcome to stand where I am standing and give us one." (Ivy Keddie, thirty-three years later, does exactly that.)
  - Vose explicitly rejects the glass explanation when it is offered to him: "**I am told, and I have been told it kindly and by men who mean well, that the state man's visit of that period explains the whole thing. It does not.**… Apparatus wears. A station in daily use since 1896 will wear its fittings out twice over in that time… **I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a date and a price against it.**" The single most important fact in the case, correctly identified and dismissed as housekeeping.
  - Vose on the payment: "It was, I say to you frankly, a generous settlement of an unproved claim. This Association paid thirty-eight hundred dollars rather than let a body of its own members go on believing they had been used badly by their neighbors. There is not a business house in Ordell County that would have done it. We did it because we are not a business house; **we are the farmers of this valley in company together, and a company that will not spend money to keep peace among its own members has not understood what it is for.**" And: "**I will not have peace bought a second time.**"
  - Vose's accusation, made without a name: "I say it without naming a name, because I do not know a name and I would not use it if I did. **A little water in a can is not a felony in a man's own mind. It is a way of making a hard year come out even, and the man who does it tells himself he will stop when the price improves.**"
  - Vose defends the 1919 circular: "I wrote it because it was my duty to write it… It said that this Association pays for butterfat and cannot pay for creek water, and **I would write every word of it again tomorrow. A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them; he is being idle at their expense.**"
  - Vose's Jersey passage: his father's herd, kept on after him, **tested 4.56 in 1910** — brought forward not to boast but to refute the trade's slur "that the Larrow Valley is poor milk country, that our land is sour and our cattle are indifferent and our figures are what a man must expect off ground like ours. That is not true… and the proof of it stood in my own barn."
  - Vose's exhortation: "**Bring it as it left the cow.** Wash the cans, cool the milk, keep the dog out of the milk house, and bring it as it left the cow. Do that, and the Ostrey Hollow tests will stay where they went in 1924, and this business will be dead and buried where it belongs, and your manager can stand up here next spring and talk to you about butter prices, which is what he is paid for." Ends: "I thank you for thirteen years of your patience and I ask for one more."
  - Vose's ordinary-business report for 1925: season opened wet and stayed wet into June; hauling heavy on the horses and heavier on the men; Larrow Green ran through the flush without a day lost; butter held its place; churn room clean; ice went into the house early and lasted; no complaint of any man on the payroll; the station ran its season out and its cream came down daily and sweet.

## 12. THE HUMAN COST — the thread that runs through all eight

- **Nine families kept the 1919 letter for decades.** Toland's father read it at the table **with his hat still on, which he never did**; read it twice; her mother stood at the stove with her back to him and never turned around; he put it in the drawer of the desk in the front room and **it was still there when he died**; her brother is believed to have it yet. "One woman I knew had hers pinned inside the lid of her trunk. **You can tell people a thing like that is only business and they will still keep the paper forty years to prove what was said of them.**"
- **A brother and sister stopped speaking for life.** Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant, from 1919 until both were "long in the ground." Toland: "**and for what.**"
- **An old man was dismissed at the end of twenty-nine seasons** on a finding of no cause, and died nine years later still under it, and was cleared twenty-four years after his death.
- **The money.** $19,000 owed at 25 cents the pound; $3,800 paid; a fifth. Toland's father's share of the settlement "did not buy a hay rake."
- **The regressivity.** The best herds lost the most and nobody could know it.
- **The delay.** Thirty-five years from condemnation to explanation; the burette that would have shown it existed from 1921; the crate that would have proved it sat under a tarpaulin (or in the office) the whole time; and the state's own inspector had written "these measures will not deliver their mark" in a pocket book that sat unread in a college library for eleven years.
