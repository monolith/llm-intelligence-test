### Story 5 — Station Gain: the Answer Kept in the Association's Own Books (1897–1953)

**Who:** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Hobart Sill, buttermaker at the churn 1893–1934;
Bertram Kyle, the clerk at the outer desk for twenty-five years; Dell Rundle, schoolteacher.

**What and when:** From 1897 the station account carried, every year, three figures and a sentence.
The 1898 entry, quoted identically in r05, r07 Item 6 and r12: *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited
to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream,
60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general
account.*

**The quantities:** 57,760 ÷ 60,800 = **0.95 exactly** — the ratio of the glass, standing in the
Association's ledger from the first full year, in ink, on an open shelf, read out annually to fifty
farmers. Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the station took in **40,000,000 lb** of
milk, 1,600,000 a year; **1,444,000 lb** of fat were credited to the patrons, **1,520,000 lb**
arrived at Larrow Green in the cream, and the difference of **76,000 lb** — worth **$19,000** at 25
cents — was carried to the general account year by year as a gain.

**Why nobody read it:** three men had it and none of them used it. Teague, who called the line his
own device, put the question to the manager once, in 1916, was told to let it alone, and let it
alone for twenty-nine more years: "a figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is
furniture," and "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." Sill had the same fact
physically: the cream coming down from the Hollow was always heavier in fat than the office sheet
said had been bought — "Always more. Never less" — and he weighed it twice himself and dismissed it
as cream's nature, and nobody ever asked him for it. Kyle copied the line for a quarter of a century
in a fair hand and never wondered at it: "It had a name. That is the thing about a name. A figure
with a name on it has been explained." Kyle also watched the manager sign: "He read the totals and
he never read the lines above them."

Rundle, a schoolteacher with no dairy training, was shown the ledger while his 1939 pamphlet was
being set, and reasoned in one afternoon what Sill missed in forty-one years: "Cream stolen at the
Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make
my paragraph stand."

**Documents:** the ledger entry (r05, r07, r12, and r23 independently describing the same annual
line from the copying desk); r14 (the receiving books, Nym's Item 16, whose Larrow Green average
runs "a little under two tenths of a point better" over the same years); r12's note added in press.

### Story 6 — The Circular, and Two Families (1919–1934)

**Who:** Selby Vose, manager, who signed it; Ansel Keddie, who carried it; Emmet Brant, his wife's
brother and the station's largest patron; Orra Keddie, sister to one and wife to the other.

**What and when:** In June 1919 a printed letter over the manager's signature went to the patrons at
the head of the valley warning them, in temperate language, that added water would be looked for and
would be dealt with. It named nobody. Its premise was false and the manager had been told so: the
state's own inspector had tested for water at that station on every visit and never found any, "and
I told him so." The 1908 committee had already found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, in
print, eleven years before.

Keddie would not put the letters in the mail nor leave them on the platform. He took them round
himself, stood at the door while each man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat
still on. Two days later Emmet Brant came up, would not come into the house, stood in the yard and
said he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and Ansel knew it better than
any man living — and then asked him why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it.
Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home and never
entered that house again.

**Quantities:** nine families of record received it (r01, r09, r17); Orra counted eleven doors her
husband stood at. Ten years of silence, 1919 to Emmet's death in the spring of 1929 — "Ten years and
neither of them would go up the road first, and now it is settled the way those things get settled."
Before it, the two families ate Sunday dinner together and had for thirty years. Orra afterward
walked the long way to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane.

Vose defended every word of it in 1926 and would have written it again. Sill, at Larrow Green,
applauded it. It is the only act in the record that a hostile witness, a friendly witness and the
state inspector all describe the same way and evaluate three different ways.

**Documents:** r24 (contemporaneous, and the only account of the delivery); r01 (a recipient's
daughter, aged seven); r03; r06 (Strawn's 1919 entry); r08 (the author's defense); r09 (Emmet's
grievance, which r24 shows was the question he actually asked in the yard); r12; r14; r23 (the clerk
who typed it).

### Story 7 — The Burette and the Board (1921–1925)

**Who:** Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector; Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin; Ansel Keddie.

**What and when:** In 1921 the state began issuing graduated burettes to its field men for checking
the delivery of measures and pipettes — the one instrument that can catch a pipette from outside. In
1922 Strawn visited Ostrey Hollow and did not run their glass, "the day being short and the road
home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened." It cost a year.

On **12 June 1923** he ran their milk pipettes against the burette: *These measures will not deliver
their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is
glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them.* The crate went up into the loft over the intake
room. The Association ordered replacements from Ammon & Sons on 28 June; the firm answered on 2
July, entered the order, shipped that month, and declined to say anything of the earlier lot. New
glass was in use from July 1923. In 1924, the first full year on true glass, the station averaged
**3.80**.

The patrons petitioned in January 1925, in the winter, when a farmer has time to be angry. The board
of arbitration sat from 17 March to 4 May under Frick, chosen chairman because he was the one of the
three who kept no cow. It sat three times: once at Ashlin, then twice in the station's own weigh
room at Frick's request, so the board might see the plant instead of hearing it described — with the
condemned crate in the loft twenty feet above their heads. Three explanations were urged, each by a
man of standing, and none proved. Keddie gave a statement of nine sentences, standing.

He was dismissed **30 April 1925**, four days before the award and while the board was still
sitting. On 4 May the board *found no cause established* and awarded the patrons **$3,800, without
admission, to be charged to the station account.** Frick: "An award without a finding is an
unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it," and "a man who cannot account for a
figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author."

**Documents:** r06 and r07 Item 9 (the condemnation entry); r02 and r07 Item 7 (the award); r03 (the
statement); r13 (the exhibit list — four books, the fourth being the 1923 notebook); r23 (the clerk
who carried those four books in and out); r08; r21; r24.

### Story 8 — A Ladder in February (1958–1972)

**Who:** Ivy Keddie, graduate student at Farland, aged twenty-seven, Ansel's granddaughter;
Professor Leland Vaught; Rosalie Cudd of Alder Corners; Halbert Nym, curator.

**What and when:** In February 1958 Ivy Keddie climbed the ladder in the old station — shut since
1946 and used for hay tools — and found the crate under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff. On **9
March 1958** she delivered the nine unbroken pipettes against a calibrated burette at Farland, five
runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. **Mean 16.72 c.c. Short
0.88. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for.**

She assembled five record sets that had never been in one room: the station test book, the Brant
weigh book (lent by Roy Brant and returned), the Alder Corners figures copied out for her by Rosalie
Cudd, Strawn's circuit notebooks (at Farland since 1947, unasked-for for eleven years), and the
Association's ledgers. Bulletin No. 471 was issued in October 1958: *No milk was watered at Ostrey
Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the
station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in
twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the
Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain.*

**The arithmetic of delay:** condemned 1923, solved 1958 — thirty-five years. Dismissed 1925 —
thirty-three. Dead since 1934 — twenty-four years when the sheet was read that cleared him.

**The aftermath, and its errors:** the *Ashlin Chronicle* ran it on the front page in October 1958,
correct on the glass and wrong on nearly every number it touched, and accused the glassmaker of
fraud. The county agent wrote a sober report in 1959 and appended two signed corrections to it.
Rundle reprinted his pamphlet with a footnote. The books went to three buildings and are none of
them the family's: test book and crate to the Historical Rooms in 1959, weigh book in 1963, the
Alder Corners book to Vessey County in 1970, Strawn's notebooks at Farland.

**Documents:** r04, r07, r10, r11, r18 (custody), r19, r20, r22.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Derivation: Ansel and Orra married in 1889;
their son Duncan was born in 1894 and taught school at Ashlin; Duncan's daughter is Ivy, born 1931.
Duncan states all three links himself in a document written expressly to fix the family record, and
notes that he had "already twice seen it printed otherwise" (r18) — which is exactly right: r19
calls her his great-niece and r22 does on its page two. r22's author then wrote to check and
appended a signed correction: "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error
on page two is mine." Both wrong versions descend from the same college press release, so they are
one source, not two. Ivy herself, in 1959, speaks of "my grandfather" throughout (r04), and r24's
editor says the same. Settled.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** Derivation, not testimony: Orra Keddie and
Emmet Brant were brother and sister. Their children — Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant — are therefore
first cousins. Their children — Ivy and Hazel — are the children of first cousins, which is second
cousins. Duncan sets it out in exactly those terms (r18); Roy Brant (r09), Rundle's 1959 footnote
(r12) and Hazel herself (r17) all agree, and Hazel adds the reason it keeps being got wrong: "it is
only that nobody wants to count." Against this stand r01 and r21, both saying "first cousin once
removed" — valley talk, and both are hearsay about a family neither narrator belongs to. The
derivation settles it regardless of how many people repeat the other phrase.

*What the sources leave open in the intervening generation:* whether Orra and Emmet were full
siblings or half. Roy Brant insists they were "full brother and sister, same mother and same father,
whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it" (r09); Hazel says Orra
was "a half sister to my grandfather Emmet, by his father's second wife" (r17) — and Hazel is Roy's
own daughter contradicting him. Orra's diary calls Emmet "my brother" throughout without qualifying
it, which is what either relation would produce. Duncan says only that his mother was "Emmet Brant's
sister, the younger of the two." I cannot settle it. It does not change the cousin degree either
way; it would only make Duncan and Roy half-first-cousins.

**B3. Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's son.** Four sources on that side, and the best of them are inside
the office: Selby says it himself in his 1926 address — "My father, who was your first president";
Teague, who worked under both, calls him the son of the late president; Duncan Keddie states it
flatly as one of the things "always got wrong" (r18); and Kyle, who sat outside his door for
twenty-five years, says "the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story
twice in the last year and it is nonsense" (r23). Against: r01 and r12, both reporting valley talk,
and Rundle's version is the store's version tidied. Settled: son.

*Why it matters:* Gideon wrote Article VII's last clause, which made the station's own test final
and unappealable; his son then managed the Association for twenty-five years, signed the annual
station sheet carrying the gain line every year, told his bookkeeper in 1916 to let the station-gain
question alone, circulated the 1919 accusation against the patrons, and in 1926 defended it and
filed the condemnation of the glass under routine housekeeping. The rule that made the error
uncorrectable and the management that declined to look at the error were the same household across
two generations — which is the fact the "nephew" version blurs.

**B4. Between the Voses and the Keddies: not established, and probably nothing.** The claim is that
Orra Keddie and old Mrs Vose were first cousins — asserted by Vira Toland as common knowledge
("everybody knew that," r01) and echoed vaguely by Cleve Oram, who had it from his mother's mother
and admits "I could not draw you the line on paper" (r21). Two hearsay reports, one of them
explicitly second-hand about the other's generation. Against them stands Orra's own son, who says
there was no connection whatever, that the confusion is with the unrelated Ashlin Voses, that he has
heard it from two women in that town, and that his mother — a woman who "could not pass a headstone
without giving you the connection" — never once put a Vose in her account of the valley's kinships
(r18). Orra's diary, thirty-three years of it, never mentions a Vose relative either, though that is
weak negative evidence.

I decline to settle it. The balance favors Duncan: he is inside the family, he addresses the rumor
directly rather than reporting it, and he offers a mechanism for the mistake. Note also that nothing
evidential turns on it. If Vira were right it would only add a motive shading to the 1919 circular —
that the letter was "somebody's cousin" speaking — and the circular's factual falsity is already
established by the state inspector, independent of who was related to whom.
