# Answer sheet — Ostrey Hollow / Larrow Valley (r01–r24)
## Part 1 of 3 — Section A, stories 1–4

*Method note for the whole sheet: a document quoted identically in two or more retellings outranks
any recollection; a contemporaneous entry outranks a later memory of the same event; arithmetic that
closes outranks arithmetic that does not; and narrators repeating a figure from a common upstream
source are counted as one source, not two.*

---

### Story 1 — A Rule Written at a Kitchen Table (1889–1892)

**Who:** Gideon Vose, farmer, and the men of the Larrow Valley; Effie Loomis, who kept their minutes
for thirty-nine years.

**What and when:** In 1889 the farmers of the valley, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking
what the merchant allowed them, met in the schoolhouse at Larrow Green in cold weather and organized
the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose was elected first president and held
the office twenty-two years, to 1911. He drafted the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, not
being a lawyer. In 1891 they built the creamery at Larrow Green on the creek below the mill, set up
the churn, and the valley began to be paid in cash once a month. In 1892, at the annual meeting the
year after the creamery opened, they adopted Article VII.

**The quantities that matter:** one sentence. *Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers
according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not
according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*
The first two clauses are a just rule and were understood as one. The last clause — *the test of the
station where he delivers shall govern* — is the hinge of everything that follows. It made one
instrument in one building final, with no appeal to the creamery, to the valley average, or to a
test made anywhere else. Under payment by weight, nothing in the seven stories that follow would
have cost any man a dollar.

**Documents:** Article VII is quoted identically in r02 (Effie Loomis), r07 Item 1 (Nym) and r12
(Rundle) — three transcriptions of one printed pamphlet, so one document, and reliable. r13 (Frick)
and r22 (Falke) gloss it. The date is contested: 1892 (r02), 1891 (r12), 1893 (r15, hedged as "as I
have always understood it"). I take 1892: Effie kept the minute book and dates it to the annual
meeting after the 1891 creamery, which fits both rival dates as approximations of the same event.
The printed copies carry no year (r07), the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office
removal (r02), and r22 rightly declines to state a year at all. Gideon Vose died in 1917 and could
never be asked what he meant by the last clause.

### Story 2 — Seventy-Seven Dollars and Ninety-Three Cents (1895–1897)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, tester; Hobart Sill, buttermaker; Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet.

**What and when:** The pull down the valley in the nineties was three hours in mud and impossible in
March, so the Association built a skimming station seven miles up at Ostrey Hollow. Ansel Keddie
went up in 1895 to get it ready. It opened 4 May 1896: milk weighed in, sampled, tested for
butterfat, separated, the cream hauled down to Larrow Green, the skim handed back at the platform
for the patron's hogs. Keddie's first leaf reads *Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own
comes.* Sill supplies the human fact behind that line — it was his own glassware, made up at his own
bench, sent up the valley on the wagon because the station's outfit had been ordered and had not
come; he had it back the next spring with one piece chipped and never said a word about it. That
borrowed glass was known-good working glass, which is why the station's first season averaged 3.80.

Ammon & Sons invoiced the station's own outfit on 14 April 1897 and it went into use on 1 May 1897.

**Quantities:** 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per
cent neck, at .22 — 31.68; 1 acid measure — .90; 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00; freight — 2.15;
**total 77.93**. Seventy-two pipettes. Every one of them blown to a mark of 17.6 c.c. and delivering,
on later measurement, a mean of 16.72 — short 0.88 c.c., one part in twenty.

**How it happened:** the firm's own historian sets it down (r20). The mold had been reground and the
lot ran short; the works books that would have shown it burned in 1931. The check that should have
caught it could not: a boy gauges the first, the tenth and the fiftieth of a lot and finds them
consistent — "they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find."

**Documents:** the invoice, quoted identically in r07 Item 2 and reproduced in facsimile in r20 —
independent custody, same text. The test book's first leaf (r03, r07 Item 3). Orra Keddie's diary,
1 May 1897: *A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green*
(r24) — contemporaneous, and it settles the date against r11's and r23's "1898." r14 for the
borrowed glass; r19's charge that Ammon & Sons sold short glass knowingly is unsupported and is
answered by r20.

### Story 3 — The Land Takes the Blame (1902–1926)

**Who:** the patrons of Ostrey Hollow; Nyle Grigg's investigating committee; Dr Roland Kepp, V.S.

**What and when:** From 1902 it was said at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk did not test with
the rest. The first men to say it out loud were laughed at. The complaint grew for twenty-three
years, "and it took the shape such things always take, which is that somebody must be doing it on
purpose." In January 1908 the board appointed a committee under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, who was
not a patron of the station and had nothing to gain. They visited each of the six Hessel Bottom
farms, some three and four times, drew and sealed their own samples, watched the herds milked and
the cans weighed in, stood by while the test was made, tried the station scales against a known
weight and the patrons' scales against the station's, and inquired into every barn's water supply.
They checked everything in that building except the pipette, and had nothing to check it with.

Their report found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter" — clearing patrons and tester alike,
in print, eleven years before the manager accused the patrons and seventeen before the board let the
bookkeeper insinuate against the tester — and then concluded that the wet hay of the bottom thinned
the cream and "the remedy lies with the land and not with the station."

**Quantities that matter:** the sampling ran **January, February and March 1908** — winter only, the
one window in which bog hay could have had any effect. Bulletin No. 214 (1911) measured the effect:
bog hay as the whole roughage depressed the test by **not more than five hundredths** in winter and
by **nothing measurable on summer pasture**. Against a gap of 0.19 standing in July as in February,
that is four times too small and the wrong shape.

**How the error hardened:** in 1919 Grigg reprinted the report with a covering letter claiming they
had sampled "through the whole of 1908 — every month," and quoting the College's figure as "two
tenths." In 1926 Kepp repeated the two tenths, laid it beside his own unmeasured "fifth of a point"
and the manager's inflated 0.21, and declared that three independent figures agreed. They were one
figure, misquoted, meeting two others that were wrong. Kepp dismissed the 1911 evidence as "six
weeks of summer figures" without ever asking what the station's other summers read.

**Documents:** r15 (the full report and Grigg's letter), r07 Item 11, r16 (Kepp), r09, r22 — whose
author conceded the hay theory and then withdrew the concession on reading Bulletin 214's actual
words.

### Story 4 — Nine Weeks in 1911

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, alone in the station, writing two lines in his own book.

**What and when:** *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.* The tests rose at
once from 3.61 to 3.80 and stood there. *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are
back where they were. ? — A.K.* They fell to 3.61 the same day. Nine weeks. Orra Keddie's diary for
8 May 1911 records only that A. came home cross about a broken measure and that the low piece was
under water again.

**Why it matters:** it is an experiment that ran itself. One variable changed — the glass. Held
constant: the same herds, the same patrons, the same ground, the same pasture in the same season,
the same acid, the same bottles, the same hand tester, the same scales, the same man turning the
crank at the same speed, the same book. The figures moved twice, in the right direction each time,
on the two days the instrument changed. Roy Brant confirms independently, from his father's weigh
book, that the rise was general: every farm on the Bottom came up together and went down together,
"and he could make nothing of it, and I could make nothing of it either, and there it sat for near
on fifty years."

It disposes at a stroke of every explanation that looks to the herds, the ground or the men, because
none of those was altered in those nine weeks and the figures were. No calculation can do that: the
arithmetic of 1958 shows the glass *could* account for the gap; the nine weeks show that when the
glass was removed the gap went with it.

**Quantities:** 8 May – 10 July 1911; nine weeks; 3.61 → 3.80 → 3.61; a rise of 0.19, which is
exactly the annual gap and exactly five percent of 3.80.

**What Keddie did with it:** he wrote a question mark and let it stand for fourteen years — "I could
not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do
not know." His son says he came home that summer saying it was something in the glass and never
dared write it (r18); the arbitration's chairman says he privately suspected the patrons (r13). Both
are single recollections of private remarks, decades after, pointing opposite ways. What is on paper
is the question mark.

**Documents:** the two entries, quoted identically in r03 and r07; the 1922 summary leaf (r03, r04,
r07) which records the nine weeks in their place at 3.80; r09; r11 §IV; r22 ¶6; r24. Two narrators
call the interval six weeks (r05, r16) and both are refuted by the book's own dates.
