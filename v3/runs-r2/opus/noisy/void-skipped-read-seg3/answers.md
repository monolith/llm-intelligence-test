# Answer sheet — Ostrey Hollow / Larrow Valley (r01–r24)
## Part 1 of 3 — Section A, stories 1–4

*Method note for the whole sheet: a document quoted identically in two or more retellings outranks
any recollection; a contemporaneous entry outranks a later memory of the same event; arithmetic that
closes outranks arithmetic that does not; and narrators repeating a figure from a common upstream
source are counted as one source, not two.*

---

### Story 1 — A Rule Written at a Kitchen Table (1889–1892)

**Who:** Gideon Vose, farmer, and the men of the Larrow Valley; Effie Loomis, who kept their minutes
for thirty-nine years.

**What and when:** In 1889 the farmers of the valley, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking
what the merchant allowed them, met in the schoolhouse at Larrow Green in cold weather and organized
the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose was elected first president and held
the office twenty-two years, to 1911. He drafted the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, not
being a lawyer. In 1891 they built the creamery at Larrow Green on the creek below the mill, set up
the churn, and the valley began to be paid in cash once a month. In 1892, at the annual meeting the
year after the creamery opened, they adopted Article VII.

**The quantities that matter:** one sentence. *Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers
according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not
according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*
The first two clauses are a just rule and were understood as one. The last clause — *the test of the
station where he delivers shall govern* — is the hinge of everything that follows. It made one
instrument in one building final, with no appeal to the creamery, to the valley average, or to a
test made anywhere else. Under payment by weight, nothing in the seven stories that follow would
have cost any man a dollar.

**Documents:** Article VII is quoted identically in r02 (Effie Loomis), r07 Item 1 (Nym) and r12
(Rundle) — three transcriptions of one printed pamphlet, so one document, and reliable. r13 (Frick)
and r22 (Falke) gloss it. The date is contested: 1892 (r02), 1891 (r12), 1893 (r15, hedged as "as I
have always understood it"). I take 1892: Effie kept the minute book and dates it to the annual
meeting after the 1891 creamery, which fits both rival dates as approximations of the same event.
The printed copies carry no year (r07), the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office
removal (r02), and r22 rightly declines to state a year at all. Gideon Vose died in 1917 and could
never be asked what he meant by the last clause.

### Story 2 — Seventy-Seven Dollars and Ninety-Three Cents (1895–1897)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, tester; Hobart Sill, buttermaker; Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet.

**What and when:** The pull down the valley in the nineties was three hours in mud and impossible in
March, so the Association built a skimming station seven miles up at Ostrey Hollow. Ansel Keddie
went up in 1895 to get it ready. It opened 4 May 1896: milk weighed in, sampled, tested for
butterfat, separated, the cream hauled down to Larrow Green, the skim handed back at the platform
for the patron's hogs. Keddie's first leaf reads *Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own
comes.* Sill supplies the human fact behind that line — it was his own glassware, made up at his own
bench, sent up the valley on the wagon because the station's outfit had been ordered and had not
come; he had it back the next spring with one piece chipped and never said a word about it. That
borrowed glass was known-good working glass, which is why the station's first season averaged 3.80.

Ammon & Sons invoiced the station's own outfit on 14 April 1897 and it went into use on 1 May 1897.

**Quantities:** 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per
cent neck, at .22 — 31.68; 1 acid measure — .90; 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00; freight — 2.15;
**total 77.93**. Seventy-two pipettes. Every one of them blown to a mark of 17.6 c.c. and delivering,
on later measurement, a mean of 16.72 — short 0.88 c.c., one part in twenty.

**How it happened:** the firm's own historian sets it down (r20). The mold had been reground and the
lot ran short; the works books that would have shown it burned in 1931. The check that should have
caught it could not: a boy gauges the first, the tenth and the fiftieth of a lot and finds them
consistent — "they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find."

**Documents:** the invoice, quoted identically in r07 Item 2 and reproduced in facsimile in r20 —
independent custody, same text. The test book's first leaf (r03, r07 Item 3). Orra Keddie's diary,
1 May 1897: *A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green*
(r24) — contemporaneous, and it settles the date against r11's and r23's "1898." r14 for the
borrowed glass; r19's charge that Ammon & Sons sold short glass knowingly is unsupported and is
answered by r20.

### Story 3 — The Land Takes the Blame (1902–1926)

**Who:** the patrons of Ostrey Hollow; Nyle Grigg's investigating committee; Dr Roland Kepp, V.S.

**What and when:** From 1902 it was said at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk did not test with
the rest. The first men to say it out loud were laughed at. The complaint grew for twenty-three
years, "and it took the shape such things always take, which is that somebody must be doing it on
purpose." In January 1908 the board appointed a committee under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, who was
not a patron of the station and had nothing to gain. They visited each of the six Hessel Bottom
farms, some three and four times, drew and sealed their own samples, watched the herds milked and
the cans weighed in, stood by while the test was made, tried the station scales against a known
weight and the patrons' scales against the station's, and inquired into every barn's water supply.
They checked everything in that building except the pipette, and had nothing to check it with.

Their report found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter" — clearing patrons and tester alike,
in print, eleven years before the manager accused the patrons and seventeen before the board let the
bookkeeper insinuate against the tester — and then concluded that the wet hay of the bottom thinned
the cream and "the remedy lies with the land and not with the station."

**Quantities that matter:** the sampling ran **January, February and March 1908** — winter only, the
one window in which bog hay could have had any effect. Bulletin No. 214 (1911) measured the effect:
bog hay as the whole roughage depressed the test by **not more than five hundredths** in winter and
by **nothing measurable on summer pasture**. Against a gap of 0.19 standing in July as in February,
that is four times too small and the wrong shape.

**How the error hardened:** in 1919 Grigg reprinted the report with a covering letter claiming they
had sampled "through the whole of 1908 — every month," and quoting the College's figure as "two
tenths." In 1926 Kepp repeated the two tenths, laid it beside his own unmeasured "fifth of a point"
and the manager's inflated 0.21, and declared that three independent figures agreed. They were one
figure, misquoted, meeting two others that were wrong. Kepp dismissed the 1911 evidence as "six
weeks of summer figures" without ever asking what the station's other summers read.

**Documents:** r15 (the full report and Grigg's letter), r07 Item 11, r16 (Kepp), r09, r22 — whose
author conceded the hay theory and then withdrew the concession on reading Bulletin 214's actual
words.

### Story 4 — Nine Weeks in 1911

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, alone in the station, writing two lines in his own book.

**What and when:** *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.* The tests rose at
once from 3.61 to 3.80 and stood there. *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are
back where they were. ? — A.K.* They fell to 3.61 the same day. Nine weeks. Orra Keddie's diary for
8 May 1911 records only that A. came home cross about a broken measure and that the low piece was
under water again.

**Why it matters:** it is an experiment that ran itself. One variable changed — the glass. Held
constant: the same herds, the same patrons, the same ground, the same pasture in the same season,
the same acid, the same bottles, the same hand tester, the same scales, the same man turning the
crank at the same speed, the same book. The figures moved twice, in the right direction each time,
on the two days the instrument changed. Roy Brant confirms independently, from his father's weigh
book, that the rise was general: every farm on the Bottom came up together and went down together,
"and he could make nothing of it, and I could make nothing of it either, and there it sat for near
on fifty years."

It disposes at a stroke of every explanation that looks to the herds, the ground or the men, because
none of those was altered in those nine weeks and the figures were. No calculation can do that: the
arithmetic of 1958 shows the glass *could* account for the gap; the nine weeks show that when the
glass was removed the gap went with it.

**Quantities:** 8 May – 10 July 1911; nine weeks; 3.61 → 3.80 → 3.61; a rise of 0.19, which is
exactly the annual gap and exactly five percent of 3.80.

**What Keddie did with it:** he wrote a question mark and let it stand for fourteen years — "I could
not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do
not know." His son says he came home that summer saying it was something in the glass and never
dared write it (r18); the arbitration's chairman says he privately suspected the patrons (r13). Both
are single recollections of private remarks, decades after, pointing opposite ways. What is on paper
is the question mark.

**Documents:** the two entries, quoted identically in r03 and r07; the 1922 summary leaf (r03, r04,
r07) which records the nine weeks in their place at 3.80; r09; r11 §IV; r22 ¶6; r24. Two narrators
call the interval six weeks (r05, r16) and both are refuted by the book's own dates.

### Story 5 — Station Gain: the Answer Kept in the Association's Own Books (1897–1953)

**Who:** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Hobart Sill, buttermaker at the churn 1893–1934;
Bertram Kyle, the clerk at the outer desk for twenty-five years; Dell Rundle, schoolteacher.

**What and when:** From 1897 the station account carried, every year, three figures and a sentence.
The 1898 entry, quoted identically in r05, r07 Item 6 and r12: *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited
to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream,
60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general
account.*

**The quantities:** 57,760 ÷ 60,800 = **0.95 exactly** — the ratio of the glass, standing in the
Association's ledger from the first full year, in ink, on an open shelf, read out annually to fifty
farmers. Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the station took in **40,000,000 lb** of
milk, 1,600,000 a year; **1,444,000 lb** of fat were credited to the patrons, **1,520,000 lb**
arrived at Larrow Green in the cream, and the difference of **76,000 lb** — worth **$19,000** at 25
cents — was carried to the general account year by year as a gain.

**Why nobody read it:** three men had it and none of them used it. Teague, who called the line his
own device, put the question to the manager once, in 1916, was told to let it alone, and let it
alone for twenty-nine more years: "a figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is
furniture," and "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." Sill had the same fact
physically: the cream coming down from the Hollow was always heavier in fat than the office sheet
said had been bought — "Always more. Never less" — and he weighed it twice himself and dismissed it
as cream's nature, and nobody ever asked him for it. Kyle copied the line for a quarter of a century
in a fair hand and never wondered at it: "It had a name. That is the thing about a name. A figure
with a name on it has been explained." Kyle also watched the manager sign: "He read the totals and
he never read the lines above them."

Rundle, a schoolteacher with no dairy training, was shown the ledger while his 1939 pamphlet was
being set, and reasoned in one afternoon what Sill missed in forty-one years: "Cream stolen at the
Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make
my paragraph stand."

**Documents:** the ledger entry (r05, r07, r12, and r23 independently describing the same annual
line from the copying desk); r14 (the receiving books, Nym's Item 16, whose Larrow Green average
runs "a little under two tenths of a point better" over the same years); r12's note added in press.

### Story 6 — The Circular, and Two Families (1919–1934)

**Who:** Selby Vose, manager, who signed it; Ansel Keddie, who carried it; Emmet Brant, his wife's
brother and the station's largest patron; Orra Keddie, sister to one and wife to the other.

**What and when:** In June 1919 a printed letter over the manager's signature went to the patrons at
the head of the valley warning them, in temperate language, that added water would be looked for and
would be dealt with. It named nobody. Its premise was false and the manager had been told so: the
state's own inspector had tested for water at that station on every visit and never found any, "and
I told him so." The 1908 committee had already found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, in
print, eleven years before.

Keddie would not put the letters in the mail nor leave them on the platform. He took them round
himself, stood at the door while each man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat
still on. Two days later Emmet Brant came up, would not come into the house, stood in the yard and
said he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and Ansel knew it better than
any man living — and then asked him why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it.
Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home and never
entered that house again.

**Quantities:** nine families of record received it (r01, r09, r17); Orra counted eleven doors her
husband stood at. Ten years of silence, 1919 to Emmet's death in the spring of 1929 — "Ten years and
neither of them would go up the road first, and now it is settled the way those things get settled."
Before it, the two families ate Sunday dinner together and had for thirty years. Orra afterward
walked the long way to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane.

Vose defended every word of it in 1926 and would have written it again. Sill, at Larrow Green,
applauded it. It is the only act in the record that a hostile witness, a friendly witness and the
state inspector all describe the same way and evaluate three different ways.

**Documents:** r24 (contemporaneous, and the only account of the delivery); r01 (a recipient's
daughter, aged seven); r03; r06 (Strawn's 1919 entry); r08 (the author's defense); r09 (Emmet's
grievance, which r24 shows was the question he actually asked in the yard); r12; r14; r23 (the clerk
who typed it).

### Story 7 — The Burette and the Board (1921–1925)

**Who:** Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector; Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin; Ansel Keddie.

**What and when:** In 1921 the state began issuing graduated burettes to its field men for checking
the delivery of measures and pipettes — the one instrument that can catch a pipette from outside. In
1922 Strawn visited Ostrey Hollow and did not run their glass, "the day being short and the road
home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened." It cost a year.

On **12 June 1923** he ran their milk pipettes against the burette: *These measures will not deliver
their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is
glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them.* The crate went up into the loft over the intake
room. The Association ordered replacements from Ammon & Sons on 28 June; the firm answered on 2
July, entered the order, shipped that month, and declined to say anything of the earlier lot. New
glass was in use from July 1923. In 1924, the first full year on true glass, the station averaged
**3.80**.

The patrons petitioned in January 1925, in the winter, when a farmer has time to be angry. The board
of arbitration sat from 17 March to 4 May under Frick, chosen chairman because he was the one of the
three who kept no cow. It sat three times: once at Ashlin, then twice in the station's own weigh
room at Frick's request, so the board might see the plant instead of hearing it described — with the
condemned crate in the loft twenty feet above their heads. Three explanations were urged, each by a
man of standing, and none proved. Keddie gave a statement of nine sentences, standing.

He was dismissed **30 April 1925**, four days before the award and while the board was still
sitting. On 4 May the board *found no cause established* and awarded the patrons **$3,800, without
admission, to be charged to the station account.** Frick: "An award without a finding is an
unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it," and "a man who cannot account for a
figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author."

**Documents:** r06 and r07 Item 9 (the condemnation entry); r02 and r07 Item 7 (the award); r03 (the
statement); r13 (the exhibit list — four books, the fourth being the 1923 notebook); r23 (the clerk
who carried those four books in and out); r08; r21; r24.

### Story 8 — A Ladder in February (1958–1972)

**Who:** Ivy Keddie, graduate student at Farland, aged twenty-seven, Ansel's granddaughter;
Professor Leland Vaught; Rosalie Cudd of Alder Corners; Halbert Nym, curator.

**What and when:** In February 1958 Ivy Keddie climbed the ladder in the old station — shut since
1946 and used for hay tools — and found the crate under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff. On **9
March 1958** she delivered the nine unbroken pipettes against a calibrated burette at Farland, five
runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. **Mean 16.72 c.c. Short
0.88. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for.**

She assembled five record sets that had never been in one room: the station test book, the Brant
weigh book (lent by Roy Brant and returned), the Alder Corners figures copied out for her by Rosalie
Cudd, Strawn's circuit notebooks (at Farland since 1947, unasked-for for eleven years), and the
Association's ledgers. Bulletin No. 471 was issued in October 1958: *No milk was watered at Ostrey
Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the
station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in
twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the
Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain.*

**The arithmetic of delay:** condemned 1923, solved 1958 — thirty-five years. Dismissed 1925 —
thirty-three. Dead since 1934 — twenty-four years when the sheet was read that cleared him.

**The aftermath, and its errors:** the *Ashlin Chronicle* ran it on the front page in October 1958,
correct on the glass and wrong on nearly every number it touched, and accused the glassmaker of
fraud. The county agent wrote a sober report in 1959 and appended two signed corrections to it.
Rundle reprinted his pamphlet with a footnote. The books went to three buildings and are none of
them the family's: test book and crate to the Historical Rooms in 1959, weigh book in 1963, the
Alder Corners book to Vessey County in 1970, Strawn's notebooks at Farland.

**Documents:** r04, r07, r10, r11, r18 (custody), r19, r20, r22.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Derivation: Ansel and Orra married in 1889;
their son Duncan was born in 1894 and taught school at Ashlin; Duncan's daughter is Ivy, born 1931.
Duncan states all three links himself in a document written expressly to fix the family record, and
notes that he had "already twice seen it printed otherwise" (r18) — which is exactly right: r19
calls her his great-niece and r22 does on its page two. r22's author then wrote to check and
appended a signed correction: "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error
on page two is mine." Both wrong versions descend from the same college press release, so they are
one source, not two. Ivy herself, in 1959, speaks of "my grandfather" throughout (r04), and r24's
editor says the same. Settled.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** Derivation, not testimony: Orra Keddie and
Emmet Brant were brother and sister. Their children — Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant — are therefore
first cousins. Their children — Ivy and Hazel — are the children of first cousins, which is second
cousins. Duncan sets it out in exactly those terms (r18); Roy Brant (r09), Rundle's 1959 footnote
(r12) and Hazel herself (r17) all agree, and Hazel adds the reason it keeps being got wrong: "it is
only that nobody wants to count." Against this stand r01 and r21, both saying "first cousin once
removed" — valley talk, and both are hearsay about a family neither narrator belongs to. The
derivation settles it regardless of how many people repeat the other phrase.

*What the sources leave open in the intervening generation:* whether Orra and Emmet were full
siblings or half. Roy Brant insists they were "full brother and sister, same mother and same father,
whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it" (r09); Hazel says Orra
was "a half sister to my grandfather Emmet, by his father's second wife" (r17) — and Hazel is Roy's
own daughter contradicting him. Orra's diary calls Emmet "my brother" throughout without qualifying
it, which is what either relation would produce. Duncan says only that his mother was "Emmet Brant's
sister, the younger of the two." I cannot settle it. It does not change the cousin degree either
way; it would only make Duncan and Roy half-first-cousins.

**B3. Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's son.** Four sources on that side, and the best of them are inside
the office: Selby says it himself in his 1926 address — "My father, who was your first president";
Teague, who worked under both, calls him the son of the late president; Duncan Keddie states it
flatly as one of the things "always got wrong" (r18); and Kyle, who sat outside his door for
twenty-five years, says "the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story
twice in the last year and it is nonsense" (r23). Against: r01 and r12, both reporting valley talk,
and Rundle's version is the store's version tidied. Settled: son.

*Why it matters:* Gideon wrote Article VII's last clause, which made the station's own test final
and unappealable; his son then managed the Association for twenty-five years, signed the annual
station sheet carrying the gain line every year, told his bookkeeper in 1916 to let the station-gain
question alone, circulated the 1919 accusation against the patrons, and in 1926 defended it and
filed the condemnation of the glass under routine housekeeping. The rule that made the error
uncorrectable and the management that declined to look at the error were the same household across
two generations — which is the fact the "nephew" version blurs.

**B4. Between the Voses and the Keddies: not established, and probably nothing.** The claim is that
Orra Keddie and old Mrs Vose were first cousins — asserted by Vira Toland as common knowledge
("everybody knew that," r01) and echoed vaguely by Cleve Oram, who had it from his mother's mother
and admits "I could not draw you the line on paper" (r21). Two hearsay reports, one of them
explicitly second-hand about the other's generation. Against them stands Orra's own son, who says
there was no connection whatever, that the confusion is with the unrelated Ashlin Voses, that he has
heard it from two women in that town, and that his mother — a woman who "could not pass a headstone
without giving you the connection" — never once put a Vose in her account of the valley's kinships
(r18). Orra's diary, thirty-three years of it, never mentions a Vose relative either, though that is
weak negative evidence.

I decline to settle it. The balance favors Duncan: he is inside the family, he addresses the rumor
directly rather than reporting it, and he offers a mechanism for the mistake. Note also that nothing
evidential turns on it. If Vira were right it would only add a motive shading to the 1919 circular —
that the letter was "somebody's cousin" speaking — and the circular's factual falsity is already
established by the state inspector, independent of who was related to whom.

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) The tests read 95 percent of the truth — short by one part in twenty.**
Nine pipettes out of the condemned crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c., delivered against a calibrated
burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71.
Mean = 150.48 ÷ 9 = **16.72 c.c.** Short 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.**
16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly.**
The reading is direct: the neck of the test bottle is ruled to read percent *for a 17.6 c.c. charge
and no other*. Put in 95 percent of the charge and the fat column is 95 percent as long, so the
figure read off is 95 percent of the true percentage. (I reject the rival mean of 16.27 c.c.; see
E1. 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244, which yields a true average of 3.905 that its own author admits cannot be
reconciled with the 3.80 the station actually made on new glass.)

**(b)** Recorded average **3.61**. True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Short by **0.19 of a
point.** Three independent confirmations that 3.80 is the true value: the station's own first season
on borrowed good glass (3.80), the nine weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure (3.80), and 1924, the
first full year on new glass (3.80). Note the gap is *proportional*, not fixed: 0.19 is five percent
of 3.80, so a rich herd lost more than a poor one — 4.56 recorded was short 0.24, 3.42 recorded was
short 0.18.

**(c)** 25 full delivery years, 1898–1922; 40,000,000 lb of milk, averaging 1,600,000 lb a year.
Credited: 40,000,000 × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb**.
True: 40,000,000 × 3.80% = **1,520,000 lb**.
Not credited: **76,000 lb of butterfat.**

**(d)** Butterfat averaged **25 cents** a pound over the period: 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**.
The patrons received the 1925 award of **$3,800**, which is $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **exactly one
fifth** — and it was charged to the station account, i.e. paid out of the account into which the
gain had been credited.

### C2 — Emmet Brant, 1910

Leaf quoted identically in r07 Item 4 and r09: *Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred
thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42.*

**(a)** Credited: 200,000 × 3.42% = **6,840 lb of butterfat.**
**(b)** True test: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60** exactly. True fat: 200,000 × 3.60% = **7,200 lb.**
Owed and not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb.** (Check: 360 ÷ 7,200 = 5.00 percent.)
**(c)** Butterfat was **24 cents** in 1910 — Roy Brant gives the year's price specifically (r09).
360 × $0.24 = **$86.40** for that year. (At the 25-cent period average it would be $90.00; I use the
year-specific price because the question asks for that year.)

### C3 — the two denominators

**(a)** Credited to the patrons over 1898–1922: **1,444,000 lb.**
**(b)** Reached Larrow Green in the station's cream: **1,520,000 lb.**
**(c)** Difference 76,000 lb, and the percentage depends entirely on what you divide by:
- 76,000 ÷ **1,520,000** (fat actually delivered) = **5.00 percent** — and this is the one that is
  also the glass ratio, one part in twenty.
- 76,000 ÷ **1,444,000** (fat credited) = **5.263 percent** — the ledger's "five and a quarter per
  cent, as usual," because a bookkeeper reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered.

Neither is an error; each is right of its own denominator and of no other. Two competent men slipped
on exactly this: Teague's prose attaches the 5¼ figure to "the fat that was actually in the cream"
(that would be 5.00), and Vaught's §V says "five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually
delivered." The single-year 1898 entry checks the same way: 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00%; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 =
5.26%; and 57,760 ÷ 60,800 = 0.95 exactly.

### C4

**(a) $77.93.** Check by addition: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **77.93**. Two narrators
render it "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents" (r19, r20) — and r20 does so on the same
page as the invoice she reproduces, which reads *Total — 77.93*. Digits transposed; arithmetic
breaks the tie.

**(b) Six dozen = 72 pipettes**, at .35 each (72 × 0.35 = 25.20, which is the invoice's own line, so
the count is checkable from the money). Each short **0.88 c.c.** (17.6 blown, 16.72 delivered). Two
narrators say five dozen (r11, r21); five dozen at .35 would be $21.00, not $25.20.

### C5

**(a) Two different numbers, and they should be different.**
- *The glass was in use* from **1 May 1897** to **12 June 1923** — 26 years and about 6 weeks. This
  is the span Bulletin 471 names, and it is the physical fact: how long the short pipettes were in
  the building.
- *The money runs over* **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922.** This is an accounting span: 1897 was
  a part year on the new glass (the test book itself records "1897, part year") and 1923 was split
  between short glass and new glass in July. The intake figure of 40,000,000 lb is the total for the
  complete years only, so the shortfall computed from it covers complete years only. The calculation
  is therefore conservative — it omits eight months of 1897 and five and a half of 1923.

**(b) Cannot be determined from the sources.** The curator states the position exactly: the
station's intake for its first season, 4 May to the end of 1896, "is not in the ledgers and is
nowhere else; the station column begins with 1897, and inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be
told there is none" (r07). Two men later supplied one from memory and they do not agree: Sill, in
1934, "about nine hundred thousand pounds"; Kyle, in 1953, "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds,
which is what I understood at the time from men who were there." That is a 22 percent spread between
two recollections, one of them explicitly second-hand, thirty-eight and fifty-seven years after the
season. What the sources establish is that no document gives the figure and that the two estimates
bracket roughly a million pounds. That is not enough to state one.

## Section D — Logic

### D1 — the 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and how long:** 8 May to 10 July 1911, **nine weeks**, at the Ostrey Hollow station.

**The variable:** the milk pipette — the station's own broke and a substitute was used. Nothing else
in the plant or on the farms was touched.

**Held constant:** the same six farms and the same herds, the same ground, the same pasture in the
same season of the same year, the same acid, the same test bottles, the same four-bottle hand
tester, the same scales, the same operator turning the crank at the speed he was taught, the same
composite-sample routine, the same book in the same hand.

**The result:** the station's average went from 3.61 to 3.80 the day the substitute came in and back
to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned — and it moved for everybody at once. Roy Brant,
reading his father's weigh book fifty years later and knowing nothing of any of this, describes the
same rise and the same fall across every farm on the Bottom.

**What it establishes that arithmetic cannot:** the 1958 measurement shows the glass *could* produce
a 5 percent shortfall. The nine weeks show that when that glass was removed the shortfall went with
it and when it came back the shortfall came back — a causal test, not a consistency check. It
excludes the herds, the feed, the wet ground, the season, the weather, the operator's method and the
operator's honesty simultaneously, because not one of them changed while the figures did, twice. No
designed trial could have controlled better, and it cost nothing; it ran itself and lay unread for
forty-seven years.

**What it does not establish:** (i) the *size* of the true error — nine summer weeks are a sample,
not an annual average, and taken alone they cannot tell you whether the deficit was 0.19 all year;
(ii) *which* instrument was at fault in what degree — only that the two differed, not that the
substitute was correct; (iii) the mechanism — that the error was proportional rather than a fixed
subtraction comes from the glassware and the ruled neck, not from the window; (iv) where the
substitute came from, which three narrators answer three ways (Larrow Green, r04; "up from town,"
r05; the state inspector's own measure, r09 — and r09 is impossible as stated, since the substitute
was in use from 8 May and the inspector did not visit until June).

### D2 — why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 — the cream arrived heavy, every year, in two independent records.** Sill weighed the
Hollow's cream himself as it came into the cold room, over the platform man's weighing, and it was
always more fat than the office sheet said had been bought at the station: "Always more. Never
less." The Association's ledger records the identical surplus as station gain, 5¼ percent, every
year from 1898. Stolen cream reaches the creamery *light*. Rundle drew the inference in one
afternoon: "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green
heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand."

**Reason 2 — the nine weeks of 1911.** The same man ran the same station under no different
supervision, one instrument changed, and the tests rose 0.19 and fell back the day his own measure
returned. A thief does not begin and end his thieving on the two days his pipette breaks and is
replaced, nor steal in exact proportion to the richness of each patron's milk.

**Reason 3 — the outside tests.** Four Hessel Bottom men hauled to Alder Corners over Redlow Ridge
in the summer flush for sixteen seasons, 1915–1930. The season averages of the four taken together
run 3.78 to 3.82, no trend, one summer with another about 3.80 — the station's *true* value, made by
a different maker on his own glass in another county. In sixteen summers Jerome Cudd never made one
complaint of those men and never docked one of them a cent.

**Reason 4 — the 1908 committee.** Farmers who drew and sealed their own samples and stood at the
tester's elbow reported "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter," and asked that the finding be
printed in the same type as the rest.

**Why these are independent:** four different observers, in four decades, using four different
physical instruments and standing in four different buildings — a buttermaker's scale at Larrow
Green, a tester's book at the station, a cheesemaker's vat in Vessey County, a committee's sealed
samples in six barns. None of them knew what the others had; none was assembled with any other until
1958. Reason 1 alone is not one source but two, since the ledger and the receiving book were kept by
different men for different purposes and agree.

**Which would still hold if the glass had never been measured:** all four, but Reason 1 is the
cleanest — it is arithmetic in the Association's own ledger, available every year from 1898, needing
no instrument and no expert.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question those entries?** No. Teague invented the line, kept it for forty
years, called it "furniture," raised the station-gain question with the manager exactly once — in
1916 — and dropped it when told to let it alone. Then in 1925 he stood before the board and put the
theft theory obliquely, describing at length how such an account would look if a man were helping
himself and then observing that he made no charge against anybody, while the ledger in his own hand
disproved it. His own verdict on himself: "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its
conscience."

### D3 — did the manager know the tests ran low?

**Yes, certainly — and the sources let us be exact about what he knew.**

He knew *the tests ran low*. The complaint had been public since 1902 and was renewed at annual
meetings for a quarter of a century; the 1908 committee reported on it to the president and
directors; he signed the annual station sheet every year from 1913; and in 1919 he printed and
circulated a letter whose entire premise is that the Hollow's milk was coming in thin. You cannot
accuse nine families of watering their cans without knowing their tests are below everyone else's.
He also knew his premise had been contradicted: the state's inspector told him to his face in 1919
that he had tested for water at that station on every visit and never found any. He circulated it
anyway, and defended every word of it in 1926.

He knew *the question had been raised inside his own office*. In 1916 his bookkeeper brought him the
station-gain question and was told to let it alone. That is the single most damaging fact in the
record about him: the exact figure that solved the case was put in front of the manager nine years
before the arbitration, by the man who kept it, and was closed down.

What is **not** established is that he understood the gain line as the counterpart of the low tests —
that the surplus fat at Larrow Green was the same 5 percent the patrons were losing. Kyle, watching
him sign for twenty-five years, says he read totals and never the lines above them: "He was not a
man who read. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign."
So: knowledge of the shortfall, yes, beyond argument; knowledge of its cause, no; and an active
refusal to have the one connected to the other in 1916. In 1926 he was still filing the condemnation
of the glass as "a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a date and a price
against it."

## Section F — Theory

### F1 — What produced the shortfall

The station's own milk pipettes, bought from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet on 14 April 1897 and put into
use on 1 May 1897, were blown to a mark of 17.6 c.c. and delivered a mean of 16.72 — short 0.88 c.c.,
one part in twenty. The butterfat test does not weigh anything. A measured charge of milk goes into a
bottle whose neck is *ruled to read percent for that charge and no other*; acid dissolves everything
but the fat; the bottle is spun; and the operator reads the length of the fat column off the rulings
and calls it a percentage. He is entitled to do that for one reason only — that the charge is a fixed
and known quantity. Put in 95 percent of the charge and every reading is 95 percent of the truth.

**Why that size:** because the error is the ratio of the glass and nothing else. 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95.
A recorded 3.61 is a true 3.80; the gap of 0.19 is five percent of 3.80, not a fixed subtraction —
which is why the richest herds lost most (4.56 short by 0.24; 3.42 short by 0.18) and why no man
could see it, since the ratio between any two patrons at that station was undisturbed. Its herds
ranked correctly against one another. Only against the outside world was it wrong.

**Why it began when it did:** the station opened in May 1896 on glass borrowed from Larrow Green —
Sill's own working glass, known good — and averaged 3.80 that season. The Ammon crate replaced it on
1 May 1897 and the average fell to 3.61 in 1898, the first full year, and stayed within three
hundredths of 3.61 for a quarter of a century. The lot ran short because a mold had been reground
and nobody caught it; a gauge check on the first, the tenth and the fiftieth of a lot finds them
consistent, and they were — consistently wrong, which is the one fault such a check cannot find.

**Why it ended when it did:** the state began issuing burettes to its field men in 1921. On 12 June
1923 the inspector ran the station's pipettes against his and condemned the whole crate. New glass
went in that July; 1924, the first full year on it, averaged 3.80.

**Why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change for either:** because the fault was in
the measure itself, and a plant cannot catch that. The scales can be checked against test weights,
the acid against a standard, the bottle's graduation with mercury, the tester's speed by counting.
The pipette is checked against nothing, because in the ordinary practice of the trade *the pipette
is the standard*. An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it. It
can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside. And the error was precise, not scattered:
a leak or a slipping scale gives a scattered error, and scatter attracts attention; a short pipette
delivers the same short charge every time, in January and in July, for a careful operator and a
careless one, for twenty-six years. It has the appearance of excellent work.

### The three competing explanations, and what defeats each

**1. Water in the cans.** Advanced by Selby Vose, the manager — circulated to the patrons over his
signature in 1919 and urged before the board in 1925, "without heat and without proof." *Defeated
by:* the state inspector's lactometer at every visit from 1907 to 1936, never once finding added
water, and his telling Vose so to his face in 1919; by the cheesemaker over the ridge, who bought
that same milk for sixteen summers — "A cheesemaker knows watered milk inside a day… the curd comes
soft and it will not knit… Cheese is a witness" — and never docked one of those men a cent; and
fatally by arithmetic, since watered milk cannot deliver *surplus* fat, and the Hollow's cream
arrived at Larrow Green heavy every year for twenty-five years.

**2. Cream stolen by the tester.** Advanced by Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, obliquely before the
board in 1925, and held by the valley as settled opinion. *Defeated by:* the cream arriving heavy
(the receiving book and the ledger, two records by two men); by the nine weeks of 1911, when the same
man under no different supervision produced figures 0.19 higher and then 0.19 lower on the two days
his measure changed; and by the 1908 committee's finding of no dishonesty of any kind, in any
quarter, made by men who sealed their own samples and stood at his elbow.

**3. The wet hay of Hessel Bottom.** Advanced by the 1908 investigating committee under Nyle Grigg,
revived and dressed in mineral chemistry by Dr Roland Kepp in 1926, and conceded briefly by the
county agent in 1959. *Defeated by:* Bulletin No. 214's actual finding — not more than five
hundredths in winter and nothing measurable on summer pasture, against a gap four times that size;
by the gap's indifference to season, standing at the same value in July as in February, in wet years
and dry, on pasture and on hay, which is the wrong shape for any feed effect; and by the Alder
Corners table, where the same milk off the same wet ground made about 3.80 over the ridge for
sixteen summers. The county agent, on reading the bulletin's real words, withdrew his concession in
writing: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in
July."

### Subsidiary 1 — Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?

**No.** The secretary took down every word of all three sessions and states flatly that the crate was
never mentioned in the hearing at all, by the patrons, by the officers or by the board, and that the
award's phrase about "the books and appliances of the station" was a form of words and not a list of
anything.

But the record of the condemnation *was* in evidence and unread. Frick's own exhibit list, reprinted
from his file, has four items, and the fourth is "The circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector
for the year 1923" — the very book containing the 12 June entry condemning the whole crate. The
clerk who carried the books in and out names the same four and adds the sharpest sentence in the
record: "I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the
second beside the fourth and looked." And two of the three sessions were held in the station's own
weigh room at Frick's request, with the crate in the loft about twenty feet above the board's heads.

### Subsidiary 2 — What did the tester himself believe?

**Undetermined, and the sources point in opposite directions.** What Keddie *wrote* is a question
mark, and a refusal to fill it: "I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will

Two men report private remarks. His son says he came home in the summer of 1911 saying it was
something in the glass, said it twice, and never dared write it, having nothing but a suspicion
against the outfit he had been taught to trust (r18). The chairman of the board says that in the
corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told him privately he had always thought the patrons were at fault and
would not say so in the room, having lived among them thirty years (r13). Each is a single
recollection of an unwitnessed conversation, set down twenty-one and forty-three years later, and
each teller has an interest — Frick complicating the martyr reading, Duncan rescuing his father's
prescience. I cannot choose between them, and I decline to.

What both agree on, and what the documents support, is the trait rather than the belief: he would
not record what he could not prove. Duncan calls it "the most expensive virtue anybody in that
valley ever had."

## Section G — Summary (150 words)

For twenty-six years the Larrow Valley creamery's hill station at Ostrey Hollow tested every
patron's milk with pipettes that delivered 16.72 c.c. where they were marked 17.6 — five percent
short — so every test read 3.61 where the truth was 3.80. Under a by-law making the station's own
test final, that cost the patrons 76,000 pounds of butterfat, about $19,000, entered year by year in
the Association's own ledger as "station gain." The valley blamed the wet hay, then the farmers,
then the tester, who was dismissed in 1925 without a finding and died disgraced in 1934. His
granddaughter found the condemned crate in a loft in 1958 and measured it.

It took eight records because no one held two at once: a ledger, a farm book, a diary, an
inspector's notebook, a cheesemaker's table, a hearing file, a glassmaker's invoice, and one
question mark.
