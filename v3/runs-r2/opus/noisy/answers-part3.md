# Section C — Math

## C1.

**(a) 95 percent — one part in twenty short.**
The pipettes were blown to a mark of **17.6 c.c.**, the standard charge in the state and the charge
the graduated neck of the test bottle is ruled for. Ivy Keddie measured the nine survivors against a
calibrated burette on 9 March 1958, five deliveries each, averaged: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72,
16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — **mean 16.72 c.c.**

    16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9500 exactly
    short 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.;  0.88 ÷ 17.6 = 0.05 = 1/20

Because the operator reads a percentage directly off a neck ruled for a fixed charge, a charge
short by one twentieth yields a reading short by one twentieth. Every test at that station read
**95 percent of the truth**.

*Cross-check, independent of the glass:* the ledger's 1898 entry gives 57,760 lb credited against
60,800 lb received; 57,760 ÷ 60,800 = 0.9500 exactly. The glass and the books agree to four places.

*Rejected:* Vaught's body text gives 16.27 c.c. and a ratio of 0.9244. 16.27 is 16.72 with the
digits transposed; it yields a true average of 3.905, which Vaught himself concedes "is not readily
reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the
glassware," and it contradicts the conclusion printed in his own bulletin. The *Ashlin Chronicle*
repeats 16.27 from the same press release. Arithmetic breaks the tie.

**(b) True average 3.80; recorded tests short by 0.19 of a point.**

    3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80
    3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19

Confirmed three ways by observation rather than calculation: 1896 on borrowed true glass read 3.80;
the nine weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure read 3.80; 1924, the first full year on new glass,
read 3.80.

**The gap was proportional, not fixed** — 0.19 is 5 percent of 3.80. A herd recorded at 4.56 lost
0.24 of a point; a herd recorded at 3.42 lost 0.18. The richer the milk, the more was taken from it.

**(c) 76,000 lb of butterfat.**
Over the 25 full delivery years 1898–1922 the station took in **40,000,000 lb** of milk, averaging
1,600,000 lb a year.

    credited at 3.61%:  40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb
    delivered at 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb
    difference                                =    76,000 lb

**(d) $19,000, of which the patrons received exactly one fifth.**

    76,000 lb × $0.25 = $19,000
    award of $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 = one fifth

Butterfat averaged 25 cents the pound over the period. That figure is not Ivy's guess: Bertram Kyle
says he was set to find it for the 1925 arbitration out of the Association's own books and did find
it — "twenty-five cents the pound, taking the fat years with the lean." Sill and Kepp both say
twenty-seven cents from impression; at 27c the same 76,000 lb would be $20,520. I use 25c because it
is the documented figure and the one the bulletin used.

*Rejected:* $19,600 (Rundle and the *Chronicle*); 67,000 lb (Frick, repeated by Oram) — that is the
patrons' own 1925 estimate, made from pay slips without knowing the mechanism, and it is a
creditable 12 percent low; 118,000 lb (Vaught's body, the 16.27 cascade).

## C2. Emmet Brant, 1910

From the weigh book leaf, quoted identically by Nym, Roy Brant and Hazel Brant: *"Year 1910.
Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing.
Paid on a test of 3.42."*

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb of butterfat.**

**(b) Owed and not credited:**

    true test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60 exactly
    true fat  = 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb
    shortfall = 7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb

(Check: 360 is 5.00% of 7,200 and 5.26% of 6,840 — the same two denominators as the ledger line.)

**(c) In money:** Roy Brant gives the price of butterfat in 1910 specifically as **twenty-four
cents**. 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40** in that one year. At the 25-cent long-run average it would be
$90.00. Roy Brant on what that means cumulatively: "Seventeen hundredths is nothing to look at on
one pay slip. Set the slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm."

## C3. The two denominators, 1898–1922

**(a) Credited to the patrons:** 1,444,000 lb.
**(b) Actually reached Larrow Green in the station's cream:** 1,520,000 lb. (The milk was correctly
weighed and correctly separated; only the *credit* was short, so the fat that physically went down
the valley was the true fat.)
**(c) The difference, 76,000 lb, expressed as a percentage — of what:**

    76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 5.00%  — of the butterfat actually delivered and received
    76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 5.26%  — of the butterfat credited to the patrons

Both are correct of their own denominator and of no other. **The Association's ledger phrase "five
and a quarter per cent, as usual" is the second one** — a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus
against the figure he has already entered. **Five percent is the one that is also the glass**
(0.88/17.6). Two narrators attach the 5.25 figure to the wrong denominator — Teague ("the gain ran
at five and a quarter percent *of the fat that was actually in the cream*") and Vaught ("The gain is
five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered") — and both are wrong in the same
way. Nym's formulation is the correct one: "each is right of its own denominator and of no other."

## C4. The invoice

**(a) $77.93.** Check the addition: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93. Two narrators —
Junia Ammon in her own prose, facing her own facsimile, and the *Ashlin Chronicle* — read it as
"seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents." The line items settle it.

**(b) Six dozen = 72 pipettes**, at 35 cents apiece (6 × 12 × 0.35 = $25.20, which is the invoice
line). Each was short by **0.88 c.c.** on the measured mean (17.6 − 16.72); across the nine
survivors the individual shortfalls ran 0.86 to 0.90 c.c.

*Rejected:* "five dozen," said by Vaught and by Cleve Oram independently of each other. The invoice
is a quoted document and its arithmetic checks: five dozen at .35 would be $21.00, not $25.20.

## C5.

**(a) The glass was in service about twenty-six years; the money runs over twenty-five full years.**

- *In service:* **1 May 1897 to July 1923** — condemned 12 June 1923, replaced within the month.
  From 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 is 26 years and 6 weeks, less the nine weeks of 1911 when a
  borrowed measure was used: call it **twenty-six years, less about three weeks, of actual short
  testing.** Junia Ammon's own phrase is "six and twenty years afterward."
- *The money calculation:* **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922.**

They are different numbers because the two end years are broken years. The station's own glass went
in on 1 May 1897, so 1897 is a part year on the new lot — the test book itself records "1897, part
year" and gives no average for it. 1923 is split between the condemned glass (to mid-June) and the
new glass (from July), so it belongs wholly to neither regime. The 25-year figure is therefore the
run of *complete* years for which the ledgers carry a full intake and a stable 3.61 average, and it
is the only span over which 40,000,000 lb and 76,000 lb can honestly be computed. The 26-year figure
is the duration of the fault. Bulletin 471 states the fault's span correctly ("from the first of
May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923") while the arithmetic underneath it runs on the 25 years —
which is right, and which no narrator explains.

**(b) The station's first-season intake cannot be determined from the sources.**

Nym is explicit: "The station's intake for its first season, 4 May to the end of 1896, is not in the
ledgers and is nowhere else; the station column begins with 1897, and inquirers wanting a figure for
1896 must be told there is none." Kyle, from the office side, independently explains *why*: that
first season the milk was weighed at the Hollow and carried on the creamery's own books, and the
sheets were never put with the station papers.

Two men supplied a number from memory anyway, and they do not agree: Sill in 1934 said "about nine
hundred thousand pounds," and Kyle in 1953 said "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is
what I understood at the time from men who were there. It is not in any book I ever ruled." They
are roughly 20 percent apart, both are recollections at forty and fifty-seven years' distance, and
neither is documentary. What the sources establish is that the station opened 4 May 1896, ran that
part-season on borrowed glass, and averaged 3.80 — not what it took in. **Answer: cannot be
determined; the range offered by the two surviving recollections is 900,000–1,100,000 lb.**

# Section D — Logic

## D1. The nine weeks of 1911

**When and how long:** 8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks and three days — recorded in the tester's
own hand in the station test book on both dates, and corroborated the same day by Orra Keddie's
diary ("A. home cross about a broken measure").

**The variable:** the milk pipette. The station's own measure broke and a borrowed one was
substituted, then withdrawn.

**Held constant:** the patrons, the herds, the pasture and the ground, the season and the weather,
the feed, the acid, the test bottles, the hand tester, the intake scales, the testing procedure and
the hour of the day, and the operator himself.

**The result:** the station's average went from 3.61 to 3.80 for the duration and returned to 3.61
on the day its own measure came back — a swing of 0.19, exactly the shortfall the glass implies.
Roy Brant confirms from his father's independent private book that the rise was general across every
farm on the Bottom, so it is not an artifact of the station's own bookkeeping.

**What it establishes that a calculation would not:** the calculation (16.72/17.6 = 0.95) rests on
an assumption — that the pipettes measured in 1958 are the lot that was in use, and are in the
condition they were in when used. The 1911 window needs no such assumption. It is a contemporaneous,
in-situ demonstration that swapping *one object* moved the figures by the full amount and swapping
it back moved them back, recorded by a man who did not know what he was recording and who set a
question mark against it because he could not explain it. It converts an inference about glassware
into an observation about this station. It also kills, in one stroke, every explanation that looks
to the land, the herds, the feed, the season or the men, because none of those was altered.

**What it does not establish.** (i) It does not fix the *size* of the error over the other
twenty-five years: the borrowed measure was never gauged, so 3.80 is the reading of an
unauthenticated substitute, not a certified truth. (ii) It does not say *what* was wrong with the
station's pipettes — only that they gave different readings from another measure. (iii) It says
nothing about when the fault began or whether it was constant; the flat month-to-month gap and the
1896 and 1924 figures do that separately. (iv) It does not by itself deductively exclude theft,
since the same man tested throughout — it makes theft absurd (a thief would have had to suspend
himself for exactly nine weeks and resume on 10 July), not impossible. (v) It cannot show that the
fault lay in delivery volume rather than in some other property of the glass; only Ivy's burette
does that.

Kepp's dismissal — "six weeks in the flush of the season… I could produce you six weeks in any year
that would prove anything you liked" — fails on the station's own book, which contains every other
summer of every other year at 3.61. If flush season lifted the figure, 1911 would not be unique.
He never asked what the other summers read.

## D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 — the cream arrived heavy at Larrow Green, every year, and was weighed twice.** Hobart
Sill, the buttermaker: "the office would send down its sheet and the sheet would say so many pounds
of fat bought at the station, and the fat that came into this room off that wagon was more than the
sheet said. Always more. Never less… The platform man weighed it and I weighed it over again when it
came into the cold room, because I have never in my life taken another man's figure for what was
going into my churn." Dell Rundle reasoned to the conclusion from the ledger and retracted his own
published accusation while the type was being set: "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached
the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand."
Bertram Kyle confirms the entry's form from twenty-five years of copying it. Stolen cream cannot
arrive as surplus fat.

**Reason 2 — the 1911 window.** Same man, same room, no change of supervision; the figures rose the
week the measure changed and fell the day it came back. Theft cannot be switched off for nine weeks
and on again to the day. Falke, the county agent, states this argument in exactly that form.

**Reason 3 — the shape of the error.** The loss was a fixed *fraction*, not a fixed quantity: a
constant twentieth of every patron's fat, so that the richest milk lost most in absolute terms
(4.56 recorded → 0.24 short; 3.42 recorded → 0.18 short), identical in July and February, in wet
years and dry, for a careful day and a careless one, for a quarter of a century. No hand can steal
to two decimal places in exact proportion to a number it has not yet computed. As Vaught puts it,
"a leaking pipe or a slipping scale gives a scattered error, and scatter attracts attention"; this
error "has the appearance of excellent work."

*(Two more, for completeness: the 1908 investigating committee stood by while the test was made and
reported "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter"; Strawn stood at Keddie's elbow and watched a
test through on every visit for twenty-eight years and never let him hurry it.)*

**Why these are independent.** Reason 1 is a physical weighing seven miles away, made by a man who
went up the valley twice in his life and who believed the opposite theory; it uses the creamery's
receiving book, not the station's book. Reason 2 uses the station's test book and Emmet Brant's
private farm book. Reason 3 is a property of the distribution of the figures and needs no witness at
all. No two of them share a source, a document or an interest.

**Which survives without the 1958 measurement:** Reason 1 entirely. The heavy cream was recorded in
Sill's receiving books from 1893 and entered in the Association's own ledgers every year from 1897;
it required no burette, no college and no glassware to read. Reason 3 also survives, since it is
derivable from the pay figures alone. Both were available in 1925 and earlier.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refute him?** Once, and then never again. Teague
put the station-gain question to Selby Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone, and let it alone
for the next twenty-nine years. He described the line as "furniture" — "a figure that comes in at
five and a quarter every year is furniture" — and excused himself on principle: "I was the
Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." Kyle, who copied the same line, says it never
crossed his mind to wonder at it and does not believe it crossed anybody else's. And then in 1925
Teague went before the board and "answered at length about how such an account would look if a man
were helping himself, and then observed that he made no charge against anybody" — using, to
insinuate theft, the one entry in the Association's books whose direction proves theft did not
occur. He never questioned it, and he never noticed that it pointed the other way.

## D3. What Selby Vose knew before June 1923

**He certainly knew the station's tests ran low. There is no evidence he knew, or suspected, that
anything at the station was wrong — and there is direct testimony that he did not read the line
that would have told him.**

For knowledge of the low tests: the complaint had been made openly since 1902 and renewed at every
annual meeting; the Association appointed a committee to investigate it in 1908 and the report went
to the president and directors; he signed the annual station sheet every year; and in 1919 he
circulated a printed letter to the Ostrey Hollow patrons whose entire premise was that their milk
came in thinned. You do not warn nine or eleven families about water unless you already know their
figures are short. He said so in his own address in 1926.

He also knew the ledger carried a standing surplus at that station, because Teague put the
station-gain question to him in 1916 and was told to let it alone. That is the single most damaging
fact about him: not that he failed to notice, but that he was asked and declined the inquiry.

Against reading anything into his signature stands Kyle, the one witness placed to see: "He read the
totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read. He would take a sheet,
turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times."

And he was told his theory was false by the state's own inspector in 1919 — Strawn: "I have tested
for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told
him so" — and went on defending the circular in 1926 without mentioning it.

So: he knew the tests were low, he knew the station's account showed a yearly gain, he had been told
the watering explanation was unsupported, and he suppressed the one inquiry that might have found
the cause. Whether he ever consciously joined the low tests to the station gain **cannot be
determined**; his conduct is fully explained by incuriosity and a manager's preference for a theory
that blames the patrons, and no source shows him thinking further. He treated the 1923 condemnation
itself as "a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a date and a price
against it."

# Section E — Contradictions

Grouped: (I) resolved conflicts; (II) conflicts the sources cannot settle; (III) two narrators
carrying the same wrong value; (IV) narrators contradicting themselves on the page. Items appear
once, in the group that matters most.

## I. Resolved

1. **The award.** $3,800 (Effie Loomis and Nym, both transcribing the minute book; Ivy, Teague,
   Vose, Frick, Falke, Kyle) vs **$3,500** (Vira Toland, Cleve Oram). *Resolution:* the award is
   quoted verbatim from the board's own minute book; a quoted document outranks two valley
   recollections at fifty-odd years.
2. **Selby Vose's paternity.** Son (Vose's own words, Teague, Duncan Keddie, Kyle) vs nephew (Vira,
   Rundle). *Resolution:* a man's own "my father, who was your first president," plus the clerk who
   sat outside his door for twenty-five years.
3. **Rosalie Cudd.** Daughter of Jerome Cudd (herself, Rundle, Duncan Keddie) vs niece (Nym, the
   *Chronicle*). *Resolution:* her own letter; and Nym's own provenance line ("from its founder to
   his daughter in 1941") contradicts his own label.
4. **Orra Brant: full or half sister to Emmet.** Full (Roy Brant emphatically, Duncan Keddie, Orra
   herself) vs half, by the father's second wife (Hazel Brant). *Resolution:* Roy denounced this
   exact rumor in 1955 — "people who like a story with a stepmother in it" — and his daughter is
   repeating it in 1981.
5. **When the station's own glass went into use.** 1 May **1897** (the invoice of 14 April 1897;
   Nym; Bulletin 471's conclusion; Orra's diary entry of that day; Junia Ammon; the *Chronicle*)
   vs 1 May **1898** (Vaught's body text; Kyle, "I have that from the correspondence").
   *Resolution:* a contemporaneous diary entry dated to the day, plus a dated invoice six weeks
   before it.
6. **The 1908 sampling window.** January–March 1908 (the report itself; corroborated by Orra's
   diary, which records the committee arriving in January and gone by March) vs "the whole of 1908,
   every month" (Grigg's covering letter). *Resolution:* the document beneath his own signature,
   plus an independent contemporaneous diary.
7. **Bulletin No. 214's finding.** "Not more than five hundredths in winter, and nothing that could
   be measured on summer pasture" (quoted identically by Ivy, Nym, Vaught, and by Falke, who sent
   for the bulletin himself in 1959) vs "two tenths" (Grigg 1919, Kepp 1926). *Resolution:* the
   bulletin's own summary paragraph; the "two tenths" is a fourfold inflation with the decisive
   summer clause dropped.
8. **The condemnation date.** 12 June 1923 (Strawn's dated notebook entry, an exhibit in 1925;
   Bulletin 471; Nym; Falke) vs 12 July 1923 (Vose, Rundle) vs 1921 (Hazel) vs 1922 (Kyle).
   *Resolution:* the inspector's own book. Kyle's 1922 is probably contaminated by Strawn's real
   1922 visit, the one at which he did *not* run the burette; Hazel's 1921 is the year the burettes
   were issued.
9. **Pipettes bought.** Six dozen (invoice) vs five dozen (Vaught, Oram). *Resolution:* the
   invoice's own arithmetic — 6 × 12 × $0.35 = $25.20, the billed line.
10. **Mean delivery.** 16.72 c.c. (Ivy's measurement sheet, transcribed by two hands) vs 16.27
    (Vaught's body, the *Chronicle*). *Resolution:* arithmetic — 16.72/17.6 = 0.95 exactly, which
    reproduces the ledger's 57,760/60,800 and the observed 3.61→3.80; 16.27 gives 3.905, which
    Vaught himself flags as irreconcilable.
11. **Station intake and average.** 40,000,000 lb and 3.61 (Effie, Ivy, Nym, Vaught, and Kyle, who
    got the figure out himself for the arbitration) vs 44,000,000 (Teague, Rundle) and 3.16
    (Teague, Frick). *Resolution:* the test book's own summary leaf says 3.61; 3.16 is 3.61
    transposed; Rundle and Frick are both downstream of Teague.
12. **The 1911 interval.** Nine weeks (the two dated entries) vs six weeks (Teague, Kepp).
    *Resolution:* subtract the dates — 8 May to 10 July.
13. **The 1924 average.** 3.80 (the station book; Strawn) vs 3.90 (Vose) vs 3.85 (Falke; also
    Sill's estimate of the Hollow's true value). *Resolution:* the station's own book, confirmed by
    the inspector's independent entry.
14. **Where the crate was found.** The station loft (Ivy, who found it; Duncan; Hazel; the
    *Chronicle*; Oram, who had put his hand on it there; Falke) vs the Association office at Ashlin
    (Nym). *Resolution:* the finder plus five corroborations, including Strawn's 1928 complaint
    about the cluttered loft.
15. **The Brant weigh book's start.** 1 January 1897 (Nym; Frick's contemporaneous exhibit list;
    Duncan Keddie) vs 1 January 1898 (Roy Brant) vs 1900 (Hazel Brant). *Resolution:* two
    documentary sources and one disinterested one. Note the drift: the keeper's son is off a year,
    the keeper's granddaughter three.
16. **Farms on Hessel Bottom.** Six (the 1908 report, Roy Brant, Kepp, Hazel, the *Chronicle*) vs
    seven (Effie Loomis). *Resolution:* the report's committee visited each farm and counted them.
17. **Keddie's exhibit book vs the Brant weigh book.** Nym heads the station test book "kept by
    E. Brant" and the farm weigh book "kept by A. Keddie." *Resolution:* transposed by the curator;
    Frick's 1925 exhibit list, the test book's own first leaf, Ivy, Roy Brant and Duncan all fix it
    the other way.
18. **Strawn's circuit start.** 1907 (Strawn himself) vs 1908 (Ivy; Falke, "from 1908 forward").
    *Resolution:* his own statement and his own 1907 first-round entry.
19. **When Vose became manager.** 1913 (Effie; Vose's own "It is thirteen years this spring, since
    1913") vs 1911 (Kyle). *Resolution:* Vose's own arithmetic in his own address.
20. **Alder Corners overlapping seasons.** Sixteen (Rosalie Cudd, tabulated in full, 1915–1930) vs
    fourteen (Ivy) vs one, the 1919 season (Nym). *Resolution:* sixteen were copied and sent; the
    overlap with the short-glass years is eight (1915–1922) or nine (through 1923); Ivy's fourteen
    fits neither and is unexplained; Nym has mistaken the specimen leaf quoted in the file for the
    whole file.
21. **Pipettes surviving.** Nine measured (Ivy's sheet, listing nine readings; Nym; Falke; Oram) vs
    eleven (Rundle's 1959 footnote; the *Chronicle*, "eleven in a condition to be measured").
    *Resolution:* reconcilable — nine sound pipettes were measured and two were chipped at the tip,
    making eleven survivors in the crate. The *Chronicle*'s gloss is the error.
22. **Cream lost in transit.** Cleve Oram's father, who drove the wagon for twenty years, "always
    said there was less of it coming off that platform than went onto it." *Resolution:* refuted by
    the receiving end. Sill weighed that cream twice himself daily and it was always heavier than
    the station's sheet, and the Association's ledger recorded the surplus as station gain every
    year. The hauler's impression is the theft theory arriving from a third direction, and the same
    document defeats it.
23. **The supplier's culpability.** The *Chronicle*: "A cheap short lot was put off on the
    Association knowingly, and the firm's silence in 1923 is the proof of it." *Resolution:* no
    source offers any evidence of knowledge; the firm's own historian, writing against her own
    house, reconstructs a reground mold and a consistency check that cannot catch a consistent
    error, and prints the 1923 letter as the thing she regrets. Silence is not proof of knowledge.
24. **The 1908 committee and the 1925 board.** The *Chronicle*: "The committee that sat on the
    matter in 1908 sat again in 1925." *Resolution:* false. The 1908 committee was Association
    members under Nyle Grigg; the 1925 body was a three-man board of arbitration chaired by Alonzo
    Frick, an attorney, and no source puts any 1908 member on it.
25. **Where the Association's office was.** Ashlin, one room over a harness shop (Teague, who sat
    in it; Nym; Duncan Keddie; Oram, who says the hearing opened "down at Ashlin, in the
    Cooperative's office") vs Larrow Green (Kyle's own account of his desk). *Resolution:* Ashlin,
    on weight and on the test book's documented custody there 1925–1959. Kyle may have begun at the
    creamery, but the office he describes — manager's door, general ledger, station accounts — was
    at Ashlin.

## II. Cannot be settled from the sources

26. **How many families received the 1919 circular.** Nine (Vira Toland, whose household got one;
    Roy Brant; Hazel Brant) vs **eleven** (Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary, from the household
    of the man who hand-delivered them). The documentary rule favors eleven; the count of
    independent memories favors nine — and Hazel is downstream of Roy, so there are two independent
    "nine"s, not three. Hazel's own gloss ("nine families, six farms — a farm can hold more than one
    family") shows both counts are of households, so one is simply wrong. **Undetermined.**
27. **The year Article VII was adopted.** 1892 (Effie Loomis) vs 1891 (Rundle) vs 1893 (Grigg, who
    hedges it himself). The printed by-laws carry no date and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in
    the 1938 removal. Falke declined to state it in 1959 and was right to. **Undetermined.**
28. **The Keddie–Vose kinship.** Asserted as hearsay by Vira and Oram, denied in detail by Duncan
    Keddie from inside the household. Probably false; not disprovable, since nothing in Orra's hand
    from before 1889 survives. **Undetermined, leaning strongly to no relation.**
29. **The first season's intake.** ~900,000 lb (Sill) vs ~1,100,000 lb (Kyle); Nym says no record
    exists and Kyle explains why. **Undetermined.**
30. **Where the 1911 substitute measure came from.** Larrow Green (Ivy) vs "up from town" (Teague)
    vs the state inspector's own (Roy Brant — chronologically impossible, since it was in use from
    8 May and Strawn's June entry records no loan). Keddie says only "one borrowed."
    **Undetermined.**
31. **The Alder Corners factory's opening year.** 1913 (Rosalie, the founder's daughter, twelve when
    the first vat was set) vs 1914 (Roy Brant, Rundle). Nothing turns on it; the haul began in 1915
    on all accounts. **Undetermined.**
32. **What Keddie himself believed.** See Section F. Three incompatible accounts, none documentary.
    **Undetermined.**
33. **Orra Keddie's birth year.** 1866 (the diary's editor) vs 1868 (Duncan) — resolvable to 1866
    only because Duncan also says she was 82 when she died in 1948, which is itself his arithmetic.
    Low confidence.

## III. Two narrators carrying the same wrong value — and what breaks each tie

| Wrong value | Carried by | What breaks the tie |
|---|---|---|
| Award $3,500 | Vira Toland; Cleve Oram | The award quoted verbatim from the minute book by two transcribers ($3,800). Both carriers are unrelated valley memories — genuinely independent, and both simply wrong. |
| "First cousin once removed" (Ivy/Hazel) | Vira Toland; Cleve Oram | Derivation from the sibling pair, stated independently by both fathers and by Hazel herself. Numerosity is irrelevant to a definition. |

## IV. Narrators contradicting themselves on the page

- **Vaught (Bulletin 471).** The worst case. His method section gives 16.27 c.c., a ratio of 0.9244,
  a true average of 3.905, a shortfall of 0.295, 118,000 lb and five dozen pipettes; his own
  reproduced conclusion gives one part in twenty, 76,000 lb and 1 May 1897. He even notices the
  wreckage without diagnosing it: "this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own
  average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware." He also misattaches the
  5.25 percent to the fat delivered. The likeliest reading: Ivy's arithmetic is in the conclusion,
  Vaught's recomputation went wrong in the body, and nobody reconciled them before printing.
- **Grigg (1919).** His covering letter says they sampled "the whole of 1908, every month"; the
  report reprinted immediately beneath his signature says "January, February and March." His entire
  defense rests on a year-round sample he did not take — and winter is the only window in which his
  own explanation could operate. He also inflates Bulletin 214 fourfold and then rests his case on
  the agreement between his finding and the College's.
- **Sill (1934).** Insists the Hollow was watering its cans, and in the same breath testifies that
  the fat arriving at Larrow Green from the Hollow was *always more* than the station's sheet said
  had been bought. Watered milk cannot deliver surplus fat. He held the disproof of his own theory
  in his own handwriting on every page of his receiving book for forty-one years. His 3.85 for the
  Hollow's "real" value also does not fit his own 3.78 for Larrow Green.
- **Rundle (1939/1959).** Chapter IV states the valley's theft verdict as settled fact; a note added
  while the pamphlet was in press retracts it on the ledger's arithmetic — and he leaves the wrong
  paragraph standing. The 1939 pamphlet and its 1959 reprint circulate the accusation and its
  refutation in the same covers.
- **Teague (1948).** Says the station-gain line was "my own device; I began it when I came in 1905"
  — and then quotes in full "the entry for 1898," with the settled phrase "as usual" already in it,
  seven years before he arrived. He also attaches the ledger's 5.25 percent to the wrong
  denominator.
- **Nym (1972).** Labels Item 3 "kept by E. Brant" and Item 4 "kept by A. Keddie," transposing the
  two keepers; and calls Rosalie Cudd a niece in a note whose own provenance line says the book
  passed "from its founder to his daughter."
- **Keddie (1925).** His letter says the 1896 glass was borrowed off "the Ashlin creamery"; the
  first leaf of his own book, which he quotes on the same page, says "Glass borrowed of Larrow
  Green." (There is no Ashlin creamery anywhere else in the record; Ashlin is the office.) His book
  outranks his memory at twenty-nine years.
- **Junia Ammon (1949).** "The house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900" — on the page
  facing her facsimile of an 1897 invoice to an Ordell County association. And she reads her own
  reproduced total, 77.93, as "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents." A third, subtler
  tension: she says the works books that would have answered the question burned in 1931, but the
  1923 letter already claimed the firm's books could tell nothing — which means either the works
  books were not consulted in 1923, or they were and she does not say so.
- **Kyle (1953).** Gives the award correctly as $3,800 and then dates it "in twenty-four," a year
  before the board sat; and has the inspector condemning the glass in 1922 with new glass "within
  the month," while the diary and the inspector's own book put the replacement in July 1923.
- **Hazel Brant (1981).** Declares her own unreliability at the top — "Quantities I have. Years I
  have not" — and then proves it exactly: the weigh book begun in 1900, the crate out from Tarnet
  in 1899, the condemnation in 1921, the hearing in 1926. Every one of her quantities (200,000 lb,
  3.42, six farms, four haulers, nineteen hundredths, nine letters) is right. A narrator who is
  reliable on one axis and useless on another, and who says so.
- **Falke (1959).** Contradicts himself deliberately and in public, which is the only honorable
  instance in the set. Paragraph 9 concedes the wet-hay theory as "a real if partial factor"; the
  appended note, written after he sent to Farland for Bulletin No. 214 and read it, withdraws the
  concession outright — "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen
  hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four" — and corrects his own
  "great-niece." This is the model the rest of the record fails to follow.

# Section F — Theory

## F1. What produced the shortfall

**In one sentence: a single lot of milk test pipettes, blown to a 17.6 c.c. mark but delivering
16.72, put every test at the Ostrey Hollow station at ninety-five percent of the truth from the day
the lot went into service until the day it was replaced, and the missing twentieth was not lost at
all — it went down the valley as real butterfat and was entered in the Association's own ledgers as
"station gain."**

*Why an error at all.* A butterfat test draws a measured charge of milk into a bottle whose neck is
ruled to read percent **for that charge and no other**. The operator weighs nothing; he reads the
length of a fat column and calls it a percentage, and he is entitled to do so only because the
charge is a fixed and known quantity. A short charge therefore gives a short reading in exact
proportion.

*Why that particular size.* 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.; 0.88 is one twentieth of 17.6. The size is a
property of the glass and of nothing in the valley. It came, on the firm's own reconstruction, from
a mold that had been reground: the lot ran short, uniformly, and every check the works could make
was a check of consistency — and the lot *was* consistent, consistently wrong, "which is the one
fault a check of that kind cannot find."

*Why it was invisible.* Three properties had to be held in mind at once and never were. **It was
proportional, not additive** — it removed a fraction, so herds still ranked correctly against one
another and the station's figures were perfectly orderly internally; only against the outside world
were they wrong. **It was precise** — a pipette that delivers 16.72 delivers 16.72 every time, in
January and July, for a careful operator and a careless one; scatter attracts attention and this had
none, it "has the appearance of excellent work." **It was uncheckable from inside** — scales can be
tried against test weights, acid against strength, bottle graduations against mercury, but the
pipette is not checked against anything, because in the practice of the trade *the pipette is the
standard*. An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it. It can
be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes did not reach the state's field
men until 1921.

*Why it began when it did.* The station opened 4 May 1896 on glass borrowed from Larrow Green —
known-good working glass — and that part-season averaged 3.80, which is the truth. The Ammon lot was
invoiced 14 April 1897 and went into use **1 May 1897**. From 1898, the first full year on it, the
average was 3.61 and stayed within three hundredths of it every year after.

*Why it ended when it did.* On **12 June 1923** the state inspector, newly equipped with a burette,
ran the station's pipettes against it, found they would not deliver their mark, and condemned the
whole crate. New glass was in use in July. **1924, the first full year on true glass: 3.80.**

*Why nothing had to change at either end.* Because nothing at the station or on the farms was ever
wrong. The same men, cows, ground, hay, water, acid, bottles, hand tester, scales and procedure
produced 3.80 in 1896, 3.61 from 1898, 3.80 for nine weeks in 1911, 3.61 again from 10 July 1911,
and 3.80 from 1924. The only thing that ever changed was which piece of glass stood on the bench.

*Where the money went.* The milk was correctly weighed and correctly separated, so the fat that
physically left the station was the true fat; only the credit was short. The surplus therefore
arrived at Larrow Green as real butterfat and was booked as station gain — 5.00 percent of the fat
received, 5.25 percent of the fat credited — and carried to the general account: 76,000 lb, about
$19,000, over 1898–1922. The Association was not robbed by anyone. It underpaid one end of its own
valley by one twentieth for twenty-five years and entered the difference as a gain, in ink, on an
open shelf, and read it out at the annual meeting.

## The three competing explanations, and what defeats each

**1. Watered milk — advanced by Selby Vose,** manager: in the 1919 circular, before the 1925 board
"without heat and without proof," and again in his 1926 address, where he said he would write every
word of it again. Endorsed by Hobart Sill.
*Defeated by:* Strawn's lactometer on every visit from 1907 to 1936, never once positive — and he
told Vose so in 1919, to his face, and Vose went on. Rosalie Cudd's professional point that watered
milk cannot be hidden from a cheesemaker: "the curd comes soft and it will not knit… Cheese is a
witness," and in sixteen summers her father never docked one of the four Hessel Bottom men or made
one complaint. The 1908 committee inquired into every barn's water supply and found "no dishonesty
of any kind, in any quarter." And decisively: watered milk cannot deliver *surplus* fat at Larrow
Green, which is what arrived, every year. The gap also predates the accusation by seventeen years
and was unchanged by it.

**2. Cream stolen by the tester — insinuated by Wilbur Teague,** the bookkeeper, at the 1925
hearing, obliquely enough that Frick judged no cross-examination could reach it; and held as the
valley's settled verdict (Vira Toland, Rundle's Chapter IV, Oram's father in a variant form).
*Defeated by:* the cream reaching Larrow Green heavy every year — "Cream stolen at the Hollow would
have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph
stand" (Rundle, retracting his own text). The proportionality of the loss: a constant twentieth of
each patron's fat, hitting the richest milk hardest, to two decimal places, for twenty-five years.
And the nine weeks of 1911, when the same man in the same room with no change of supervision
produced true figures the moment the measure changed.

**3. The wet hay of Hessel Bottom — the 1908 committee under Nyle Grigg,** revived by Grigg in 1919
and elaborated by Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926; briefly conceded by Falke in 1959.
*Defeated by:* Bulletin No. 214, which found bog hay as the whole roughage depressed the test by
**not more than 0.05 in winter and by nothing measurable on summer pasture** — four times too small
and, more importantly, the wrong shape, against a gap that stood at 0.19 in July as in February.
By the Alder Corners table: the same milk off the same wet ground ran 3.78–3.82 for sixteen summers
over the ridge, with no trend and no wet-year movement. By the 1911 window, in which the ground did
not change and the figures did. And by the committee's own sampling window — January to March, the
only months in which its own theory could operate. Falke, who alone actually sent for the bulletin,
read it and withdrew his concession.

## Subsidiary 1: Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?

**No — and the record of its condemnation was in the room the whole time.**

Effie Loomis, who took down every word: "The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all…
Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the
board, and the award's phrase about 'the books and appliances of the station' is a form of words the
board used and not a list of anything." Frick agrees the crate was never discussed.

But Frick's own exhibit list, reprinted from his file exactly as made up, includes **"The circuit
notebook of the state dairy inspector for the year 1923"** — the book containing the 12 June entry
that condemns the glass. Kyle, who compiled that list and checked it off twice and carried the books
in and out, says: "I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room
laid the second beside the fourth and looked." So the document that solves the case was formally in
evidence, and either was not read or was read without being understood. Frick's own line —
"I did not understand chemistry" — is the explanation.

And the physical crate was twenty feet away. Two of the three sessions were held in the station's
own weigh room at Frick's own request, so the board might see the plant rather than hear it
described — with the condemned glass in the loft directly overhead. Four independent sources put the
sittings there: Frick, Cleve Oram (who stood on the platform in the rain outside one of them),
Duncan Keddie, and Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary. Duncan's summary is the whole affair in one
line: "Twenty feet of ladder, thirty-five years, and two of the three sessions of that arbitration
sat in the room underneath it."

## Subsidiary 2: What did the tester himself believe?

**The sources give three answers and they cannot all be true; I cannot settle it, and I can say what
each rests on.**

*In writing, nothing.* His nine-sentence statement to the board says "I cannot tell you why the
figures are what they are." His unsent letter says of the 1911 question mark: "I could not answer it
then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know." That
is the only contemporaneous evidence, and it says he held no conclusion he was prepared to record.

*Privately, the patrons.* Frick, writing in 1946, adds what is not in the minutes: in the corridor at
Ashlin, Keddie told him privately "that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not
say so in the room… it has been said since that he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true,
and that he suspected nobody, which is not." Frick is a disinterested lawyer with no motive to invent
it and it does not flatter his own board.

*Privately, the glass.* Duncan Keddie, 1968: his father came home in the summer of 1911 saying it was
something in the glass, said it twice, at supper and again by the door, "which was not his way," and
never dared put it in the book. Duncan was seventeen and names the room. He then draws the
consequence: "He had, after all, nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass,
and against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the
county. So he kept on… and the figures were what convicted him."

*How they might fit.* The most economical reconstruction — and I flag it as inference — is that in
1911 he suspected the instrument, could not prove it against the whole settled practice of his trade,
and would not write what he could not prove; and that over the fourteen years afterward, with the
glass replaced in 1923 and the blame still on him, he fell back on the only remaining explanation
that did not accuse himself. Duncan's account is fifty-seven years after the fact from a man who was
seventeen at the time, and it is the only account that makes the question mark a suppressed
hypothesis rather than mere bafflement. Frick's is a recollection of a corridor remark twenty-one
years later. Neither is documentary. **The documentary record shows only the question mark, and the
question mark is the honest answer.**

# Section G — Summary

*(150 words)*

For a quarter century the Larrow Valley creamery's hill station at Ostrey Hollow tested its
patrons' milk one twentieth low, because a single lot of pipettes delivered 16.72 c.c. against a
17.6 mark. Nobody suspected the glass — a pipette is what a plant measures with, so nothing checks
it. The valley blamed the wet land, then the farmers' honesty, then the tester, and in 1925 an
arbitration board dismissed Ansel Keddie without finding anything against him. He died in 1934 under
it; his granddaughter proved the glass in 1958, twenty-four years too late.

It takes eight records because every witness held one disproof and none held two: the inspector's
lactometer, the buttermaker's heavy cream, the farmer's agreeing scales, the cheesemaker's vat, the
ledger's own "station gain," the tester's question mark, the supplier's reground mold, the
schoolmaster's family arithmetic. The answer was public the whole time, with a name on it.
