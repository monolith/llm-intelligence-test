## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) Proportion.** Nine surviving pipettes, stems blown 17.6 c.c., delivered against a calibrated burette (mean of five runs each): 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Sum = 150.48; ÷ 9 = **16.72 c.c.** Ratio 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly** (17.6 × 0.95 = 16.72). The test-bottle neck is ruled in percent for a 17.6 c.c. charge; a charge 95 % of that puts up a fat column 95 % as long, read against the same rulings. So every recorded test = 0.95 × true — the tests read **5 % low, one part in twenty** (equivalently, the truth is 1 ÷ 0.95 = 1.0526 × the recorded figure, 5.26 % above it). Vaught's §III/§V value of 16.27 c.c. (ratio 0.9244) is rejected: it is not the mean of the nine readings on the sheet, and his own conclusion says "one part in twenty."

**(b) True average and shortfall.** Recorded average 1898–1922: 3.61 (summary leaf). True = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Short by 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 of a point** (= 5 % of 3.80). Cross-checks: 1896 on borrowed glass 3.80; the nine weeks of 1911 3.80; 1924 on new glass 3.80; Alder Corners on the same herds ≈ 3.80.

**(c) Pounds not credited.** Milk 1898–1922: 40,000,000 lb (ledgers; 1,600,000 × 25 = 40,000,000).
Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
Delivered: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**.
Not credited: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 lb** (= 0.0019 × 40,000,000). (Strictly, the nine weeks of 1911 at 3.80 shave a few hundred pounds off this; the sources compute it flat, and so do I.)

**(d) Money and fraction received.** Fat averaged 25¢ over the period (ledger figure; Loomis, Kyle, Roy Brant, Ivy). 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**. Award: $3,800. 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **1/5 = 20 %**. (The 27¢ of Sill and Kepp is rejected as memory against the ledger; at 27¢ it would be $20,520.)

### C2 — Emmet Brant, 1910

Weigh-book leaf: 200,000 lb delivered, paid on a test of 3.42; fat 24¢ that year (Roy Brant).
**(a)** Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
**(b)** True test: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60. True fat: 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb** (= 5 % of 7,200; = 5.26 % of 6,840; Ivy's "a herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18" agrees: 0.18 × 2,000 = 360).
**(c)** 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40** for that year (at the period average of 25¢ it would be $90.00; I use the year's own price since the question asks for that year).

### C3 — Full delivery years 1898–1922

**(a)** Credited to patrons on the station test: **1,444,000 lb** (40,000,000 × 3.61 %).
**(b)** Received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: **1,520,000 lb** (40,000,000 × 3.80 %; the ledger's yearly form — 57,760 credited, 60,800 received, gain 3,040 on 1,600,000 lb — scales to exactly these totals over 25 years).
**(c)** Difference 76,000 lb. As a percentage **of the fat received/delivered** (1,520,000): 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00 %**. As a percentage **of the fat credited** (1,444,000): 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26 % ≈ 5¼ %**. The ledger's "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" is the second — a bookkeeper reckons the surplus against the figure already entered. Teague's and Vaught's wording, "five and a quarter percent of the fat actually delivered/in the cream," is the wrong denominator: 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.0 %, 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26 %. Five percent is the figure that is also the glass.

### C4 — The invoice

**(a)** 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **$77.93** (invoice of 14 April 1897, transcribed identically by Nym and Ammon; Ivy reads 77.93). The "$77.39" of the Chronicle and of Ammon's own prose fails the addition.
**(b)** 6 dozen = **72 pipettes** (6 doz. × $0.35 = $25.20, which only works for six dozen; five dozen would be $17.50). Each short by 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.** — the mean of the nine survivors, individually 0.86 to 0.90 c.c.; the other 63 were not measured, and the lot is presumed uniform (the nine agree within 0.04 c.c., and the station's average sat within 0.03 of 3.61 for 25 years).

### C5

**(a) Two different spans.** The short glass was in use from **1 May 1897** (Orra's diary; office file; Bulletin 471) to **12 June 1923** (Strawn's entry): **26 years and 42 days** — 26 years, 1 month, 11 days, about 26 years and six weeks — less the nine weeks of 1911 when a borrowed measure was substituted. The money calculation runs over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**. They differ because 1897 and 1923 were partial years on that glass (January–April 1897 on Sill's borrowed glass — the summary leaf says "1897, part year" — and 1923 on the short glass only to 12 June, new glass from July), while 1896 was wholly on borrowed glass and is in no ledger at all. Only 1898 through 1922 are whole calendar years tested entirely with the short pipettes at a flat 3.61, so the 40,000,000 lb and the 76,000 lb are counted over those.

**(b) First season (4 May–31 December 1896): cannot be determined from the sources.** The curator states the intake "is not in the ledgers and is nowhere else"; the station column begins with 1897, the first season having been carried on the creamery's books with sheets never filed with the station papers. Sill remembers "about nine hundred thousand pounds"; Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand… from men who were there… not in any book I ever ruled." Two unsupported recollections 200,000 lb apart, and no record: no figure can be given.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.** When: 8 May to 10 July 1911, 63 days, nine weeks (dated entries in the test book; Orra's diary confirms 8 May). Variable: the milk pipette — the station's own broke and a borrowed measure was used, then the station's own returned. Held constant: the patrons and herds, the pasture and season from week to week, the acid, bottles, hand tester, scale, the tester and his habits, and the degree of supervision (Strawn passed through in June and noted nothing). Result: the station average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 on the first morning, held there nine weeks, and fell back to 3.61 the day the old glass returned — every farm together. What it establishes that a calculation cannot: causation, not correspondence. The 1958 burette gives a ratio of 0.95 and shows it *could* account for 0.19; the 1911 interval shows that changing the one instrument, with nothing else touched, *did* move the whole gap and nothing else was needed — so the herds, the ground, the water in the cans and the man's honesty are excluded by direct trial, not inference. What it does not establish: which of the two measures was the true one (a borrowed pipette could over-deliver; that direction was only fixed in 1958 by the burette, and corroborated by Larrow Green's 3.78 and Alder Corners' 3.80); the mechanism (short delivery into a neck ruled for the full charge); that the same error held for all 26 years rather than for 1911 only (that rests on the summary leaf and the ledger's constant 5¼ %); the size of the loss in pounds or dollars; who was at fault for the glass; or where the borrowed measure came from (Ivy says Larrow Green; Roy's "state man in June" fails on the date; the book says only "borrowed").

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**
1. *The Association's own ledger* (Teague, Kyle, Rundle's retraction): every year more fat arrived at Larrow Green in the station's cream than the station had credited — 60,800 against 57,760 in 1898, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual." Theft makes cream arrive light; this cream arrived heavy. Holds without 1958.
2. *The buttermaker's receiving book* (Sill): an independent weighing of the same cans at the other end — "Always more. Never less," in his own hand from 1893. Sill believed in watered milk, not theft, so this is not advocacy. Holds without 1958.
3. *The 1911 interval* (Keddie's book; Falke §8): the same man, under no different supervision, produced tests 0.19 higher for nine weeks when only the pipette changed. A thief writing low tests to cover a loss has no reason to write higher ones because the glass changed. Holds without 1958.
4. *The 1924 rise under the same tester* (Strawn: "tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80"): the figures went right in the season *after the new glass* and *before* the petition (January 1925) and the dismissal (April 1925), while Keddie was still at the bench. The valley's "the figures went right when the man who kept it did not" (Rundle, Toland) has the sequence backward. Holds without 1958.
5. *The shape of the error* (Ivy, Vaught): the shortfall was a fixed fraction of each herd's test — richer milk short by more, 0.24 on a 4.80 herd, 0.18 on a 3.60 herd — flat in every month for 25 years. A man helping himself takes a quantity; a short pipette takes a proportion, with machine regularity.
6. *The other side of the ridge* (Rosalie Cudd): four of the same herds tested about 3.80 for sixteen summers on glass Keddie never touched, and their milk never gave a cheesemaker a soft curd. Independent county, independent man, no kin.
7. *The burette itself* (Ivy, 9 March 1958): 0.95 accounts for 3.61 against 3.80 to the hundredth, leaving no residual for anyone to have stolen.
These are independent because they come from different books kept by different hands for different purposes — the bookkeeper's ledger, the buttermaker's receiving book, the tester's own book, the inspector's circuit book, a cheesemaker's book in another county, and a laboratory sheet — and from narrators who did not share a theory: Teague and Sill accused, Strawn refused to have an opinion, Cudd never saw the station's figures. Reasons 1–4 and 6 would all stand if the crate had never been opened. *The bookkeeper:* by his own account Teague raised the account with Vose once, in 1916, was told to let it alone, and did (Kyle says Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years"). He never questioned what the line meant: he called its evenness "furniture," carried it to the general account "where it belonged," and in 1925 urged the theft explanation before a board without noticing that the entry in his own hand — cream coming in heavy — refuted it. He wrote in 1948 that he had not changed his mind.

**D3. Did Vose know before 1923 that the station's tests ran low? Yes — by his own words.** In 1926 he said: "For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green, and every year we were told it was the wet hay, or the tester, or the office, or the weather"; and he defended the 1919 circular because "a manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them… is being idle at their expense." The complaint was raised in 1902, investigated in 1908, and reopened in 1919; the whole point of the circular was that the tests ran low. He signed the annual sheet with the station-gain line every spring (Teague), and Teague put the account to him in 1916 and was told to let it alone. What must be distinguished: he knew the *tests were low* and believed the cause was water in the patrons' cans; there is no evidence he understood that the ledger's surplus meant the station was *under-reading* the fat — Kyle says he read totals and never the lines above them, and Strawn told him in 1919 that no water had ever been found and he disregarded it. Knowledge of the symptom: certain. Knowledge of the cause: he had the evidence in front of him yearly and was told once, and did not look.

## Section F — Theory

**The theory.** In April 1897 Ammon & Sons of Tarnet shipped the Ostrey Hollow station six dozen milk-test pipettes blown to a 17.6 c.c. mark that delivered 16.72 c.c. — a lot run consistently short, most likely from a reground mold, and uncatchable by the maker's own gauge because every piece was wrong by the same amount. The Babcock-type test reads fat as the length of a column in a bottle neck ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge; a charge of 95 % of that gives a column 95 % as long, so every reading at the station was 0.95 × the truth. That fixes the **size**: not a fixed number of points but one part in twenty — on milk truly averaging 3.80, a reading of 3.61, a gap of 0.19 that is 5 % of 3.80; 0.24 on a 4.80 herd, 0.18 on a 3.60 herd; and, at the other end of the valley, a surplus of exactly 5 % of the fat received arriving at Larrow Green every year, booked as "station gain, five and a quarter per cent [of the credited fat], as usual." It **began on 1 May 1897** because that is the day the Ammon lot replaced Sill's borrowed glass — the 1896 season on borrowed glass read 3.80, the first full year on the new glass read 3.61 — and it **ended in June–July 1923** because the state had issued burettes in 1921, the one instrument that can check a pipette from outside, and on 12 June 1923 Strawn ran the pipettes against his and condemned the lot; new glass delivering its mark went in from July, and 1924 read 3.80. **Nothing on the farms or at the station had to change** for either the start or the end, because the milk, the herds, the scales, the acid, the bottles and the man were never the cause; only the measuring glass was — and a pipette is the standard the plant measures with, so it cannot be caught by anything inside the plant. The error was precise (same every time), proportional (ranking of herds undisturbed), invisible from inside, and constant in every month and every year, which is why it looked like excellent work for twenty-six years. Two natural interruptions prove the mechanism: nine weeks in 1911 on a borrowed measure, 3.80; sixteen summers at Alder Corners on another man's glass, 3.80.

**The three competing explanations and what defeats each.**
1. *Added water in the patrons' cans* — Selby Vose (1919 circular; 1926 address), seconded by Sill and Oram's father. Defeated by: Strawn's lactometer, which found no added water at every visit from 1912 to 1934 — and he told Vose so in 1919; the station gain — watered milk yields no surplus fat at the creamery, yet the Hollow's cream arrived heavy every year; Cudd's vats — a cheesemaker knows watered milk "the next morning," and in sixteen summers never had a soft curd from those men; the Brant scales agreeing daily; the 1911 and 1924 rises, when no patron changed anything; and Vose's own chronology — the tests rose in 1924, before the petition and hearing he says frightened the watering into stopping.
2. *The tester stealing cream and writing low tests* — Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing; the valley's "settled opinion" (Rundle 1939), Toland, Oram's father. Defeated by: the ledger and receiving book — stolen cream arrives light, this arrived heavy (Rundle retracted on exactly this); the 1911 interval — same man, same supervision, tests up 0.19 when only the glass changed; 1924 — tests rose with Keddie still at the bench, before his dismissal; the proportional shape of the error; Strawn's repeated observation of him testing "slow and clean"; the burette's 0.95, which leaves no residue to have been stolen; Frick's judgment that his denial was accurate.
3. *The wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinning the cream* — Grigg's 1908 committee, Grigg's 1919 letter, Dr. Kepp (1926), Falke provisionally (1959). Defeated by: Bulletin No. 214 (1911) — at most 0.05 in winter and nothing measurable on summer pasture, four times too small and the wrong shape; the gap flat in July and February alike (Keddie, Roy, Hazel, Falke); Alder Corners — the same herds off the same wet ground tested 3.80 in the summer flush; the 1911 interval with the feed unchanged; and the true Hollow figure, 3.80, being a shade *above* Larrow Green's 3.78 — the bottom cows gave slightly better milk, not worse.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware? No.** Its exhibits were four books; the crate was never mentioned in any of the three sessions (Loomis, who took every word); it sat in the loft twenty feet above the two sessions held in the weigh room (Duncan, Oram); Frick's "every physical appliance… before us" refers to the plant then in use, the new glass; the board had Strawn's 1923 notebook recording the condemnation and nobody drew the inference or laid it beside the test book (Kyle). The award's "books and appliances of the station" was a form of words.

**What the tester himself believed.** From the summer of 1911 he suspected the glass — he came home saying "it was something in the glass," twice (Duncan) — but had only a suspicion against the practice of every station in the county, would not write what he could not prove, and set a question mark in the book instead. In 1925 his statement and unsent letter say he "cannot tell you why the figures are what they are," refuse to "put anything in its place that I do not know," and assert that he knew the milk came in whole — an implicit rejection of the water charge. Frick's recollection that he privately blamed the patrons contradicts that written position and cannot be verified; on the weight of evidence, he believed the fault was in the glass and could not prove it, and said so to no one but his family.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

In 1897 a Tarnet glass house sold the Ostrey Hollow skimming station six dozen milk pipettes that delivered 95 % of their mark. Because the co-op's Article VII paid patrons on the station's own test with no appeal, every farmer at the head of the valley was paid on readings one-twentieth low for twenty-six years — 76,000 lb of butterfat, $19,000 — while the surplus arrived at the creamery and sat in the ledger as "station gain, as usual." The valley blamed wet hay (1908), watered cans (1919) and a thieving tester (1925), and dismissed Ansel Keddie four days before a board found "no cause" and paid $3,800. Nobody suspected the one instrument a plant cannot check. Each proof lay in a different book — the tester's, the bookkeeper's, a farmer's, a cheesemaker's, an inspector's — kept by people who never compared them, until Keddie's granddaughter opened the crate in 1958. (145 words)
