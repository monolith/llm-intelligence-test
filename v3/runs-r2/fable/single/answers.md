## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — The Association and the Rule That Made the Test the Master (1889–1892; the office to 1945)

**Who.** The farmers of the Larrow Valley, Ordell County; Gideon Vose, first president; the office that later administered his rule — Effie Loomis (secretary 1901–1940), Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper 1905–1945), Hobart Sill (buttermaker at Larrow Green 1893–1934), Bertram Kyle (outer-desk clerk), and Selby Vose, Gideon's **son** [not nephew, as Toland and Rundle have it — Selby's own printed words: "My father, who was your first president"], manager 1913–1938 [Kyle's 1911 is wrong].

**What happened.** In 1889 the valley's farmers met at Larrow Green (the schoolhouse, by valley memory) and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of taking the Ashlin merchant's price for butter. Gideon Vose was elected president and held it twenty-two years, to 1911; he died in 1917. Not a lawyer, he wrote the by-laws himself at his kitchen table. In 1891 the main creamery was built at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek and the valley began to be paid in cash monthly. Before it, every man's arithmetic was his own.

The by-law that governs everything afterward is **Article VII**: every patron is paid according to the pounds of butterfat in his milk "ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." The last clause is the hinge. It allows no appeal to the creamery's test, the valley average, or any retest: the instrument at a man's own station is the whole bargain. Under payment by weight, nothing in the seven stories that follow would have cost anyone a dollar.

**When adopted — cannot be determined from the sources.** Loomis says 1892; Rundle 1891; Grigg "as I have always understood it" 1893. The printed pamphlet carries no year, only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the office move of 1938 — carried out with the rest and never arrived. Loomis herself concedes that nobody can say what the first four annual meetings resolved, which undercuts her own 1892. What is settled is only that payment by test was in force by the early 1890s.

**Quantities.** Founded 1889; creamery 1891; presidency 22 years; office (one room over a harness shop at Ashlin in Teague's day) moved 1938. On paper the Association was three things: creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin, skimming station seven miles up at Ostrey Hollow.

**Documents.** Printed by-laws pamphlet (accession Item 1); Loomis's "Notes toward a history" (1941) and her minute book from 1901; Rundle's jubilee pamphlet (1939); Grigg's 1919 covering letter; Falke's 1959 county report (declines to date the article); Vose's 1926 address.

### Story 2 — The Station at Ostrey Hollow and the Short Glass (1895–1898)

**Who.** Ansel Keddie (born 1861; married Orra Brant 1889), tester and separator man; Hobart Sill, who lent the first outfit; Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet, and its later historian Junia Ammon.

**What happened.** In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley: a board building with a covered platform, an intake room holding scale, vat and separator, and a loft over the intake room reached by a ladder. Keddie went up in 1895 to ready it. It opened **4 May 1896**: milk weighed, sampled, tested, separated; cream wagoned down to Larrow Green daily, skim handed back for hogs and calves. Its own outfit had not come, so Sill made one up from his own glass at Larrow Green. The test book's first leaf: "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie." [Keddie's later "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery" is contradicted by his own leaf and by Sill.] On that borrowed glass the 1896 average was **3.80**.

The Ammon invoice of **14 April 1897**: 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68; 1 acid measure — .90; 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00; freight — 2.15; **total $77.93** [not $77.39; the lines sum to 77.93]. Seventy-two pipettes [not five dozen]. The outfit went into use **1 May 1897** [not 1898] — Orra's diary that day: "the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green." Sill's glass went back, one piece chipped. From then the tests read **3.61** — "1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since." The milk had not changed; the lot was short. Junia Ammon's reconstruction: a reground mold, a lot consistently wrong, an accident no gauge check could catch; the works books burned in 1931.

**Keddie's method** (his unsent letter): day began 5:30; every can weighed on a state-tested scale; a composite sample per patron over ten days, warmed blood-warm and poured vessel to vessel; a 17.6 c.c. charge blown into the numbered bottle; acid down the side; four bottles in the hand tester at the taught speed; fat read with dividers. Book in five columns — date, patron, pounds, test, fat; errors struck and initialed, never overwritten. Twenty-nine seasons, 1896–1924; thirty-one tests a week.

**Quantities.** First-season intake (4 May–end 1896): **cannot be determined** — not in the ledgers or anywhere (Nym); Sill's "about 900,000 lb" and Kyle's "a good 1,100,000" are both hearsay. The station column begins 1897. Nine patron families; six Hessel Bottom farms plus places on the hill.

**Documents.** Test book (first leaf; 1922 summary leaf); Ammon invoice (Item 2; facsimile in the firm's history); Orra's diary, 1 May 1897; Sill's 1934 remarks; Keddie's 1925 letter; Nym's accession notes.

*(~400 words)*

### Story 3 — Twenty-Five Years of Complaint and the Hay Theory (1902–1926)

**Who.** The Hessel Bottom patrons — chiefly Emmet Brant (1854–1929), largest patron, sixty-odd acres of the 160-acre meadow, thirty-eight cows; Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, committee chairman 1908; Dr. Roland Kepp, veterinary surgeon of Ashlin; the State Agricultural College at Farland.

**What happened.** From 1902 the Hollow men said their milk tested under the same class of milk at Larrow Green (Orra's diary, March 1902: "A. came home and would not eat"). The station's average stood at **3.61** against Larrow Green's **3.78** — 0.17 below the creamery's actual milk, **0.19** below what the Hollow milk truly was — and the gap was flat in every month, July as February, wet years and dry: not the shape a feed effect takes. Emmet Brant's weigh book, begun **1 January 1897** [not 1898 or 1900], showed his own barn scale and the station's agreeing day after day; whatever was wrong, it was not the weighing. His 1910 leaf: "two hundred thousand pounds even… Paid on a test of 3.42." Fat was 24¢ that year.

In January 1908 the board named a committee of three under Grigg, who hauled to Larrow Green and had no stake. It sampled the six Bottom farms [Loomis's seven is wrong] through **January, February and March 1908 only** — the report's own words, and Orra has them arriving by sleigh in January and gone by March [Grigg's 1919 letter claiming "every month of that year… on the pasture" is contradicted by his own report]. It tried both sets of scales, found the milk whole, "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter," and laid the cause to the wet sedge hay fed November to April: "the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." Emmet read it twice and said the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not. The report closed the question for eleven years.

**Bulletin No. 214** (1911), the College's controlled feeding trial: bog hay as the whole roughage "depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Grigg's 1919 covering letter and Kepp's 1926 Farm Club paper both cite it as "two tenths in the winter feeding" — a misquotation. Kepp put the gap at 0.21 and fat at 27¢, called the 1911 interval "six weeks of summer," and told the floor it was not good sense to look for a second cause in a building. The county agent in 1959 first allowed hay as a partial factor, then withdrew it on reading the bulletin: five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July.

**Quantities.** 3.61 vs 3.78 vs 3.80 true; gap 0.19 year-round; Bulletin 214: ≤0.05 winter, 0 summer; 160 acres, six farms; 200,000 lb at 3.42 in 1910; 24¢.

**Documents.** 1908 report (Item 11; reprinted 1919); Bulletin 214 (Item 12); Brant weigh book (Item 4); Orra's diary 1902 and 1908; Kepp's paper with discussion; Grigg's letter; Falke's report and added note.

*(~400 words)*

### Story 4 — Nine Weeks in 1911

**Who.** Ansel Keddie at the bench; every patron of the station; Merle Strawn, state inspector, who passed through in June.

**What happened.** On **8 May 1911** the station's own pipette broke — Orra that evening: "A. home cross about a broken measure. Rain all day." Keddie made shift with a borrowed one. Ivy Keddie says it came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon; Roy Brant's memory that the state man left his own in June cannot stand, because the break was 8 May and Strawn's June entry reads "Watched a test through. Nothing here to note." The test book: "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." From that morning the station's average went to **3.80** and held. "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." Eight May to 10 July is 63 days — **nine weeks** exactly [Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" are wrong; Kepp's "June and July" is wrong too]. Every farm on the Bottom rose together and fell together the same day. Teague entered a small replacement charge and nothing else.

Everything else was held constant: the same patrons and herds, the same pasture and season from one week to the next, the same acid, bottles, hand tester and scale, the same man at the crank under no different supervision. One variable changed — the measure — and the whole gap of nineteen hundredths went with it, then came back with the old glass. Keddie came home that summer saying "it was something in the glass," twice, at supper and by the door — but he would not write what he could not prove; the question mark stood fourteen years, and he went on writing what the glass gave him. Vaught in 1958: an experiment that ran itself under conditions no designed trial could better, unread for forty-seven years, disposing of every explanation that looks to the herds, the ground or the men, because none of those was altered and the figures were.

What the interval did not settle in 1911: which measure was the true one — a borrowed pipette could as easily have been over-delivering — nor the mechanism, nor the size over other years. Against Keddie's suspicion stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county.

**Quantities.** 8 May–10 July 1911; 63 days = 9 weeks; 3.61 → 3.80 → 3.61; difference 0.19; 3.80 × 0.95 = 3.61.

**Documents.** Test book entries of 8 May and 10 July 1911 and the 1922 summary leaf ("save the nine weeks of 1911… when it stood at 3.80"); Orra's diary, 8 May 1911; Strawn's 1911 entry; Teague's chapter; Ivy's 1959 talk; Bulletin 471 §IV; Duncan Keddie's notes; Kepp's "objection."

*(~390 words)*

### Story 5 — "Station Gain, Five and a Quarter Per Cent, As Usual" (1897–1925)

**Who.** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Hobart Sill, buttermaker, keeper of the receiving and churn books; Bertram Kyle, the clerk who copied the line every year; Selby Vose, who signed the annual sheet.

**What happened.** Because the station separated on the spot and the cream came down to Larrow Green, the Association held two figures for the same fat: what the station's tests credited to the patrons, and what was weighed and tested into the vats at Larrow Green. The second was always larger. The 1898 entry, complete: "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." The line stands in that form for every year of the station column, which begins 1897 [Teague's claim to have invented it in 1905 is contradicted by the 1898 entry he himself quotes]. Sill weighed the Hollow cream over again himself and wrote it in the receiving book from 1893: it "ran heavy. Every year… Always more. Never less" — and he made nothing of it, and nobody asked. Kyle copied the line "more times than I can count" and never wondered: a figure with a name on it has been explained.

The percentages: 3,040 is 5¼ % of the 57,760 **credited** and 5 % of the 60,800 **received** — Teague and Vaught both say "of the fat actually delivered," the wrong denominator. Over the twenty-five full delivery years **1898–1922** the ledgers show **40,000,000 lb** of milk — 1,600,000 a year [Teague's and Rundle's 44 million fits neither the yearly figure nor the fat totals] — **1,444,000 lb** credited at 3.61, **1,520,000 lb** received at 3.80, and station gain **76,000 lb**. Fat averaged **25¢** [not 27¢], so **$19,000** stood credited to the Association as gain, in ink, dated, on an open shelf, read out at every annual meeting to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees.

Vose signed the sheet each spring — "a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him" — though Kyle says he put his thumb on the bottom figure and never read the lines above it. Teague put the account to Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone; he let it alone. Rundle, shown the ledger while his 1939 pamphlet was in type, retracted his cream-theft chapter: stolen cream would reach the Green light, and it reached the Green heavy every year. The 1925 award of $3,800 was charged to this same station account, "and it balanced."

**Documents.** Annual ledgers, station account (Item 6); Teague's memoir chapter (1948); Sill's remarks (1934) and the Larrow Green receiving and churn books (Item 16); Kyle's memoir (1953); Loomis §7; Rundle's "Note added while in press"; Bulletin 471 §V.

### Story 6 — The Water Circular of 1919 and the Brothers-in-law (1919–1929)

**Who.** Selby Vose, who signed it; Bertram Kyle, who typed it; Ansel Keddie, who carried it; Emmet Brant and his sister Orra Keddie; nine patron families — among them Vira Toland's father and the Brant household of Roy and his daughter Hazel.

**What happened.** The question closed in 1908 was reopened in 1919 by "men who were boys" then; Grigg reprinted his report with a letter saying the College had proved him right. In June 1919 a circular went out over Vose's name warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons that added water would be looked for and dealt with; it named nobody and said the Association pays for butterfat and cannot pay for creek water. Vose's reason, given in 1926: a manager who sees his figures go one way for twenty years and says nothing is idle at the men's expense. It went to **nine families** — Keddie, Toland, Roy and Hazel Brant all say nine; Orra's diary that night says eleven, the one contemporaneous count, and it stands unexplained. Keddie would not post the copies or leave them on the platform; he took them round himself and stood at each door while it was read, home at eleven at night with his hat on. Toland's father read his twice at the table with his hat on and kept it in the desk drawer till he died; Hazel's mother read hers twice and put it in the stove. Nobody answered it, "which in that valley is a shout."

Two days later Emmet Brant stood in the Keddies' yard — he did not come in — and said he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and Ansel knew it better than any man living; then asked why Ansel had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet never entered the house again. Roy's version: his father held that the man who kept the test book should have stood up in front of that circular and did not. Brother-in-law and brother-in-law within sight of each other's chimney smoke, ten years without a word; Orra walked down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane; Emmet died in the spring of 1929 unreconciled, and Roy met her kindly at the gate.

The inspector's answer, in his book the same year: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so." Strawn found no added water at any visit, 1912 through 1934. Vose defended the circular in 1926 and would "write every word of it again tomorrow."

**Documents.** The circular itself (kept in the patrons' drawers; not in the collection); Orra's diary, June 1919 and spring 1929; Strawn's 1919 entry; Vose's 1926 address; Grigg's 1919 letter; Kyle's memoir; Toland, Roy and Hazel Brant recollections; Keddie's letter ("nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since").

*(~400 words)*

### Story 7 — Condemned Glass, Dismissed Man (1921–1934)

**Who.** Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit 1907–1936, kin to nobody in the valley; Ammon & Sons; Alonzo Frick, attorney, chairman of a board of three; Ansel Keddie; Vose and Teague for the office; the petitioning patrons, Emmet Brant's name among them.

**What happened.** The state issued burettes to its field men in 1921. Strawn skipped the check at Ostrey Hollow in 1922, the day being short. On **12 June 1923**: "Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." [Vose's and Rundle's "12 July" is wrong: the replacement order is dated 28 June.] Ammon & Sons replied 2 July — six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles entered, "our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." New glass was in use from July 1923; in October it delivered its mark. The condemned crate went into the loft over the intake room. In 1924, the same man at the bench, the book gave **3.80** — "tests improved" [not 3.90 or 3.85]. Nobody connected the two things. The burette program was discontinued in 1926.

In January 1925 the patrons petitioned. The board sat **17 March–4 May 1925**: the first session at the Association's office at Ashlin, the second and third in the station's weigh room at Frick's insistence — the crate twenty feet above their heads. Four exhibits and nothing else: the test book (4 May 1896–31 December 1924), the station receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book (begun 1 January 1897), and Strawn's 1923 notebook. The crate was never mentioned in any session; nobody laid the second book beside the fourth. Three explanations were urged: water (Vose), cream theft (Teague, charging nobody), hay (the 1908 report read in). Keddie's nine-sentence statement of 17 March: tested as taught and as the state man saw, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons; never took a pound of cream; "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are." Frick believed him. On **30 April 1925** the Association dismissed him — "twenty-nine seasons and a letter"; he hung his apron behind the shed door and ate his supper. On **4 May** the award: "finds no cause established… awards… the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account" [not $3,500]. Vose told the 1926 meeting it was a generous settlement of an unproved claim and that water had stopped "when the looking began" — though the 1924 rise came before the petition. Keddie kept four or five cows, turned carpenter, and died in February 1934 uncleared; Orra died in 1948.

**Documents.** Strawn's 1923 notebook (Item 9, photostat) and covering note; Ammon's letter of 2 July 1923 (Item 10); the board's minute book (Item 7); Keddie's statement (Item 8) and letter; Frick's exhibit list; Kyle's memoir; Orra's diary 1923–1934; Vose's 1926 address; Loomis §6 and §8.

### Story 8 — Over the Ridge and Up the Ladder (1913–1970)

**Who.** Jerome Cudd, who built the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County in 1913 (opened 1914; died March 1941), and his **daughter** Rosalie [not niece — her own letter: "My father, Jerome Cudd"]; four Hessel Bottom men; Ivy Keddie (born 1931), Ansel's **granddaughter** [not great-niece], graduate student at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught; Roy Brant; Halbert Nym, curator.

**What happened.** From 1915 through 1930 four Bottom farms hauled over Redlow Ridge — four miles of switchback, shut November to April — to Cudd's factory from about 1 June to mid-September, **sixteen seasons**, for cheese prices and a shorter pull. Cudd tested on his own glass and compared with nobody. Summer 1919: "3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." Sixteen summer averages between 3.78 and 3.82, about **3.80**; never a soft curd, never a man docked — cheese is a witness against watered milk. The same cows tested 3.61 at home.

In February 1958 Ivy Keddie climbed the ladder at the station (closed since 1946) and found under a tarpaulin the crate stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, **nine** pipettes unbroken [not eleven; Nym's "found in the Association office" is wrong]. On **9 March 1958** at Farland: stems blown 17.6 c.c.; mean of five runs each 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71; **mean 16.72 c.c., short 0.88, 95 hundredths of the mark** [Vaught's 16.27 is a transposition]. She brought five records into one room for the first time: the test book (Association office since 1925), Emmet Brant's weigh book (**lent**, not left, by Roy), Cudd's figures copied by Rosalie (the book never left Vessey County), Strawn's notebooks (Farland library since 1947, unasked-for for eleven years), and the ledgers. **Bulletin No. 471**, October 1958, Vaught and Keddie: from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 the tests were low by one part in twenty because the pipettes were short by one part in twenty; the difference, **76,000 lb** of butterfat, **$19,000**, stands in the ledgers as station gain; the award of $3,800 was a fifth of it. Thirty-five years from condemnation to cause; thirty-three from dismissal; Keddie dead twenty-four.

*The Ashlin Chronicle* (3 October 1958) printed "great-niece," eleven pipettes, 16.27, $19,600 and $77.39, and accused Ammon of knowing; the county agent's 1959 report repeated "great-niece," then corrected it. Ivy's 1959 talk to the Historical Society put the crate on the table: "It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette." Test book and crate to the Rooms 1959; weigh book to Hazel 1962, to the Rooms 1963; Cudd's book to the Vessey County Historical Society 1970 — five books in three buildings.

**Documents.** Rosalie Cudd's letter and table (1958; Item 13 file); measurement sheet (Item 14); Bulletin 471 (Item 15); the Chronicle; Falke's report; Ivy's talk; Nym's accession notes; Duncan Keddie's notes; Roy Brant's 1958 postscript.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie and Ansel Keddie — granddaughter.** Chain: Ansel Keddie (1861–1934) married Orra Brant in 1889; their son Duncan was born 1894 (Orra's diary has him five in September 1899 and seventeen in October 1911, which fits); Duncan's daughter is Ivy, born 1931 (twenty-seven in 1958, per the Chronicle, which fits). Ivy opens her 1959 talk "Ansel Keddie… was my grandfather. My father, Duncan Keddie… was his son"; Duncan's family notes say the same, and Hazel Brant calls Ivy "Duncan's girl." The sources that point elsewhere are the *Chronicle* ("great-niece," assembled in a day and a half from a press release and two phone calls) and Falke's report, whose paragraph 2 repeats "great-niece" and whose closing note withdraws it: "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." Two carriers of one press error against the father, the daughter, and the diary: granddaughter.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant — second cousins.** Emmet Brant (1854–1929) and Orra Keddie (1866–1948) were brother and sister. Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan are therefore first cousins — "the children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole definition." Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy, the children of first cousins, are second cousins. Roy, Hazel, Duncan and Rundle's 1959 footnote all say so; Toland's and Oram's "first cousin once removed" is valley talk and is a different relation belonging to a different generation. What the sources leave open: whether Emmet and Orra were full or half siblings. Roy says full, "same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it"; Hazel says half, "by his father's second wife"; Duncan says only "brother and sister." Orra's diary never says, and her pages before 1889 were not preserved, so nothing in her hand can settle it. The twelve-year age gap fits either. If Hazel is right, the two women are half-second cousins; the label "second cousins" survives either way. (A side note: Duncan gives his mother's birth as 1868, but his own "eighty-two when she died in 1948" and her diary's "I am sixty-four" in 1930 both give 1866.)

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose.** Selby's own printed words to the 1926 meeting: "My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron… is paid on the test of the station where he delivers." Loomis ("son of the late president"), Kyle ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin") and Duncan agree; Toland and Rundle say nephew, from memory, against the man's own statement. Why it matters: the father wrote Article VII, whose last clause made the station's own instrument the whole bargain with no appeal; the son administered that rule for twenty-five years (1913–1938), signed every annual sheet carrying the station-gain line, was told about the line by Teague in 1916 and said to let it alone, wrote the 1919 water circular, and in 1926 stood up and defended both the circular and the by-law. The man least likely to suspect the rule's instrument was the one enforcing his father's rule — and he used his father's Jersey herd, 4.56 in 1910, as proof that the valley's milk was good and the Hollow's figures therefore the patrons' fault. The family, Toland says, "always thought the valley belonged to them a little."

**B4. Vose and Keddie families — no relationship is established.** Two narrators assert one: Toland ("Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that") and Oram ("cousins of some sort… My mother had it from her mother… I could not draw you the line on paper"). Both are hearsay from the same valley talk, so they count as one source, not two. Against them: Duncan Keddie, Orra's son, says flatly there was no connection, that people confused Gideon's family with the unrelated Ashlin Voses, and that his mother — who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" — never once put a Vose in it. Orra's diary mentions no Vose kin. A negative cannot be proved from these sources, and her pre-1889 pages are lost, but the direct family testimony plus the diary's silence outweigh an untraceable rumor: on the evidence, none. The rumor matters only because Toland uses it to make the 1919 circular "somebody's cousin saying it."

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) Proportion.** Nine surviving pipettes, stems blown 17.6 c.c., delivered against a calibrated burette (mean of five runs each): 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Sum = 150.48; ÷ 9 = **16.72 c.c.** Ratio 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly** (17.6 × 0.95 = 16.72). The test-bottle neck is ruled in percent for a 17.6 c.c. charge; a charge 95 % of that puts up a fat column 95 % as long, read against the same rulings. So every recorded test = 0.95 × true — the tests read **5 % low, one part in twenty** (equivalently, the truth is 1 ÷ 0.95 = 1.0526 × the recorded figure, 5.26 % above it). Vaught's §III/§V value of 16.27 c.c. (ratio 0.9244) is rejected: it is not the mean of the nine readings on the sheet, and his own conclusion says "one part in twenty."

**(b) True average and shortfall.** Recorded average 1898–1922: 3.61 (summary leaf). True = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Short by 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 of a point** (= 5 % of 3.80). Cross-checks: 1896 on borrowed glass 3.80; the nine weeks of 1911 3.80; 1924 on new glass 3.80; Alder Corners on the same herds ≈ 3.80.

**(c) Pounds not credited.** Milk 1898–1922: 40,000,000 lb (ledgers; 1,600,000 × 25 = 40,000,000).
Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
Delivered: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**.
Not credited: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 lb** (= 0.0019 × 40,000,000). (Strictly, the nine weeks of 1911 at 3.80 shave a few hundred pounds off this; the sources compute it flat, and so do I.)

**(d) Money and fraction received.** Fat averaged 25¢ over the period (ledger figure; Loomis, Kyle, Roy Brant, Ivy). 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**. Award: $3,800. 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **1/5 = 20 %**. (The 27¢ of Sill and Kepp is rejected as memory against the ledger; at 27¢ it would be $20,520.)

### C2 — Emmet Brant, 1910

Weigh-book leaf: 200,000 lb delivered, paid on a test of 3.42; fat 24¢ that year (Roy Brant).
**(a)** Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
**(b)** True test: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60. True fat: 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb** (= 5 % of 7,200; = 5.26 % of 6,840; Ivy's "a herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18" agrees: 0.18 × 2,000 = 360).
**(c)** 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40** for that year (at the period average of 25¢ it would be $90.00; I use the year's own price since the question asks for that year).

### C3 — Full delivery years 1898–1922

**(a)** Credited to patrons on the station test: **1,444,000 lb** (40,000,000 × 3.61 %).
**(b)** Received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: **1,520,000 lb** (40,000,000 × 3.80 %; the ledger's yearly form — 57,760 credited, 60,800 received, gain 3,040 on 1,600,000 lb — scales to exactly these totals over 25 years).
**(c)** Difference 76,000 lb. As a percentage **of the fat received/delivered** (1,520,000): 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00 %**. As a percentage **of the fat credited** (1,444,000): 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26 % ≈ 5¼ %**. The ledger's "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" is the second — a bookkeeper reckons the surplus against the figure already entered. Teague's and Vaught's wording, "five and a quarter percent of the fat actually delivered/in the cream," is the wrong denominator: 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.0 %, 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26 %. Five percent is the figure that is also the glass.

### C4 — The invoice

**(a)** 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **$77.93** (invoice of 14 April 1897, transcribed identically by Nym and Ammon; Ivy reads 77.93). The "$77.39" of the Chronicle and of Ammon's own prose fails the addition.
**(b)** 6 dozen = **72 pipettes** (6 doz. × $0.35 = $25.20, which only works for six dozen; five dozen would be $17.50). Each short by 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.** — the mean of the nine survivors, individually 0.86 to 0.90 c.c.; the other 63 were not measured, and the lot is presumed uniform (the nine agree within 0.04 c.c., and the station's average sat within 0.03 of 3.61 for 25 years).

### C5

**(a) Two different spans.** The short glass was in use from **1 May 1897** (Orra's diary; office file; Bulletin 471) to **12 June 1923** (Strawn's entry): **26 years and 42 days** — 26 years, 1 month, 11 days, about 26 years and six weeks — less the nine weeks of 1911 when a borrowed measure was substituted. The money calculation runs over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**. They differ because 1897 and 1923 were partial years on that glass (January–April 1897 on Sill's borrowed glass — the summary leaf says "1897, part year" — and 1923 on the short glass only to 12 June, new glass from July), while 1896 was wholly on borrowed glass and is in no ledger at all. Only 1898 through 1922 are whole calendar years tested entirely with the short pipettes at a flat 3.61, so the 40,000,000 lb and the 76,000 lb are counted over those.

**(b) First season (4 May–31 December 1896): cannot be determined from the sources.** The curator states the intake "is not in the ledgers and is nowhere else"; the station column begins with 1897, the first season having been carried on the creamery's books with sheets never filed with the station papers. Sill remembers "about nine hundred thousand pounds"; Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand… from men who were there… not in any book I ever ruled." Two unsupported recollections 200,000 lb apart, and no record: no figure can be given.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.** When: 8 May to 10 July 1911, 63 days, nine weeks (dated entries in the test book; Orra's diary confirms 8 May). Variable: the milk pipette — the station's own broke and a borrowed measure was used, then the station's own returned. Held constant: the patrons and herds, the pasture and season from week to week, the acid, bottles, hand tester, scale, the tester and his habits, and the degree of supervision (Strawn passed through in June and noted nothing). Result: the station average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 on the first morning, held there nine weeks, and fell back to 3.61 the day the old glass returned — every farm together. What it establishes that a calculation cannot: causation, not correspondence. The 1958 burette gives a ratio of 0.95 and shows it *could* account for 0.19; the 1911 interval shows that changing the one instrument, with nothing else touched, *did* move the whole gap and nothing else was needed — so the herds, the ground, the water in the cans and the man's honesty are excluded by direct trial, not inference. What it does not establish: which of the two measures was the true one (a borrowed pipette could over-deliver; that direction was only fixed in 1958 by the burette, and corroborated by Larrow Green's 3.78 and Alder Corners' 3.80); the mechanism (short delivery into a neck ruled for the full charge); that the same error held for all 26 years rather than for 1911 only (that rests on the summary leaf and the ledger's constant 5¼ %); the size of the loss in pounds or dollars; who was at fault for the glass; or where the borrowed measure came from (Ivy says Larrow Green; Roy's "state man in June" fails on the date; the book says only "borrowed").

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**
1. *The Association's own ledger* (Teague, Kyle, Rundle's retraction): every year more fat arrived at Larrow Green in the station's cream than the station had credited — 60,800 against 57,760 in 1898, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual." Theft makes cream arrive light; this cream arrived heavy. Holds without 1958.
2. *The buttermaker's receiving book* (Sill): an independent weighing of the same cans at the other end — "Always more. Never less," in his own hand from 1893. Sill believed in watered milk, not theft, so this is not advocacy. Holds without 1958.
3. *The 1911 interval* (Keddie's book; Falke §8): the same man, under no different supervision, produced tests 0.19 higher for nine weeks when only the pipette changed. A thief writing low tests to cover a loss has no reason to write higher ones because the glass changed. Holds without 1958.
4. *The 1924 rise under the same tester* (Strawn: "tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80"): the figures went right in the season *after the new glass* and *before* the petition (January 1925) and the dismissal (April 1925), while Keddie was still at the bench. The valley's "the figures went right when the man who kept it did not" (Rundle, Toland) has the sequence backward. Holds without 1958.
5. *The shape of the error* (Ivy, Vaught): the shortfall was a fixed fraction of each herd's test — richer milk short by more, 0.24 on a 4.80 herd, 0.18 on a 3.60 herd — flat in every month for 25 years. A man helping himself takes a quantity; a short pipette takes a proportion, with machine regularity.
6. *The other side of the ridge* (Rosalie Cudd): four of the same herds tested about 3.80 for sixteen summers on glass Keddie never touched, and their milk never gave a cheesemaker a soft curd. Independent county, independent man, no kin.
7. *The burette itself* (Ivy, 9 March 1958): 0.95 accounts for 3.61 against 3.80 to the hundredth, leaving no residual for anyone to have stolen.
These are independent because they come from different books kept by different hands for different purposes — the bookkeeper's ledger, the buttermaker's receiving book, the tester's own book, the inspector's circuit book, a cheesemaker's book in another county, and a laboratory sheet — and from narrators who did not share a theory: Teague and Sill accused, Strawn refused to have an opinion, Cudd never saw the station's figures. Reasons 1–4 and 6 would all stand if the crate had never been opened. *The bookkeeper:* by his own account Teague raised the account with Vose once, in 1916, was told to let it alone, and did (Kyle says Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years"). He never questioned what the line meant: he called its evenness "furniture," carried it to the general account "where it belonged," and in 1925 urged the theft explanation before a board without noticing that the entry in his own hand — cream coming in heavy — refuted it. He wrote in 1948 that he had not changed his mind.

**D3. Did Vose know before 1923 that the station's tests ran low? Yes — by his own words.** In 1926 he said: "For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green, and every year we were told it was the wet hay, or the tester, or the office, or the weather"; and he defended the 1919 circular because "a manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them… is being idle at their expense." The complaint was raised in 1902, investigated in 1908, and reopened in 1919; the whole point of the circular was that the tests ran low. He signed the annual sheet with the station-gain line every spring (Teague), and Teague put the account to him in 1916 and was told to let it alone. What must be distinguished: he knew the *tests were low* and believed the cause was water in the patrons' cans; there is no evidence he understood that the ledger's surplus meant the station was *under-reading* the fat — Kyle says he read totals and never the lines above them, and Strawn told him in 1919 that no water had ever been found and he disregarded it. Knowledge of the symptom: certain. Knowledge of the cause: he had the evidence in front of him yearly and was told once, and did not look.

## Section F — Theory

**The theory.** In April 1897 Ammon & Sons of Tarnet shipped the Ostrey Hollow station six dozen milk-test pipettes blown to a 17.6 c.c. mark that delivered 16.72 c.c. — a lot run consistently short, most likely from a reground mold, and uncatchable by the maker's own gauge because every piece was wrong by the same amount. The Babcock-type test reads fat as the length of a column in a bottle neck ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge; a charge of 95 % of that gives a column 95 % as long, so every reading at the station was 0.95 × the truth. That fixes the **size**: not a fixed number of points but one part in twenty — on milk truly averaging 3.80, a reading of 3.61, a gap of 0.19 that is 5 % of 3.80; 0.24 on a 4.80 herd, 0.18 on a 3.60 herd; and, at the other end of the valley, a surplus of exactly 5 % of the fat received arriving at Larrow Green every year, booked as "station gain, five and a quarter per cent [of the credited fat], as usual." It **began on 1 May 1897** because that is the day the Ammon lot replaced Sill's borrowed glass — the 1896 season on borrowed glass read 3.80, the first full year on the new glass read 3.61 — and it **ended in June–July 1923** because the state had issued burettes in 1921, the one instrument that can check a pipette from outside, and on 12 June 1923 Strawn ran the pipettes against his and condemned the lot; new glass delivering its mark went in from July, and 1924 read 3.80. **Nothing on the farms or at the station had to change** for either the start or the end, because the milk, the herds, the scales, the acid, the bottles and the man were never the cause; only the measuring glass was — and a pipette is the standard the plant measures with, so it cannot be caught by anything inside the plant. The error was precise (same every time), proportional (ranking of herds undisturbed), invisible from inside, and constant in every month and every year, which is why it looked like excellent work for twenty-six years. Two natural interruptions prove the mechanism: nine weeks in 1911 on a borrowed measure, 3.80; sixteen summers at Alder Corners on another man's glass, 3.80.

**The three competing explanations and what defeats each.**
1. *Added water in the patrons' cans* — Selby Vose (1919 circular; 1926 address), seconded by Sill and Oram's father. Defeated by: Strawn's lactometer, which found no added water at every visit from 1912 to 1934 — and he told Vose so in 1919; the station gain — watered milk yields no surplus fat at the creamery, yet the Hollow's cream arrived heavy every year; Cudd's vats — a cheesemaker knows watered milk "the next morning," and in sixteen summers never had a soft curd from those men; the Brant scales agreeing daily; the 1911 and 1924 rises, when no patron changed anything; and Vose's own chronology — the tests rose in 1924, before the petition and hearing he says frightened the watering into stopping.
2. *The tester stealing cream and writing low tests* — Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing; the valley's "settled opinion" (Rundle 1939), Toland, Oram's father. Defeated by: the ledger and receiving book — stolen cream arrives light, this arrived heavy (Rundle retracted on exactly this); the 1911 interval — same man, same supervision, tests up 0.19 when only the glass changed; 1924 — tests rose with Keddie still at the bench, before his dismissal; the proportional shape of the error; Strawn's repeated observation of him testing "slow and clean"; the burette's 0.95, which leaves no residue to have been stolen; Frick's judgment that his denial was accurate.
3. *The wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinning the cream* — Grigg's 1908 committee, Grigg's 1919 letter, Dr. Kepp (1926), Falke provisionally (1959). Defeated by: Bulletin No. 214 (1911) — at most 0.05 in winter and nothing measurable on summer pasture, four times too small and the wrong shape; the gap flat in July and February alike (Keddie, Roy, Hazel, Falke); Alder Corners — the same herds off the same wet ground tested 3.80 in the summer flush; the 1911 interval with the feed unchanged; and the true Hollow figure, 3.80, being a shade *above* Larrow Green's 3.78 — the bottom cows gave slightly better milk, not worse.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware? No.** Its exhibits were four books; the crate was never mentioned in any of the three sessions (Loomis, who took every word); it sat in the loft twenty feet above the two sessions held in the weigh room (Duncan, Oram); Frick's "every physical appliance… before us" refers to the plant then in use, the new glass; the board had Strawn's 1923 notebook recording the condemnation and nobody drew the inference or laid it beside the test book (Kyle). The award's "books and appliances of the station" was a form of words.

**What the tester himself believed.** From the summer of 1911 he suspected the glass — he came home saying "it was something in the glass," twice (Duncan) — but had only a suspicion against the practice of every station in the county, would not write what he could not prove, and set a question mark in the book instead. In 1925 his statement and unsent letter say he "cannot tell you why the figures are what they are," refuse to "put anything in its place that I do not know," and assert that he knew the milk came in whole — an implicit rejection of the water charge. Frick's recollection that he privately blamed the patrons contradicts that written position and cannot be verified; on the weight of evidence, he believed the fault was in the glass and could not prove it, and said so to no one but his family.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

In 1897 a Tarnet glass house sold the Ostrey Hollow skimming station six dozen milk pipettes that delivered 95 % of their mark. Because the co-op's Article VII paid patrons on the station's own test with no appeal, every farmer at the head of the valley was paid on readings one-twentieth low for twenty-six years — 76,000 lb of butterfat, $19,000 — while the surplus arrived at the creamery and sat in the ledger as "station gain, as usual." The valley blamed wet hay (1908), watered cans (1919) and a thieving tester (1925), and dismissed Ansel Keddie four days before a board found "no cause" and paid $3,800. Nobody suspected the one instrument a plant cannot check. Each proof lay in a different book — the tester's, the bookkeeper's, a farmer's, a cheesemaker's, an inspector's — kept by people who never compared them, until Keddie's granddaughter opened the crate in 1958. (145 words)
