## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — The Association and the Rule That Made the Test the Master (1889–1892; the office to 1945)

**Who.** The farmers of the Larrow Valley, Ordell County; Gideon Vose, first president; the office that later administered his rule — Effie Loomis (secretary 1901–1940), Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper 1905–1945), Hobart Sill (buttermaker at Larrow Green 1893–1934), Bertram Kyle (outer-desk clerk), and Selby Vose, Gideon's **son** [not nephew, as Toland and Rundle have it — Selby's own printed words: "My father, who was your first president"], manager 1913–1938 [Kyle's 1911 is wrong].

**What happened.** In 1889 the valley's farmers met at Larrow Green (the schoolhouse, by valley memory) and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of taking the Ashlin merchant's price for butter. Gideon Vose was elected president and held it twenty-two years, to 1911; he died in 1917. Not a lawyer, he wrote the by-laws himself at his kitchen table. In 1891 the main creamery was built at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek and the valley began to be paid in cash monthly. Before it, every man's arithmetic was his own.

The by-law that governs everything afterward is **Article VII**: every patron is paid according to the pounds of butterfat in his milk "ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." The last clause is the hinge. It allows no appeal to the creamery's test, the valley average, or any retest: the instrument at a man's own station is the whole bargain. Under payment by weight, nothing in the seven stories that follow would have cost anyone a dollar.

**When adopted — cannot be determined from the sources.** Loomis says 1892; Rundle 1891; Grigg "as I have always understood it" 1893. The printed pamphlet carries no year, only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the office move of 1938 — carried out with the rest and never arrived. Loomis herself concedes that nobody can say what the first four annual meetings resolved, which undercuts her own 1892. What is settled is only that payment by test was in force by the early 1890s.

**Quantities.** Founded 1889; creamery 1891; presidency 22 years; office (one room over a harness shop at Ashlin in Teague's day) moved 1938. On paper the Association was three things: creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin, skimming station seven miles up at Ostrey Hollow.

**Documents.** Printed by-laws pamphlet (accession Item 1); Loomis's "Notes toward a history" (1941) and her minute book from 1901; Rundle's jubilee pamphlet (1939); Grigg's 1919 covering letter; Falke's 1959 county report (declines to date the article); Vose's 1926 address.

*(~370 words)*

### Story 2 — The Station at Ostrey Hollow and the Short Glass (1895–1898)

**Who.** Ansel Keddie (born 1861; married Orra Brant 1889), tester and separator man; Hobart Sill, who lent the first outfit; Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet, and its later historian Junia Ammon.

**What happened.** In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley: a board building with a covered platform, an intake room holding scale, vat and separator, and a loft over the intake room reached by a ladder. Keddie went up in 1895 to ready it. It opened **4 May 1896**: milk weighed, sampled, tested, separated; cream wagoned down to Larrow Green daily, skim handed back for hogs and calves. Its own outfit had not come, so Sill made one up from his own glass at Larrow Green. The test book's first leaf: "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie." [Keddie's later "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery" is contradicted by his own leaf and by Sill.] On that borrowed glass the 1896 average was **3.80**.

The Ammon invoice of **14 April 1897**: 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68; 1 acid measure — .90; 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00; freight — 2.15; **total $77.93** [not $77.39; the lines sum to 77.93]. Seventy-two pipettes [not five dozen]. The outfit went into use **1 May 1897** [not 1898] — Orra's diary that day: "the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green." Sill's glass went back, one piece chipped. From then the tests read **3.61** — "1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since." The milk had not changed; the lot was short. Junia Ammon's reconstruction: a reground mold, a lot consistently wrong, an accident no gauge check could catch; the works books burned in 1931.

**Keddie's method** (his unsent letter): day began 5:30; every can weighed on a state-tested scale; a composite sample per patron over ten days, warmed blood-warm and poured vessel to vessel; a 17.6 c.c. charge blown into the numbered bottle; acid down the side; four bottles in the hand tester at the taught speed; fat read with dividers. Book in five columns — date, patron, pounds, test, fat; errors struck and initialed, never overwritten. Twenty-nine seasons, 1896–1924; thirty-one tests a week.

**Quantities.** First-season intake (4 May–end 1896): **cannot be determined** — not in the ledgers or anywhere (Nym); Sill's "about 900,000 lb" and Kyle's "a good 1,100,000" are both hearsay. The station column begins 1897. Nine patron families; six Hessel Bottom farms plus places on the hill.

**Documents.** Test book (first leaf; 1922 summary leaf); Ammon invoice (Item 2; facsimile in the firm's history); Orra's diary, 1 May 1897; Sill's 1934 remarks; Keddie's 1925 letter; Nym's accession notes.

*(~400 words)*

### Story 3 — Twenty-Five Years of Complaint and the Hay Theory (1902–1926)

**Who.** The Hessel Bottom patrons — chiefly Emmet Brant (1854–1929), largest patron, sixty-odd acres of the 160-acre meadow, thirty-eight cows; Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, committee chairman 1908; Dr. Roland Kepp, veterinary surgeon of Ashlin; the State Agricultural College at Farland.

**What happened.** From 1902 the Hollow men said their milk tested under the same class of milk at Larrow Green (Orra's diary, March 1902: "A. came home and would not eat"). The station's average stood at **3.61** against Larrow Green's **3.78** — 0.17 below the creamery's actual milk, **0.19** below what the Hollow milk truly was — and the gap was flat in every month, July as February, wet years and dry: not the shape a feed effect takes. Emmet Brant's weigh book, begun **1 January 1897** [not 1898 or 1900], showed his own barn scale and the station's agreeing day after day; whatever was wrong, it was not the weighing. His 1910 leaf: "two hundred thousand pounds even… Paid on a test of 3.42." Fat was 24¢ that year.

In January 1908 the board named a committee of three under Grigg, who hauled to Larrow Green and had no stake. It sampled the six Bottom farms [Loomis's seven is wrong] through **January, February and March 1908 only** — the report's own words, and Orra has them arriving by sleigh in January and gone by March [Grigg's 1919 letter claiming "every month of that year… on the pasture" is contradicted by his own report]. It tried both sets of scales, found the milk whole, "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter," and laid the cause to the wet sedge hay fed November to April: "the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." Emmet read it twice and said the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not. The report closed the question for eleven years.

**Bulletin No. 214** (1911), the College's controlled feeding trial: bog hay as the whole roughage "depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Grigg's 1919 covering letter and Kepp's 1926 Farm Club paper both cite it as "two tenths in the winter feeding" — a misquotation. Kepp put the gap at 0.21 and fat at 27¢, called the 1911 interval "six weeks of summer," and told the floor it was not good sense to look for a second cause in a building. The county agent in 1959 first allowed hay as a partial factor, then withdrew it on reading the bulletin: five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July.

**Quantities.** 3.61 vs 3.78 vs 3.80 true; gap 0.19 year-round; Bulletin 214: ≤0.05 winter, 0 summer; 160 acres, six farms; 200,000 lb at 3.42 in 1910; 24¢.

**Documents.** 1908 report (Item 11; reprinted 1919); Bulletin 214 (Item 12); Brant weigh book (Item 4); Orra's diary 1902 and 1908; Kepp's paper with discussion; Grigg's letter; Falke's report and added note.

*(~400 words)*

### Story 4 — Nine Weeks in 1911

**Who.** Ansel Keddie at the bench; every patron of the station; Merle Strawn, state inspector, who passed through in June.

**What happened.** On **8 May 1911** the station's own pipette broke — Orra that evening: "A. home cross about a broken measure. Rain all day." Keddie made shift with a borrowed one. Ivy Keddie says it came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon; Roy Brant's memory that the state man left his own in June cannot stand, because the break was 8 May and Strawn's June entry reads "Watched a test through. Nothing here to note." The test book: "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." From that morning the station's average went to **3.80** and held. "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." Eight May to 10 July is 63 days — **nine weeks** exactly [Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" are wrong; Kepp's "June and July" is wrong too]. Every farm on the Bottom rose together and fell together the same day. Teague entered a small replacement charge and nothing else.

Everything else was held constant: the same patrons and herds, the same pasture and season from one week to the next, the same acid, bottles, hand tester and scale, the same man at the crank under no different supervision. One variable changed — the measure — and the whole gap of nineteen hundredths went with it, then came back with the old glass. Keddie came home that summer saying "it was something in the glass," twice, at supper and by the door — but he would not write what he could not prove; the question mark stood fourteen years, and he went on writing what the glass gave him. Vaught in 1958: an experiment that ran itself under conditions no designed trial could better, unread for forty-seven years, disposing of every explanation that looks to the herds, the ground or the men, because none of those was altered and the figures were.

What the interval did not settle in 1911: which measure was the true one — a borrowed pipette could as easily have been over-delivering — nor the mechanism, nor the size over other years. Against Keddie's suspicion stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county.

**Quantities.** 8 May–10 July 1911; 63 days = 9 weeks; 3.61 → 3.80 → 3.61; difference 0.19; 3.80 × 0.95 = 3.61.

**Documents.** Test book entries of 8 May and 10 July 1911 and the 1922 summary leaf ("save the nine weeks of 1911… when it stood at 3.80"); Orra's diary, 8 May 1911; Strawn's 1911 entry; Teague's chapter; Ivy's 1959 talk; Bulletin 471 §IV; Duncan Keddie's notes; Kepp's "objection."

*(~390 words)*
