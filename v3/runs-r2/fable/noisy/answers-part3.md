# Answer sheet — noisy mode, r2, Fable — Part 3 of 3

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
(a) The pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 c.c.; Ivy's sheet of 9 March 1958 gives nine pipettes, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 → mean 16.72 c.c. Short 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.; 0.88 ÷ 17.6 = 0.05; 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95. The test reads fat as a percentage of a charge assumed to be 17.6 c.c. into a bottle neck ruled for that charge, so a charge 95% of the mark gives a reading 95% of the truth: **the tests were low by one part in twenty (5%), proportionally.** Vaught's introduction (16.27, ratio 0.9244) and the Chronicle (copying the press release) are rejected: the nine listed values average 16.72, the conclusion Vaught signed says "one part in twenty," and 0.9244 would put the true test at 3.905, irreconcilable (as Vaught himself notes) with 3.80 read on three other glasses.
(b) Recorded average 3.61 (summary leaf: "3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year"). True = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**; short by **0.19 points**. Confirmed independently: 3.80 in 1896 on Sill's glass, 3.80 in the nine weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure, 3.80 in 1924 on new glass, 3.796 for the four Bottom men at Alder Corners 1915–30, 3.78 at Larrow Green. The shortfall was proportional, not fixed: a 4.56 reading was truly 4.80 (short 0.24), a 3.42 reading truly 3.60 (short 0.18).
(c) 25 full delivery years 1898–1922, 40,000,000 lb milk: 40,000,000 × (0.0380 − 0.0361) = 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb**. Check: the 1898 ledger gain 3,040 lb × 25 years = 76,000.
(d) At the 25-cent average butterfat price (Effie, Ivy, Roy, Kyle — the figure got out from the annual sheets for the arbitration): 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons received the $3,800 award: 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **0.20, one fifth**. (Sill's and Kepp's 27 cents would give $20,520 and 18.5%; rejected as memory against the office's computed average.)

**C2.** Emmet Brant, 1910 leaf: 200,000 lb, paid on 3.42; butterfat 24 cents that year (Roy).
(a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
(b) True test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60; true fat 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb; owed and not credited **360 lb** (= 6,840 ÷ 19).
(c) 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40** (at the 25-cent series average, $90.00).

**C3.** Same 25 years, 40,000,000 lb.
(a) Credited on the station test: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb** (ledger 57,760 × 25).
(b) Received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb** (60,800 × 25).
(c) Difference 76,000 lb. As a percentage **of the fat credited** (the ledger's base): 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%, the ledger's "five and a quarter."** As a percentage **of the fat delivered**: 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00%, the "one part in twenty" of the glass.** Teague ("of the fat that was actually in the cream") and Vaught ("of the butterfat actually delivered") both name the wrong base; Ivy and Nym have it right.

**C4.**
(a) 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **$77.93**, as the invoice states; the Chronicle's and Junia Ammon's "$77.39" transpose the digits of the document they both reproduce.
(b) "6 doz." = **72 pipettes** (72 × $0.35 = $25.20 confirms; five dozen would be $21.00). Each short **0.88 c.c.** on the mean (individually 0.86 to 0.90).

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glass was in use from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923: 26 years and 42 days** (26 years, 1 month, 11 days), less the 63 days of 8 May–10 July 1911 on a borrowed measure — about 26 years less three weeks of actual use. The money runs over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**. They differ because 1897 (May–December) and 1923 (January–June) were part years on the short glass, and the ledger's station account is annual: only complete calendar years can be read from it (the summary leaf marks "1897, part year"). Inference: the two part-years, roughly 13.5 months, would add about 3,400 lb (~$850) that no source counts.
(b) **Cannot be determined from the sources.** Nym: "inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is none." Kyle: that season's milk was carried on the creamery's own books, the sheets never filed with the station papers, "not in any book I ever ruled." Two memories only: Sill, "about nine hundred thousand pounds," May to the end of 1896; Kyle, "a good eleven hundred thousand," from hearsay. Both are plausible against the later 1,600,000 lb full years (eight months ≈ 1,070,000 pro rata), and nothing chooses between them.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from 8 May 1911 ("Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure") to 10 July 1911 ("Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."): nine weeks, 63 days. Variable: the milk pipette alone — the station's own replaced by a borrowed one and then restored. Held constant: the patrons and their herds, the meadow and its hay, the weather and season across the switch-back on 10 July, the acid, the test bottles, the hand tester and its speed, the scales, the sampling jars, and the man and his method (Strawn watched him in June: "Nothing here to note"). Result: every Bottom farm's test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 the day the borrowed measure went in and fell to 3.61 the day the station's own came back. It establishes, as the 1958 burette cannot, that the station's measure was short *while it was in use*, in 1911, and that the whole 0.19 lay in it — a 1958 calculation shows what nine surviving pipettes delivered on a bench decades later, and could be answered by "they wore" or "the survivors are unrepresentative"; a step change and reversal on known dates in the working plant cannot. It does not establish: the calibration of the borrowed measure (nobody ran it against a burette; that it read true is inferred from 3.80's agreement with 1896, 1924 and Alder Corners); that the fault was constant across all twenty-six years (it samples one point); that all seventy-two pipettes were short; the cause (manufacture versus wear); whose measure it was; or, on its own, that nothing else was ever wrong — it shows the glass was sufficient, not that it was the only thing anybody could have suspected.

**D2.** Independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream:
1. *The Association's own ledger* (Teague, Kyle, Nym Item 6, Rundle's press note, Sill's receiving book): the cream reached Larrow Green *heavy* every year — 60,800 lb received against 57,760 credited in 1898, "station gain, as usual." Cream stolen at the Hollow would arrive light. Holds with no 1958 measurement at all — Rundle drew it in 1939.
2. *The station's own test book* (Ansel; Vaught §IV; Falke ¶8): in the nine weeks of 1911 the same man, alone, with the same cans, produced tests 0.19 higher on a borrowed measure and 0.19 lower again on his own. A thief does not raise his figures for nine weeks and mark the fall with a question mark. Holds without 1958.
3. *A Vessey County cheese book* (Rosalie Cudd; Roy): four of the same men's summer milk tested 3.78–3.82 over the ridge for sixteen seasons on Jerome Cudd's own new glass, and made cheese that never showed water — the fat was in the milk when it left the farms, and the home deficit matches the true test exactly. Holds without 1958.
4. *The state's circuit books* (Strawn): an inspector "kin to nobody" watched the test through nearly every year from 1907 — "slow and clean," "I did not let him hurry it" — and the 1908 committee stood by while the test was made and found "no dishonesty of any kind." Holds without 1958.
5. *The shape of the error* (Ivy, Vaught): it was a proportion of the fat present, hitting Jerseys at 0.24 and a 3.42 herd at 0.18, identical in July and February, wet and dry. A man dipping cream takes a quantity; only a short measure takes a fraction. Partly inferable before 1958 from the 1910 leaf and the Jerseys, fully only after.
6. *The glass itself* (Ivy's sheet, 1958): nine pipettes 5% short account for the whole gap with nothing left over.
These are independent because they rest on different records kept by different hands with different interests in different buildings — the Association's ledger at the office, the tester's book at the station, a cheesemaker's book in another county, the state's notebooks, a farm weigh book — "no two of them had ever been in one room before last year" (Ivy). Reasons 1–4 stand if the crate had never been opened.
The bookkeeper: Teague wrote the "station gain, as usual" line for forty years, said in 1925 that "cream is portable," and never questioned the entry that refuted him. By his own account he put the station account to Vose once, in 1916, was told to let it alone, and "a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time ... has generally begun to look for another employer"; Kyle says he "never asked anybody anything in forty years." He called the figure "furniture" and himself "the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." So: no — at most one mention of the account as an oddity, never as evidence, and he argued the opposite before the board with his own ledger in the room.

**D3.** Yes — Selby Vose knew the station's tests ran low before 1923, and there is no room to doubt it: the complaint had been in the talk at every annual meeting since 1902 (Grigg), the 1908 committee reported on it to the board, Teague put the station account to him in 1916, Strawn told him in 1919 that no water had ever been found there, and he wrote and signed the 1919 circular precisely because the Hollow's tests were low and he believed watering explained it. His own 1926 address concedes "for a generation the station tested below Larrow Green." What he did not know — or did not read — was that the ledger sheets he signed each spring from 1913 showed the missing fat arriving at Larrow Green: "he read the totals and he never read the lines above them ... put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" (Kyle); "a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me" (Teague). He carried the water theory to the 1925 board "without heat and without proof" (Frick) and was still defending it in 1926. The sources therefore establish that he knew the fact of the low tests throughout, had the refuting evidence under his hand yearly and once in words (1916), and chose not to look; whether he ever grasped what the gain line meant is not established.

14. **Sill**: the Hollow cream "ran heavy. Every year ... Always more. Never less" — while holding that water was in the cans, which would make it run light.
15. **Hazel**: "Quantities I have. Years I have not" — and then supplies 1900, 1899, 1921 and 1926, every one wrong; an honest self-warning rather than a contradiction, but it is on the page.
16. **Strawn**: twenty-eight books, "one to the year," for a circuit of 1907–1936.
17. **Rosalie**: "thirty-odd years nobody from over the ridge has ever asked" against a factory of 1913 (forty-five years) or her own tenure from 1941 (seventeen).

## Section F — Theory

**F1. The theory.** On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons of Tarnet shipped the Ostrey Hollow station six dozen milk pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. that delivered 16.72 c.c. — nineteen-twentieths of the mark, every one of them alike (Junia Ammon's guess of a reground mold is plausible and unproven). The Babcock-type test reads fat as a percentage of a charge the bottle's neck assumes to be 17.6 c.c.; a charge 5% short gives a reading 5% short, multiplicatively. Hence the size: 5% of the Bottom's true 3.80 is 0.19 — the same fraction, not the same number, for every herd, so 4.56 read 0.24 low and 3.42 read 0.18 low, and July read as short as February. It began on 1 May 1897, the day that glass replaced Sill's borrowed outfit (on which 1896 had read 3.80), and ended on 12 June 1923, when Strawn's burette condemned the lot and the July replacement read the mark (1924: 3.80). Nothing at the station or on the farms had to change, because nothing there was wrong: the scales were true to sealed weights, the acid and crank and bottles were checkable and checked, the man tested "slow and clean" before the state's own inspector — and the pipette "is the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else," the standard by which everything else was judged. The fat itself was never lost: it went down the valley in the cream and was booked each year as station gain, 5¼% "as usual." The one accident that could reveal it — nine weeks on another measure in 1911 — was recorded with a question mark and read by nobody.

**The three competing explanations.**
1. *Wet hay of Hessel Bottom* — Grigg's 1908 committee; Kepp's 1926 paper; Falke's first draft. Defeated by: Bulletin 214's own figures (≤0.05 in winter, nothing in summer) against a 0.19 that never varied by season; the three hill farms sharing the deficit; the same cows off the same meadow reading 3.80 at Alder Corners; the 1911 interval removing the gap with the hay unchanged; and Grigg's and Kepp's reliance on a misquoted "two tenths."
2. *Watered milk* — Selby Vose's 1919 circular and 1925 testimony; Sill; Vose again in 1926. Defeated by: Strawn's lactometer at every visit, "no added water, as always here," which he told Vose in 1919; the 1908 committee's inquiry into every barn's water; Rosalie's vats ("Cheese is a witness"), never a soft curd in sixteen summers; watered milk would make the cream arrive light, and it arrived heavy; and Vose's own inverted chronology — the rise of 1924 followed new glass, not a warning.
3. *The tester stealing cream* — Teague's insinuation before the board ("Cream is portable"); Vira's belief; Rundle's "settled opinion"; the cream hauler's conviction (Oram). Defeated by: the station-gain line Teague himself wrote — cream reached the Green heavy every year (Rundle saw this in 1939 and retracted); the 1911 interval, in which the supposed thief's own figures rose; Strawn's repeated observation of the test; the proportional shape of the error; and the crate.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No. Effie: the crate "was never mentioned in the hearing at all ... not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions." Frick's exhibit list is "four books and nothing else"; the appliances tried in the weigh room in April 1925 were the new glass in use since July 1923. The condemned crate sat in the loft twenty feet above sessions two and three (Duncan, Oram). The board did have Strawn's 1923 notebook — exhibit 4, with "These measures will not deliver their mark" in it — and nobody drew the inference (Kyle: no man "laid the second beside the fourth and looked").

**What the tester believed.** Publicly and in writing, nothing he could prove: "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are" (1925); "? — A.K." (1911); the unsent letter carries no charge against anyone. Duncan, who was in the kitchen, says that in the summer of 1911 his father came home saying "it was something in the glass," said it twice, and would not write it because he could not prove it against "the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county." Frick's 1946 recollection that Keddie privately "always thought the patrons were at fault" is uncorroborated and contradicted by Ansel's own unsent letter and by Roy's account of the family quarrel; Ivy's "he never knew" is a granddaughter's kindness, not testimony. Best resolution: he suspected the glass from 1911, could not prove it, and went to his grave — as Frick also says — accusing nobody.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** For twenty-six years (1897–1923) the Ostrey Hollow skimming station of the Larrow Valley creamery cooperative paid its patrons on butterfat tests one-twentieth too low, because the six dozen pipettes bought from Ammon & Sons in 1897 delivered 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 mark. The missing fat — 76,000 lb, $19,000 — went down to Larrow Green in the cream and was booked yearly as "station gain, 5¼%, as usual." The valley blamed wet hay (1908 committee, 1926 veterinarian), watered milk (the manager's 1919 circular), or the tester, Ansel Keddie, dismissed in 1925 while a board paid $3,800 "without admission." An inspector condemned the glass in 1923 without drawing the inference. In 1958 Keddie's granddaughter Ivy measured the crated pipettes. It took eight records — test book, ledgers, a farm weigh book, a cheese book over the ridge, the inspector's notebook, an invoice, a nine-week accident of 1911, a family — because each held one piece and none had ever been laid beside another.
