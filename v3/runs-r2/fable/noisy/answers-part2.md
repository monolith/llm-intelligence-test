# Answer sheet — noisy mode, r2, Fable — Part 2 of 3

## Section A — Reconstruction (stories 5–8)

### Story 5 — Over Redlow Ridge: the Alder Corners book (1913–1930; opened 1958)

**Who:** Jerome Cudd and his daughter Rosalie, Alder Corners Cheese Company, Vessey County; four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers (Roy Brant not among them).

**What happened.** Jerome Cudd built his factory in 1913 on the flat below the church, "because there was spring water on the lot and a hard road out to the east," and opened it in 1914. From 1915 four Bottom men hauled their summer-flush milk (about 1 June to mid-September) over the four-mile lumber-road switchback of Redlow Ridge — shut from November to April — for sixteen seasons, the last in 1930, because cheese paid better in the flush, Cudd bought hard, and it was a shorter pull. Their cans were on his platform by seven. Cudd tested on his own bench with glassware bought new, kept his own book, and never compared a figure with anybody's; he thought the Ordell people kept their theory about that bottom "the way some families have a ghost: it explained things, and it cost nothing, and nobody was ever going to test it." At home the four were talked of "as though they had gone over to the enemy"; Roy stayed because "the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about."

**Quantities.** Rosalie's table, copied for Ivy in 1958: 1915 3.79, 1916 3.81, 1917 3.78, 1918 3.80, 1919 3.80, 1920 3.82, 1921 3.79, 1922 3.78, 1923 3.81, 1924 3.80, 1925 3.79, 1926 3.82, 1927 3.78, 1928 3.80, 1929 3.81, 1930 3.79 — sixteen seasons, mean 3.796, "wet years and dry ones, whether the meadow had been under water in May or hard as a road." Cudd's 1919 entry: "3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." The same milk was paid at home on 3.61. Watered milk will not knit in the vat — "Cheese is a witness" — and in sixteen summers he never docked a cent. Only 1915–1923, eight or nine seasons, fall inside the short-glass years; Ivy's "fourteen overlapping seasons" and Nym's copy of 1919 alone are both wrong counts.

**Afterward.** Jerome died in March 1941 "between the setting and the cutting of a vat"; Rosalie — twelve when the first vat was set; his daughter, not his niece as Ivy and the Chronicle had it — took the factory that spring and by 1958 had changed "the boiler and the roof and nothing else." Nobody from Ordell asked for the figures until Ivy's four-line letter: "I have listened to forty years of your valley explaining that meadow to itself, and I never once heard anybody go up there and look." The book went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970.

**Documents:** r10, r09, r12 §V, r07 (Item 13), r04, r18, r17, r19.

### Story 6 — Two scales, one family, and the water letter (1897–1929)

**Who:** Emmet Brant (1854–1929), largest farmer on Hessel Bottom; his younger sister Orra Keddie (1866–1948), Ansel's wife; their children Roy Brant (born c. 1888) and Duncan Keddie (born 1894); Selby Vose; Bertram Kyle.

**What happened.** Emmet kept sixty-odd acres of the 160-acre bottom and thirty-eight cows, and from 1 January 1897 (Frick's 1925 exhibit list, Nym's deposit slip, Duncan; Roy's "1898" and Hazel's "1900" are memory) wrote every can into a weigh book: his own scale in one column, the station's in the other. They agreed "barring a pound or two ... when a can got slopped on the hill." Whatever was wrong at the station, "it was not the weighing." The 1910 leaf: "two hundred thousand pounds even ... Paid on a test of 3.42" — true test 3.60; 360 lb of fat, about $86 at that year's 24 cents. Emmet, one of the first to complain in 1902, was Ansel's brother-in-law and had eaten at his table on Sundays since 1889.

In June 1919 Selby Vose sent the Hollow's patrons a printed warning about water in their milk — typed by Kyle, signed by Vose and no one else; Strawn had already told him no lactometer test at that station had ever found any. Ansel would not mail it: he carried it door to door and stood while each man read it, coming home at eleven "with his hat still on." Orra's diary that day says eleven families; Vira, Ansel's own 1925 statement, Roy, Sill and Hazel say nine — the count cannot be settled. Vira's father read it twice with his hat on; Hazel's mother put hers in the stove. Two days later Emmet stood in the Keddies' yard: he had hauled whole milk since the day the station opened and Ansel knew it — why carry the paper if he did not believe it? "He carried it because he would not have another man carry it." Emmet never entered the house again; Orra walked to Larrow Green rather than pass his lane. Roy: the quarrel was not about water but that the man who kept the book "ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not."

Emmet's name was on the January 1925 petition. He died in spring 1929 — "Ten years and neither of them would go up the road first." Roy kept the book the same way, lent it to Ivy in 1958 ("lent, not left"), and at his death in 1962 it went to Hazel, and in 1963 to the Rooms.

**Kinship settled.** Orra and Emmet were full siblings (Roy: "whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it"; Duncan); Hazel's "half sister by his father's second wife" is rejected.

**Documents:** r09, r17, r18, r24, r01, r03, r14, r23, r07 (Item 4), r13, r08, r06.

### Story 7 — Condemned, arbitrated, dismissed (1923–1934)

**Who:** Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector; Selby Vose; the Ostrey Hollow patrons; Alonzo Frick's board of three; Ansel Keddie.

**What happened.** The state issued burettes in 1921; Strawn skipped the Hollow's glass in 1922 ("the day being short") and on 12 June 1923 — his own dated entry, corroborated by the 28 June order and Ammon's 2 July reply; Vose's and Rundle's "12 July," Kyle's "'22" and Hazel's "'21" are memory — ran the pipettes against it: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them ... Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." The crate went up the ladder into the loft. Ammon regretted the trouble and could say nothing of the lot; new glass came in July (Orra: "new glass at the station and new pails, and the roof is still bad"), delivered its mark in October, and the 1924 average stood at 3.80 (Vose's 3.90 and Falke's 3.85 fall to Ansel's book, read by Strawn and Vaught). In the office it got "no more said than a broken window."

In January 1925 the patrons petitioned — $19,600 "to the dollar"; 67,000 lb by their reckoning — and a board of three under Frick, "the one of the three who kept no cow," sat from 17 March at the Association's office at Ashlin and then twice, in April, in the station weigh room, with the crate twenty feet overhead. Four exhibits: the test book, the receiving book, Emmet's weigh book (lent, wanted back), Strawn's 1923 notebook — "four books and nothing else"; nobody laid the second beside the fourth. Three explanations were argued: water (Vose, "without heat and without proof"), cream-taking (Teague: "Cream is portable"), wet hay (the 1908 report, read for an afternoon). Ansel's nine sentences: "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are." He was put out on 30 April — "Twenty-nine seasons and a letter"; he hung up his apron and ate his ham — four days before the award of 4 May: "finds no cause established ... thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account." One fifth of the $19,000 that account had accumulated. Vira's and Oram's "$3,500" fall to the minute book. A quick new tester was at the bench that fall.

**After.** Vose, 1926: "I will not have peace bought a second time." Ansel kept four or five cows, built the cow shed and whistled, turned carpenter at sixty-six for a quarter of the pay, and died at home in February 1934, with men at the burying who had not spoken to him in nine years. "A man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

**Documents:** r06, r07 (Items 7–10), r08, r13, r02, r03, r05, r23, r24, r21, r17, r18, r12, r20, r22, r01.

### Story 8 — Up the ladder: the crate, the bulletin, the books (1958–1972)

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (born 1931), Ansel's granddaughter, graduate student at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught; Rosalie Cudd; Roy Brant; the *Ashlin Chronicle*; Orin Falke; Halbert Nym.

**What happened.** In February 1958 Ivy climbed the ladder into the loft over the intake room and found the crate — AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET stenciled on the end — under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, where it had sat since June 1923 (Nym's "found in the Association office at Ashlin" is wrong against Ivy, Duncan, Hazel, Oram and Falke). Nine pipettes survived, two chipped. On 9 March at Farland she ran each five times against a certified burette: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — mean 16.72 c.c. against 17.6 blown; short 0.88 c.c.; "95 hundredths of the mark." She then brought five records into one room that had never met: the test book, Emmet's weigh book (borrowed from Roy), Strawn's 1923 notebook (in the college library since 1947, unasked-for for eleven years), Rosalie's sixteen seasons, and the ledgers; she also sat in Vira's kitchen and heard about the letter in the drawer.

Bulletin No. 471, October 1958, under Vaught's and her names, concludes: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain." Vaught's own introduction carries a transposed mean (16.27), ratio 0.9244, shortfall 0.295, 118,000 lb, "1898," and "five dozen" — sound arithmetic on a wrong premise, which he himself flags as irreconcilable with 1924's 3.80.

**The retellings of the retelling.** The Chronicle (3 October 1958) made Ivy a great-niece, counted eleven pipettes at 16.27, priced the loss at $19,600 and the invoice at $77.39, and accused Ammon & Sons of knowingly selling short; Ivy's own words: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not." Falke's 1959 county report corrected "great-niece" and withdrew his wet-hay concession. Ivy read "What the Crate Held" to the Historical Society in 1959; Rundle's reprint added a footnote ("Eleven pipettes"). The test book and crate reached the Rooms in 1959, the weigh book in 1963, the Alder Corners book the Vessey County Society in 1970; Nym's 1972 accession notes swap the two keepers' names. The station had closed in 1946. Duncan, 1970: "the five books are now in three buildings and are none of them ours."

**Documents:** r04, r11, r07, r19, r22, r18, r17, r21, r10, r09, r12, r01, r24 (editor's note).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Derivation: Ansel (born 1861) married Orra Brant in 1889; their son Duncan was born 1894 (r18 "My father was Ansel Keddie"; Orra's diary has Duncan five years old in September 1899 and going to teach at Ashlin at seventeen in October 1911); Duncan's daughter Ivy was born 1931 (r18; the Chronicle's "twenty-seven" in October 1958 agrees). Ivy herself says "my grandfather" (r04); Nym, Roy ("Duncan's girl Ivy"), Hazel, Rundle and Oram concur. Pointing elsewhere: the Chronicle (r19) calls her his "great-niece," and Falke (r22) copied it — he says his report was prompted by the newspaper notice — then corrected himself on the page: "the granddaughter and not the great-niece." Two narrators, one source, and the source was "put together in a day and a half from a college press release and two telephone calls." The father's own account outranks it.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant were sister and brother; their children Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant were therefore first cousins; Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel are the children of first cousins, i.e. second cousins. Roy, Duncan, Hazel and Rundle's 1959 footnote all give it so; Duncan and Hazel each spell out the derivation. Vira and Oram say "first cousin once removed," which would need Ivy and Roy to stand a generation apart from where they do; both are outsiders repeating valley talk ("that is the way of it"). Left open by the sources: whether Orra and Emmet were full siblings (Roy, Duncan) or half-siblings by their father's second wife (Hazel) — Roy explicitly rejects the stepmother story and is a generation nearer, so I take full siblings, but it does not change the cousin degree either way; Orra's birth year (1866 by her own diary and the editor, 1868 by Duncan, who also makes her 82 at death in 1948, which gives 1866); Emmet's wife and Roy's wife are never named; nothing is said of Ivy's mother.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose**, the Association's founder, first president 1889–1911, and author of the by-laws including Article VII. Selby says so himself in the 1926 address ("My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law"); Effie, secretary from 1901, says "son of the late president"; Duncan and Kyle — the clerk who sat outside Selby's door for twenty-five years and calls the nephew story "nonsense" — agree. Against: Vira and Rundle say "nephew," Rundle even quoting Selby on "his uncle"; but Rundle's pamphlet is by his own account "what the valley remembers, tidied," and Vira's is hearsay. A man's own statement of his parentage, in print, to a meeting that knew both men, outranks two memories. Why it matters: the rule that made the station's pipette the whole of every patron's pay was written by the father; the son signed every ledger sheet carrying the "station gain" line from 1913, told Teague in 1916 to let it alone, wrote and signed the 1919 water circular, argued water before the 1925 board "without heat and without proof," and in 1926 refused to have "peace bought a second time." An inherited office read as an inheritance — "the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little" — explains a manager who put his thumb on the total and signed, and why the water letter landed at Ostrey Hollow as "somebody's cousin saying it." His own Jerseys, tested at the home place at 4.56, never passed through the short glass.

**B4. None is established.** Vira says Orra was "first cousin of old Mrs. Vose" — something "everybody up here knew and nobody wrote down anywhere"; Oram says the Keddies and Voses "were cousins of some sort," from his grandmother, and "could not draw you the line on paper." Against them Duncan, Orra's son, writing to set the family right: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses"; people confuse them with the Ashlin Voses; his mother, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never put a Vose in it. Orra's diary, which names Loomises, Griggs and Brants, never mentions a Vose. I cannot prove a negative from these sources, but the only first-hand family witness denies it and both affirmers concede there is no record. The relationship should be treated as valley talk — talk that mattered, because it made the 1919 circular feel like a cousin's accusation.
