### Story 1 — Article VII and the outfit from Tarnet (1889–1897)

**Who:** Gideon Vose and the farmers of the Larrow Valley; Hobart Sill, buttermaker; Ansel Keddie, tester; Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet.

**What happened.** In 1889 the valley's farmers, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin for whatever the merchant allowed, organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association at a schoolhouse meeting in Larrow Green. Gideon Vose, "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man" who had once thought of the law, was president 1889–1911 and drafted the by-laws at his own kitchen table. The creamery went up on Larrow Creek below the mill in 1891; Sill came to its churn in 1893. Article VII — every patron paid "according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test ... and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — made the test the whole of the bargain. Its year cannot be fixed: Effie says 1892, Rundle 1891, Grigg 1893; the printed by-laws say only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the 1889–92 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, for the six Hessel Bottom farms and three hill places. Ansel Keddie (born 1861, a native of the Hollow, married to Orra Brant in 1889) went up in 1895 to ready it and tested from the opening on 4 May 1896. No glass had come, so Sill sent up his own used outfit from the Green — the first leaf reads "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes"; Ansel's later "Ashlin creamery" is a slip against his own hand. On that borrowed glass the 1896 average was 3.80. Intake that first season is unrecorded (Nym); Sill remembered about 900,000 lb, Kyle 1,100,000.

**The quantities that matter.** The invoice of 14 April 1897: 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at $.35 = $25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at $.22 = $31.68; acid measure $.90; four-bottle hand tester $18.00; freight $2.15; total $77.93 (the lines sum; the Chronicle's and Junia Ammon's "$77.39" misread it). Orra's diary, 1 May 1897: "the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green." That dated entry, Sill's "the spring after," and the bulletin's conclusion settle 1 May 1897 against Vaught's introduction and Kyle (1898). The 72 pipettes were blown short — they delivered 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 mark, nineteen-twentieths. Junia Ammon's 1949 guess is a reground mold, the lot "consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find"; the works books burned in 1931, so it stays a guess. Her claim that the house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900 is refuted by her own facsimile.

### Story 2 — "Station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual" (1897–1922; retracted 1939)

**Who:** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Bertram Kyle, outer-desk clerk; Selby Vose, manager 1913–1938; Hobart Sill at the churn; Effie Loomis, secretary; Dell Rundle.

**What happened.** From the station column's first year the annual ledger carried three figures and a sentence for Ostrey Hollow. The 1898 entry, quoted identically by Teague, Nym, Rundle and Kyle: butterfat credited to patrons on station test 57,760 lb; butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream 60,800 lb; "Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." It recurred every year in the bookkeeper's hand, was copied fair by Kyle, signed at the foot by Selby Vose each spring from 1913 with his thumb on the total, and read out at the annual meeting "to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees." Sill, who weighed the Hollow's cream at the Green from 1893, had it heavy every year — "Always more. Never less" — and "nobody ever asked me." Teague put the account to Vose once, in 1916, and was told to let it alone; Kyle says Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years." Teague's own verdict: "A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture."

**Quantities.** Twenty-five full delivery years, 1898–1922: 40,000,000 lb of milk, 1,600,000 a year; credited at 3.61 = 1,444,000 lb fat; received at 3.80 = 1,520,000 lb; the gain 76,000 lb, which is 5.26 per cent of the credited fat (the ledger's base) and 5.00 per cent of the fat delivered — Teague and Vaught both name the wrong base. At the 25-cent average Kyle "got out for the arbitration," $19,000. Teague's 44,000,000 lb and 3.16 are rejected by the ledger's own arithmetic (57,760 ÷ 1,600,000 = 3.61 per cent; 1,600,000 × 25 = 40,000,000); Rundle repeats the 44 million and Frick the 3.16 from him.

**Oddities.** Teague says he devised the line in 1905, yet the 1898 entry already reads "as usual" and Nym finds the column from 1897. The cream hauler (Oram's father) believed less cream came off the station platform than went on; the receiving book says the opposite. In 1939 Rundle, seeing the entry on another errand, retracted his cream-theft chapter while in press — "It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand" — nineteen years before the bulletin; the pamphlet was reissued in 1959 with the chapter unaltered. The $3,800 award of 1925 was charged to this same station account.

**Documents:** r05, r23, r07 (Items 6, 16), r14, r12, r02, r04, r11, r21, r13.

### Story 3 — The wet hay of Hessel Bottom (1902–1926; withdrawn 1959)

**Who:** The Hessel Bottom patrons; Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green and his 1908 committee; the State Agricultural College (Bulletin No. 214, 1911); Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin; Orin Falke, county agent.

**What happened.** In 1902 it was said at the store that the Hollow's milk "runs poorer than theirs down the valley"; Emmet Brant was among the first to say it aloud and was laughed at; Ansel came home and would not eat — "it is my figures they will look at." In January 1908 the board appointed a three-man committee under Grigg, not a patron of the station, later "abused for the finding by men on both sides of it." Orra's diary has "four men up the road today in Grigg's sleigh" in January and "the committee gone back down" in March, confirming the report's own "January, February and March" against Grigg's 1919 claim of "every month of that year." The report: six farms on a springy meadow under water every spring; sedge-and-rush hay fed November to April; herds thin in the flank; "the milk is whole and the scales are honest"; "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter"; the wet hay thins the cream and "the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." Emmet read it twice, put it in a drawer, and said the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not.

Bulletin No. 214 (1911) fed bog hay as the whole roughage and found the fat depressed "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Grigg, reprinting his report in 1919 when the question reopened, and Kepp, in his 1926 Farm Club paper, both cite it as "two tenths" — a misquotation, since four sources print the paragraph verbatim. Kepp put the county-wide wet-bottom deficit at a fifth of a point and the Hollow's at 0.21 (Vose's figure of the same year), called the matter "settled already, in the literature," dismissed 1911 as "six weeks of summer figures," and had "never in his life read a test."

**Why it fails.** The gap was 0.19 in July as in February, "flat as a floor" (Ansel, Roy, Hazel, Falke) — four times Bulletin 214's winter ceiling and beyond its summer nothing; the hill farms suffered equally; four Bottom men's milk off the same meadow tested 3.80 at Alder Corners; nine weeks of 1911 removed the gap without touching a cow. Falke's 1959 report first allowed hay as "a real if partial factor," then, on reading Bulletin 214, withdrew the concession: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July."

### Story 4 — Nine weeks on a borrowed measure (8 May–10 July 1911)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie; the Hessel Bottom herds; Merle Strawn; Duncan Keddie, then seventeen.

**What happened.** On 8 May 1911 the station's pipette broke — "A. home cross about a broken measure. Rain all day, the low piece under water again" (Orra's diary). Ansel wrote in the test book: "Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." For nine weeks — 63 days — every Bottom farm's test stood at 3.80 instead of 3.61. Strawn came through in June, watched a test, and wrote "Nothing here to note." On 10 July: "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." The 1922 summary leaf records the interval as the one exception in twenty-five years. Nothing else changed: same patrons, herds, pasture, acid, bottles, crank and man — "one object changed" (Ivy). Teague booked "a small charge for replacement" and remembered six weeks; Kepp made it "six weeks of June and July" with the stock on the aftermath; the dated entries make it nine.

Duncan remembers the kitchen: his father came home that summer saying "it was something in the glass," said it twice, at supper and by the door, "which was not his way" — and put only a question mark in the book, because he would not write what he could not prove. He kept on fourteen more years writing the figures the glass gave him, "and the figures were what convicted him."

**What it establishes.** The shortfall lay in the station's own measure — not in the land, the cows, the water or the man — and its whole size: the full 0.19, the same 3.80 the station read on borrowed glass in 1896, on new glass in 1924, and that the Bottom's milk read at Alder Corners. Vaught: an experiment "that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years."

**Loose ends.** Whose measure it was is unresolved — up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon (Ivy), "from town" (Teague), or left by the state man (Roy; but Strawn's June note is silent and he carried no burette until 1921). Why a station that bought six dozen pipettes in 1897 had to borrow one is explained by nobody. And nobody read the interval as evidence until 1958.

**Documents:** r03, r04, r11 §IV, r09, r05, r16, r18, r24, r22 ¶6–8, r06, r17.
