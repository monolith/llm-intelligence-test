### Story 1 — Article VII and the outfit from Tarnet (1889–1897)

**Who:** Gideon Vose and the farmers of the Larrow Valley; Hobart Sill, buttermaker; Ansel Keddie, tester; Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet.

**What happened.** In 1889 the valley's farmers, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin for whatever the merchant allowed, organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association at a schoolhouse meeting in Larrow Green. Gideon Vose, "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man" who had once thought of the law, was president 1889–1911 and drafted the by-laws at his own kitchen table. The creamery went up on Larrow Creek below the mill in 1891; Sill came to its churn in 1893. Article VII — every patron paid "according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test ... and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — made the test the whole of the bargain. Its year cannot be fixed: Effie says 1892, Rundle 1891, Grigg 1893; the printed by-laws say only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the 1889–92 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, for the six Hessel Bottom farms and three hill places. Ansel Keddie (born 1861, a native of the Hollow, married to Orra Brant in 1889) went up in 1895 to ready it and tested from the opening on 4 May 1896. No glass had come, so Sill sent up his own used outfit from the Green — the first leaf reads "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes"; Ansel's later "Ashlin creamery" is a slip against his own hand. On that borrowed glass the 1896 average was 3.80. Intake that first season is unrecorded (Nym); Sill remembered about 900,000 lb, Kyle 1,100,000.

**The quantities that matter.** The invoice of 14 April 1897: 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at $.35 = $25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at $.22 = $31.68; acid measure $.90; four-bottle hand tester $18.00; freight $2.15; total $77.93 (the lines sum; the Chronicle's and Junia Ammon's "$77.39" misread it). Orra's diary, 1 May 1897: "the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green." That dated entry, Sill's "the spring after," and the bulletin's conclusion settle 1 May 1897 against Vaught's introduction and Kyle (1898). The 72 pipettes were blown short — they delivered 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 mark, nineteen-twentieths. Junia Ammon's 1949 guess is a reground mold, the lot "consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find"; the works books burned in 1931, so it stays a guess. Her claim that the house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900 is refuted by her own facsimile.

### Story 2 — "Station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual" (1897–1922; retracted 1939)

**Who:** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Bertram Kyle, outer-desk clerk; Selby Vose, manager 1913–1938; Hobart Sill at the churn; Effie Loomis, secretary; Dell Rundle.

**What happened.** From the station column's first year the annual ledger carried three figures and a sentence for Ostrey Hollow. The 1898 entry, quoted identically by Teague, Nym, Rundle and Kyle: butterfat credited to patrons on station test 57,760 lb; butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream 60,800 lb; "Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." It recurred every year in the bookkeeper's hand, was copied fair by Kyle, signed at the foot by Selby Vose each spring from 1913 with his thumb on the total, and read out at the annual meeting "to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees." Sill, who weighed the Hollow's cream at the Green from 1893, had it heavy every year — "Always more. Never less" — and "nobody ever asked me." Teague put the account to Vose once, in 1916, and was told to let it alone; Kyle says Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years." Teague's own verdict: "A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture."

**Quantities.** Twenty-five full delivery years, 1898–1922: 40,000,000 lb of milk, 1,600,000 a year; credited at 3.61 = 1,444,000 lb fat; received at 3.80 = 1,520,000 lb; the gain 76,000 lb, which is 5.26 per cent of the credited fat (the ledger's base) and 5.00 per cent of the fat delivered — Teague and Vaught both name the wrong base. At the 25-cent average Kyle "got out for the arbitration," $19,000. Teague's 44,000,000 lb and 3.16 are rejected by the ledger's own arithmetic (57,760 ÷ 1,600,000 = 3.61 per cent; 1,600,000 × 25 = 40,000,000); Rundle repeats the 44 million and Frick the 3.16 from him.

**Oddities.** Teague says he devised the line in 1905, yet the 1898 entry already reads "as usual" and Nym finds the column from 1897. The cream hauler (Oram's father) believed less cream came off the station platform than went on; the receiving book says the opposite. In 1939 Rundle, seeing the entry on another errand, retracted his cream-theft chapter while in press — "It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand" — nineteen years before the bulletin; the pamphlet was reissued in 1959 with the chapter unaltered. The $3,800 award of 1925 was charged to this same station account.

**Documents:** r05, r23, r07 (Items 6, 16), r14, r12, r02, r04, r11, r21, r13.

### Story 3 — The wet hay of Hessel Bottom (1902–1926; withdrawn 1959)

**Who:** The Hessel Bottom patrons; Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green and his 1908 committee; the State Agricultural College (Bulletin No. 214, 1911); Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin; Orin Falke, county agent.

**What happened.** In 1902 it was said at the store that the Hollow's milk "runs poorer than theirs down the valley"; Emmet Brant was among the first to say it aloud and was laughed at; Ansel came home and would not eat — "it is my figures they will look at." In January 1908 the board appointed a three-man committee under Grigg, not a patron of the station, later "abused for the finding by men on both sides of it." Orra's diary has "four men up the road today in Grigg's sleigh" in January and "the committee gone back down" in March, confirming the report's own "January, February and March" against Grigg's 1919 claim of "every month of that year." The report: six farms on a springy meadow under water every spring; sedge-and-rush hay fed November to April; herds thin in the flank; "the milk is whole and the scales are honest"; "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter"; the wet hay thins the cream and "the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." Emmet read it twice, put it in a drawer, and said the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not.

Bulletin No. 214 (1911) fed bog hay as the whole roughage and found the fat depressed "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Grigg, reprinting his report in 1919 when the question reopened, and Kepp, in his 1926 Farm Club paper, both cite it as "two tenths" — a misquotation, since four sources print the paragraph verbatim. Kepp put the county-wide wet-bottom deficit at a fifth of a point and the Hollow's at 0.21 (Vose's figure of the same year), called the matter "settled already, in the literature," dismissed 1911 as "six weeks of summer figures," and had "never in his life read a test."

**Why it fails.** The gap was 0.19 in July as in February, "flat as a floor" (Ansel, Roy, Hazel, Falke) — four times Bulletin 214's winter ceiling and beyond its summer nothing; the hill farms suffered equally; four Bottom men's milk off the same meadow tested 3.80 at Alder Corners; nine weeks of 1911 removed the gap without touching a cow. Falke's 1959 report first allowed hay as "a real if partial factor," then, on reading Bulletin 214, withdrew the concession: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July."

**Documents:** r15, r16, r22, r04, r07 (Items 11–12), r11 §VI, r09, r24, r03, r12, r01, r17.

### Story 4 — Nine weeks on a borrowed measure (8 May–10 July 1911)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie; the Hessel Bottom herds; Merle Strawn; Duncan Keddie, then seventeen.

**What happened.** On 8 May 1911 the station's pipette broke — "A. home cross about a broken measure. Rain all day, the low piece under water again" (Orra's diary). Ansel wrote in the test book: "Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." For nine weeks — 63 days — every Bottom farm's test stood at 3.80 instead of 3.61. Strawn came through in June, watched a test, and wrote "Nothing here to note." On 10 July: "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." The 1922 summary leaf records the interval as the one exception in twenty-five years. Nothing else changed: same patrons, herds, pasture, acid, bottles, crank and man — "one object changed" (Ivy). Teague booked "a small charge for replacement" and remembered six weeks; Kepp made it "six weeks of June and July" with the stock on the aftermath; the dated entries make it nine.

Duncan remembers the kitchen: his father came home that summer saying "it was something in the glass," said it twice, at supper and by the door, "which was not his way" — and put only a question mark in the book, because he would not write what he could not prove. He kept on fourteen more years writing the figures the glass gave him, "and the figures were what convicted him."

**What it establishes.** The shortfall lay in the station's own measure — not in the land, the cows, the water or the man — and its whole size: the full 0.19, the same 3.80 the station read on borrowed glass in 1896, on new glass in 1924, and that the Bottom's milk read at Alder Corners. Vaught: an experiment "that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years."

**Loose ends.** Whose measure it was is unresolved — up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon (Ivy), "from town" (Teague), or left by the state man (Roy; but Strawn's June note is silent and he carried no burette until 1921). Why a station that bought six dozen pipettes in 1897 had to borrow one is explained by nobody. And nobody read the interval as evidence until 1958.

**Documents:** r03, r04, r11 §IV, r09, r05, r16, r18, r24, r22 ¶6–8, r06, r17.

### Story 5 — Over Redlow Ridge: the Alder Corners book (1913–1930; opened 1958)

**Who:** Jerome Cudd and his daughter Rosalie, Alder Corners Cheese Company, Vessey County; four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers (Roy Brant not among them).

**What happened.** Jerome Cudd built his factory in 1913 on the flat below the church, "because there was spring water on the lot and a hard road out to the east," and opened it in 1914. From 1915 four Bottom men hauled their summer-flush milk (about 1 June to mid-September) over the four-mile lumber-road switchback of Redlow Ridge — shut from November to April — for sixteen seasons, the last in 1930, because cheese paid better in the flush, Cudd bought hard, and it was a shorter pull. Their cans were on his platform by seven. Cudd tested on his own bench with glassware bought new, kept his own book, and never compared a figure with anybody's; he thought the Ordell people kept their theory about that bottom "the way some families have a ghost: it explained things, and it cost nothing, and nobody was ever going to test it." At home the four were talked of "as though they had gone over to the enemy"; Roy stayed because "the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about."

**Quantities.** Rosalie's table, copied for Ivy in 1958: 1915 3.79, 1916 3.81, 1917 3.78, 1918 3.80, 1919 3.80, 1920 3.82, 1921 3.79, 1922 3.78, 1923 3.81, 1924 3.80, 1925 3.79, 1926 3.82, 1927 3.78, 1928 3.80, 1929 3.81, 1930 3.79 — sixteen seasons, mean 3.796, "wet years and dry ones, whether the meadow had been under water in May or hard as a road." Cudd's 1919 entry: "3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." The same milk was paid at home on 3.61. Watered milk will not knit in the vat — "Cheese is a witness" — and in sixteen summers he never docked a cent. Only 1915–1923, eight or nine seasons, fall inside the short-glass years; Ivy's "fourteen overlapping seasons" and Nym's copy of 1919 alone are both wrong counts.

**Afterward.** Jerome died in March 1941 "between the setting and the cutting of a vat"; Rosalie — twelve when the first vat was set; his daughter, not his niece as Ivy and the Chronicle had it — took the factory that spring and by 1958 had changed "the boiler and the roof and nothing else." Nobody from Ordell asked for the figures until Ivy's four-line letter: "I have listened to forty years of your valley explaining that meadow to itself, and I never once heard anybody go up there and look." The book went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970.

**Documents:** r10, r09, r12 §V, r07 (Item 13), r04, r18, r17, r19.

### Story 6 — Two scales, one family, and the water letter (1897–1929)

**Who:** Emmet Brant (1854–1929), largest farmer on Hessel Bottom; his younger sister Orra Keddie (1866–1948), Ansel's wife; their children Roy Brant (born c. 1888) and Duncan Keddie (born 1894); Selby Vose; Bertram Kyle.

**What happened.** Emmet kept sixty-odd acres of the 160-acre bottom and thirty-eight cows, and from 1 January 1897 (Frick's 1925 exhibit list, Nym's deposit slip, Duncan; Roy's "1898" and Hazel's "1900" are memory) wrote every can into a weigh book: his own scale in one column, the station's in the other. They agreed "barring a pound or two ... when a can got slopped on the hill." Whatever was wrong at the station, "it was not the weighing." The 1910 leaf: "two hundred thousand pounds even ... Paid on a test of 3.42" — true test 3.60; 360 lb of fat, about $86 at that year's 24 cents. Emmet, one of the first to complain in 1902, was Ansel's brother-in-law and had eaten at his table on Sundays since 1889.

In June 1919 Selby Vose sent the Hollow's patrons a printed warning about water in their milk — typed by Kyle, signed by Vose and no one else; Strawn had already told him no lactometer test at that station had ever found any. Ansel would not mail it: he carried it door to door and stood while each man read it, coming home at eleven "with his hat still on." Orra's diary that day says eleven families; Vira, Ansel's own 1925 statement, Roy, Sill and Hazel say nine — the count cannot be settled. Vira's father read it twice with his hat on; Hazel's mother put hers in the stove. Two days later Emmet stood in the Keddies' yard: he had hauled whole milk since the day the station opened and Ansel knew it — why carry the paper if he did not believe it? "He carried it because he would not have another man carry it." Emmet never entered the house again; Orra walked to Larrow Green rather than pass his lane. Roy: the quarrel was not about water but that the man who kept the book "ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not."

Emmet's name was on the January 1925 petition. He died in spring 1929 — "Ten years and neither of them would go up the road first." Roy kept the book the same way, lent it to Ivy in 1958 ("lent, not left"), and at his death in 1962 it went to Hazel, and in 1963 to the Rooms.

**Kinship settled.** Orra and Emmet were full siblings (Roy: "whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it"; Duncan); Hazel's "half sister by his father's second wife" is rejected.

**Documents:** r09, r17, r18, r24, r01, r03, r14, r23, r07 (Item 4), r13, r08, r06.

### Story 7 — Condemned, arbitrated, dismissed (1923–1934)

**Who:** Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector; Selby Vose; the Ostrey Hollow patrons; Alonzo Frick's board of three; Ansel Keddie.

**What happened.** The state issued burettes in 1921; Strawn skipped the Hollow's glass in 1922 ("the day being short") and on 12 June 1923 — his own dated entry, corroborated by the 28 June order and Ammon's 2 July reply; Vose's and Rundle's "12 July," Kyle's "'22" and Hazel's "'21" are memory — ran the pipettes against it: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them ... Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." The crate went up the ladder into the loft. Ammon regretted the trouble and could say nothing of the lot; new glass came in July (Orra: "new glass at the station and new pails, and the roof is still bad"), delivered its mark in October, and the 1924 average stood at 3.80 (Vose's 3.90 and Falke's 3.85 fall to Ansel's book, read by Strawn and Vaught). In the office it got "no more said than a broken window."

In January 1925 the patrons petitioned — $19,600 "to the dollar"; 67,000 lb by their reckoning — and a board of three under Frick, "the one of the three who kept no cow," sat from 17 March at the Association's office at Ashlin and then twice, in April, in the station weigh room, with the crate twenty feet overhead. Four exhibits: the test book, the receiving book, Emmet's weigh book (lent, wanted back), Strawn's 1923 notebook — "four books and nothing else"; nobody laid the second beside the fourth. Three explanations were argued: water (Vose, "without heat and without proof"), cream-taking (Teague: "Cream is portable"), wet hay (the 1908 report, read for an afternoon). Ansel's nine sentences: "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are." He was put out on 30 April — "Twenty-nine seasons and a letter"; he hung up his apron and ate his ham — four days before the award of 4 May: "finds no cause established ... thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account." One fifth of the $19,000 that account had accumulated. Vira's and Oram's "$3,500" fall to the minute book. A quick new tester was at the bench that fall.

**After.** Vose, 1926: "I will not have peace bought a second time." Ansel kept four or five cows, built the cow shed and whistled, turned carpenter at sixty-six for a quarter of the pay, and died at home in February 1934, with men at the burying who had not spoken to him in nine years. "A man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

**Documents:** r06, r07 (Items 7–10), r08, r13, r02, r03, r05, r23, r24, r21, r17, r18, r12, r20, r22, r01.

### Story 8 — Up the ladder: the crate, the bulletin, the books (1958–1972)

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (born 1931), Ansel's granddaughter, graduate student at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught; Rosalie Cudd; Roy Brant; the *Ashlin Chronicle*; Orin Falke; Halbert Nym.

**What happened.** In February 1958 Ivy climbed the ladder into the loft over the intake room and found the crate — AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET stenciled on the end — under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, where it had sat since June 1923 (Nym's "found in the Association office at Ashlin" is wrong against Ivy, Duncan, Hazel, Oram and Falke). Nine pipettes survived, two chipped. On 9 March at Farland she ran each five times against a certified burette: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — mean 16.72 c.c. against 17.6 blown; short 0.88 c.c.; "95 hundredths of the mark." She then brought five records into one room that had never met: the test book, Emmet's weigh book (borrowed from Roy), Strawn's 1923 notebook (in the college library since 1947, unasked-for for eleven years), Rosalie's sixteen seasons, and the ledgers; she also sat in Vira's kitchen and heard about the letter in the drawer.

Bulletin No. 471, October 1958, under Vaught's and her names, concludes: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain." Vaught's own introduction carries a transposed mean (16.27), ratio 0.9244, shortfall 0.295, 118,000 lb, "1898," and "five dozen" — sound arithmetic on a wrong premise, which he himself flags as irreconcilable with 1924's 3.80.

**The retellings of the retelling.** The Chronicle (3 October 1958) made Ivy a great-niece, counted eleven pipettes at 16.27, priced the loss at $19,600 and the invoice at $77.39, and accused Ammon & Sons of knowingly selling short; Ivy's own words: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not." Falke's 1959 county report corrected "great-niece" and withdrew his wet-hay concession. Ivy read "What the Crate Held" to the Historical Society in 1959; Rundle's reprint added a footnote ("Eleven pipettes"). The test book and crate reached the Rooms in 1959, the weigh book in 1963, the Alder Corners book the Vessey County Society in 1970; Nym's 1972 accession notes swap the two keepers' names. The station had closed in 1946. Duncan, 1970: "the five books are now in three buildings and are none of them ours."

**Documents:** r04, r11, r07, r19, r22, r18, r17, r21, r10, r09, r12, r01, r24 (editor's note).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Derivation: Ansel (born 1861) married Orra Brant in 1889; their son Duncan was born 1894 (r18 "My father was Ansel Keddie"; Orra's diary has Duncan five years old in September 1899 and going to teach at Ashlin at seventeen in October 1911); Duncan's daughter Ivy was born 1931 (r18; the Chronicle's "twenty-seven" in October 1958 agrees). Ivy herself says "my grandfather" (r04); Nym, Roy ("Duncan's girl Ivy"), Hazel, Rundle and Oram concur. Pointing elsewhere: the Chronicle (r19) calls her his "great-niece," and Falke (r22) copied it — he says his report was prompted by the newspaper notice — then corrected himself on the page: "the granddaughter and not the great-niece." Two narrators, one source, and the source was "put together in a day and a half from a college press release and two telephone calls." The father's own account outranks it.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant were sister and brother; their children Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant were therefore first cousins; Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel are the children of first cousins, i.e. second cousins. Roy, Duncan, Hazel and Rundle's 1959 footnote all give it so; Duncan and Hazel each spell out the derivation. Vira and Oram say "first cousin once removed," which would need Ivy and Roy to stand a generation apart from where they do; both are outsiders repeating valley talk ("that is the way of it"). Left open by the sources: whether Orra and Emmet were full siblings (Roy, Duncan) or half-siblings by their father's second wife (Hazel) — Roy explicitly rejects the stepmother story and is a generation nearer, so I take full siblings, but it does not change the cousin degree either way; Orra's birth year (1866 by her own diary and the editor, 1868 by Duncan, who also makes her 82 at death in 1948, which gives 1866); Emmet's wife and Roy's wife are never named; nothing is said of Ivy's mother.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose**, the Association's founder, first president 1889–1911, and author of the by-laws including Article VII. Selby says so himself in the 1926 address ("My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law"); Effie, secretary from 1901, says "son of the late president"; Duncan and Kyle — the clerk who sat outside Selby's door for twenty-five years and calls the nephew story "nonsense" — agree. Against: Vira and Rundle say "nephew," Rundle even quoting Selby on "his uncle"; but Rundle's pamphlet is by his own account "what the valley remembers, tidied," and Vira's is hearsay. A man's own statement of his parentage, in print, to a meeting that knew both men, outranks two memories. Why it matters: the rule that made the station's pipette the whole of every patron's pay was written by the father; the son signed every ledger sheet carrying the "station gain" line from 1913, told Teague in 1916 to let it alone, wrote and signed the 1919 water circular, argued water before the 1925 board "without heat and without proof," and in 1926 refused to have "peace bought a second time." An inherited office read as an inheritance — "the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little" — explains a manager who put his thumb on the total and signed, and why the water letter landed at Ostrey Hollow as "somebody's cousin saying it." His own Jerseys, tested at the home place at 4.56, never passed through the short glass.

**B4. None is established.** Vira says Orra was "first cousin of old Mrs. Vose" — something "everybody up here knew and nobody wrote down anywhere"; Oram says the Keddies and Voses "were cousins of some sort," from his grandmother, and "could not draw you the line on paper." Against them Duncan, Orra's son, writing to set the family right: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses"; people confuse them with the Ashlin Voses; his mother, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never put a Vose in it. Orra's diary, which names Loomises, Griggs and Brants, never mentions a Vose. I cannot prove a negative from these sources, but the only first-hand family witness denies it and both affirmers concede there is no record. The relationship should be treated as valley talk — talk that mattered, because it made the 1919 circular feel like a cousin's accusation.

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
(a) The pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 c.c.; Ivy's sheet of 9 March 1958 gives nine pipettes, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 → mean 16.72 c.c. Short 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.; 0.88 ÷ 17.6 = 0.05; 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95. The test reads fat as a percentage of a charge assumed to be 17.6 c.c. into a bottle neck ruled for that charge, so a charge 95% of the mark gives a reading 95% of the truth: **the tests were low by one part in twenty (5%), proportionally.** Vaught's introduction (16.27, ratio 0.9244) and the Chronicle (copying the press release) are rejected: the nine listed values average 16.72, the conclusion Vaught signed says "one part in twenty," and 0.9244 would put the true test at 3.905, irreconcilable (as Vaught himself notes) with 3.80 read on three other glasses.
(b) Recorded average 3.61 (summary leaf: "3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year"). True = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**; short by **0.19 points**. Confirmed independently: 3.80 in 1896 on Sill's glass, 3.80 in the nine weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure, 3.80 in 1924 on new glass, 3.796 for the four Bottom men at Alder Corners 1915–30, 3.78 at Larrow Green. The shortfall was proportional, not fixed: a 4.56 reading was truly 4.80 (short 0.24), a 3.42 reading truly 3.60 (short 0.18).
(c) 25 full delivery years 1898–1922, 40,000,000 lb milk: 40,000,000 × (0.0380 − 0.0361) = 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb**. Check: the 1898 ledger gain 3,040 lb × 25 years = 76,000.
(d) At the 25-cent average butterfat price (Effie, Ivy, Roy, Kyle — the figure got out from the annual sheets for the arbitration): 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons received the $3,800 award: 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **0.20, one fifth**. (Sill's and Kepp's 27 cents would give $20,520 and 18.5%; rejected as memory against the office's computed average.)

**C2.** Emmet Brant, 1910 leaf: 200,000 lb, paid on 3.42; butterfat 24 cents that year (Roy).
(a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
(b) True test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60; true fat 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb; owed and not credited **360 lb** (= 6,840 ÷ 19).
(c) 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40** (at the 25-cent series average, $90.00).

**C3.** Same 25 years, 40,000,000 lb.
(a) Credited on the station test: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb** (ledger 57,760 × 25).
(b) Received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb** (60,800 × 25).
(c) Difference 76,000 lb. As a percentage **of the fat credited** (the ledger's base): 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%, the ledger's "five and a quarter."** As a percentage **of the fat delivered**: 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00%, the "one part in twenty" of the glass.** Teague ("of the fat that was actually in the cream") and Vaught ("of the butterfat actually delivered") both name the wrong base; Ivy and Nym have it right.

**C4.**
(a) 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **$77.93**, as the invoice states; the Chronicle's and Junia Ammon's "$77.39" transpose the digits of the document they both reproduce.
(b) "6 doz." = **72 pipettes** (72 × $0.35 = $25.20 confirms; five dozen would be $21.00). Each short **0.88 c.c.** on the mean (individually 0.86 to 0.90).

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glass was in use from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923: 26 years and 42 days** (26 years, 1 month, 11 days), less the 63 days of 8 May–10 July 1911 on a borrowed measure — about 26 years less three weeks of actual use. The money runs over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**. They differ because 1897 (May–December) and 1923 (January–June) were part years on the short glass, and the ledger's station account is annual: only complete calendar years can be read from it (the summary leaf marks "1897, part year"). Inference: the two part-years, roughly 13.5 months, would add about 3,400 lb (~$850) that no source counts.
(b) **Cannot be determined from the sources.** Nym: "inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is none." Kyle: that season's milk was carried on the creamery's own books, the sheets never filed with the station papers, "not in any book I ever ruled." Two memories only: Sill, "about nine hundred thousand pounds," May to the end of 1896; Kyle, "a good eleven hundred thousand," from hearsay. Both are plausible against the later 1,600,000 lb full years (eight months ≈ 1,070,000 pro rata), and nothing chooses between them.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from 8 May 1911 ("Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure") to 10 July 1911 ("Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."): nine weeks, 63 days. Variable: the milk pipette alone — the station's own replaced by a borrowed one and then restored. Held constant: the patrons and their herds, the meadow and its hay, the weather and season across the switch-back on 10 July, the acid, the test bottles, the hand tester and its speed, the scales, the sampling jars, and the man and his method (Strawn watched him in June: "Nothing here to note"). Result: every Bottom farm's test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 the day the borrowed measure went in and fell to 3.61 the day the station's own came back. It establishes, as the 1958 burette cannot, that the station's measure was short *while it was in use*, in 1911, and that the whole 0.19 lay in it — a 1958 calculation shows what nine surviving pipettes delivered on a bench decades later, and could be answered by "they wore" or "the survivors are unrepresentative"; a step change and reversal on known dates in the working plant cannot. It does not establish: the calibration of the borrowed measure (nobody ran it against a burette; that it read true is inferred from 3.80's agreement with 1896, 1924 and Alder Corners); that the fault was constant across all twenty-six years (it samples one point); that all seventy-two pipettes were short; the cause (manufacture versus wear); whose measure it was; or, on its own, that nothing else was ever wrong — it shows the glass was sufficient, not that it was the only thing anybody could have suspected.

**D2.** Independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream:
1. *The Association's own ledger* (Teague, Kyle, Nym Item 6, Rundle's press note, Sill's receiving book): the cream reached Larrow Green *heavy* every year — 60,800 lb received against 57,760 credited in 1898, "station gain, as usual." Cream stolen at the Hollow would arrive light. Holds with no 1958 measurement at all — Rundle drew it in 1939.
2. *The station's own test book* (Ansel; Vaught §IV; Falke ¶8): in the nine weeks of 1911 the same man, alone, with the same cans, produced tests 0.19 higher on a borrowed measure and 0.19 lower again on his own. A thief does not raise his figures for nine weeks and mark the fall with a question mark. Holds without 1958.
3. *A Vessey County cheese book* (Rosalie Cudd; Roy): four of the same men's summer milk tested 3.78–3.82 over the ridge for sixteen seasons on Jerome Cudd's own new glass, and made cheese that never showed water — the fat was in the milk when it left the farms, and the home deficit matches the true test exactly. Holds without 1958.
4. *The state's circuit books* (Strawn): an inspector "kin to nobody" watched the test through nearly every year from 1907 — "slow and clean," "I did not let him hurry it" — and the 1908 committee stood by while the test was made and found "no dishonesty of any kind." Holds without 1958.
5. *The shape of the error* (Ivy, Vaught): it was a proportion of the fat present, hitting Jerseys at 0.24 and a 3.42 herd at 0.18, identical in July and February, wet and dry. A man dipping cream takes a quantity; only a short measure takes a fraction. Partly inferable before 1958 from the 1910 leaf and the Jerseys, fully only after.
6. *The glass itself* (Ivy's sheet, 1958): nine pipettes 5% short account for the whole gap with nothing left over.
These are independent because they rest on different records kept by different hands with different interests in different buildings — the Association's ledger at the office, the tester's book at the station, a cheesemaker's book in another county, the state's notebooks, a farm weigh book — "no two of them had ever been in one room before last year" (Ivy). Reasons 1–4 stand if the crate had never been opened.
The bookkeeper: Teague wrote the "station gain, as usual" line for forty years, said in 1925 that "cream is portable," and never questioned the entry that refuted him. By his own account he put the station account to Vose once, in 1916, was told to let it alone, and "a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time ... has generally begun to look for another employer"; Kyle says he "never asked anybody anything in forty years." He called the figure "furniture" and himself "the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." So: no — at most one mention of the account as an oddity, never as evidence, and he argued the opposite before the board with his own ledger in the room.

**D3.** Yes — Selby Vose knew the station's tests ran low before 1923, and there is no room to doubt it: the complaint had been in the talk at every annual meeting since 1902 (Grigg), the 1908 committee reported on it to the board, Teague put the station account to him in 1916, Strawn told him in 1919 that no water had ever been found there, and he wrote and signed the 1919 circular precisely because the Hollow's tests were low and he believed watering explained it. His own 1926 address concedes "for a generation the station tested below Larrow Green." What he did not know — or did not read — was that the ledger sheets he signed each spring from 1913 showed the missing fat arriving at Larrow Green: "he read the totals and he never read the lines above them ... put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" (Kyle); "a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me" (Teague). He carried the water theory to the 1925 board "without heat and without proof" (Frick) and was still defending it in 1926. The sources therefore establish that he knew the fact of the low tests throughout, had the refuting evidence under his hand yearly and once in words (1916), and chose not to look; whether he ever grasped what the gain line meant is not established.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. The theory.** On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons of Tarnet shipped the Ostrey Hollow station six dozen milk pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. that delivered 16.72 c.c. — nineteen-twentieths of the mark, every one of them alike (Junia Ammon's guess of a reground mold is plausible and unproven). The Babcock-type test reads fat as a percentage of a charge the bottle's neck assumes to be 17.6 c.c.; a charge 5% short gives a reading 5% short, multiplicatively. Hence the size: 5% of the Bottom's true 3.80 is 0.19 — the same fraction, not the same number, for every herd, so 4.56 read 0.24 low and 3.42 read 0.18 low, and July read as short as February. It began on 1 May 1897, the day that glass replaced Sill's borrowed outfit (on which 1896 had read 3.80), and ended on 12 June 1923, when Strawn's burette condemned the lot and the July replacement read the mark (1924: 3.80). Nothing at the station or on the farms had to change, because nothing there was wrong: the scales were true to sealed weights, the acid and crank and bottles were checkable and checked, the man tested "slow and clean" before the state's own inspector — and the pipette "is the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else," the standard by which everything else was judged. The fat itself was never lost: it went down the valley in the cream and was booked each year as station gain, 5¼% "as usual." The one accident that could reveal it — nine weeks on another measure in 1911 — was recorded with a question mark and read by nobody.

**The three competing explanations.**
1. *Wet hay of Hessel Bottom* — Grigg's 1908 committee; Kepp's 1926 paper; Falke's first draft. Defeated by: Bulletin 214's own figures (≤0.05 in winter, nothing in summer) against a 0.19 that never varied by season; the three hill farms sharing the deficit; the same cows off the same meadow reading 3.80 at Alder Corners; the 1911 interval removing the gap with the hay unchanged; and Grigg's and Kepp's reliance on a misquoted "two tenths."
2. *Watered milk* — Selby Vose's 1919 circular and 1925 testimony; Sill; Vose again in 1926. Defeated by: Strawn's lactometer at every visit, "no added water, as always here," which he told Vose in 1919; the 1908 committee's inquiry into every barn's water; Rosalie's vats ("Cheese is a witness"), never a soft curd in sixteen summers; watered milk would make the cream arrive light, and it arrived heavy; and Vose's own inverted chronology — the rise of 1924 followed new glass, not a warning.
3. *The tester stealing cream* — Teague's insinuation before the board ("Cream is portable"); Vira's belief; Rundle's "settled opinion"; the cream hauler's conviction (Oram). Defeated by: the station-gain line Teague himself wrote — cream reached the Green heavy every year (Rundle saw this in 1939 and retracted); the 1911 interval, in which the supposed thief's own figures rose; Strawn's repeated observation of the test; the proportional shape of the error; and the crate.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No. Effie: the crate "was never mentioned in the hearing at all ... not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions." Frick's exhibit list is "four books and nothing else"; the appliances tried in the weigh room in April 1925 were the new glass in use since July 1923. The condemned crate sat in the loft twenty feet above sessions two and three (Duncan, Oram). The board did have Strawn's 1923 notebook — exhibit 4, with "These measures will not deliver their mark" in it — and nobody drew the inference (Kyle: no man "laid the second beside the fourth and looked").

**What the tester believed.** Publicly and in writing, nothing he could prove: "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are" (1925); "? — A.K." (1911); the unsent letter carries no charge against anyone. Duncan, who was in the kitchen, says that in the summer of 1911 his father came home saying "it was something in the glass," said it twice, and would not write it because he could not prove it against "the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county." Frick's 1946 recollection that Keddie privately "always thought the patrons were at fault" is uncorroborated and contradicted by Ansel's own unsent letter and by Roy's account of the family quarrel; Ivy's "he never knew" is a granddaughter's kindness, not testimony. Best resolution: he suspected the glass from 1911, could not prove it, and went to his grave — as Frick also says — accusing nobody.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** For twenty-six years (1897–1923) the Ostrey Hollow skimming station of the Larrow Valley creamery cooperative paid its patrons on butterfat tests one-twentieth too low, because the six dozen pipettes bought from Ammon & Sons in 1897 delivered 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 mark. The missing fat — 76,000 lb, $19,000 — went down to Larrow Green in the cream and was booked yearly as "station gain, 5¼%, as usual." The valley blamed wet hay (1908 committee, 1926 veterinarian), watered milk (the manager's 1919 circular), or the tester, Ansel Keddie, dismissed in 1925 while a board paid $3,800 "without admission." An inspector condemned the glass in 1923 without drawing the inference. In 1958 Keddie's granddaughter Ivy measured the crated pipettes. It took eight records — test book, ledgers, a farm weigh book, a cheese book over the ridge, the inspector's notebook, an invoice, a nine-week accident of 1911, a family — because each held one piece and none had ever been laid beside another.
