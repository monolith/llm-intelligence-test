# Answers — Part 1 (Section A, stories 1–4)

Written from memory after one sequential reading of r01–r24. Where I resolve a conflict I say how; where I infer I say so.

## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1. The rule and the station: origins, 1889–1897

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose was elected first president and held the office to 1911; he drafted the by-laws himself and died in 1917. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek in 1891. Article VII paid every patron by the pounds of butterfat found by test, not by weight of milk, and closed with the clause that turned everything afterward into money: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — no re-test, no appeal. Its adoption year cannot be fixed: Loomis says 1892, Rundle 1891, Grigg 1893; the printed by-laws say only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move. The long-serving officers were Effie Loomis (secretary 1901–40), Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper 1905–45), Hobart Sill (buttermaker 1893–1934) and Selby Vose, Gideon's son, manager 1913–38 (Kyle's 1911 is wrong; Strawn's 1913 entry records the new manager).

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow, serving Hessel Bottom (six farms — Loomis's seven is the sole dissent) and three hill places: nine patron families. Ansel Keddie (born 1861) went up in 1895 to ready it and opened it on 4 May 1896. The station's own outfit had not come, so Sill made one up from his own glass at Larrow Green; Keddie's first leaf reads "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes" (his later "Ashlin creamery" contradicts his own leaf and Sill, and I reject it). On that borrowed glass the 1896 average was 3.80.

Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet, invoiced the outfit on 14 April 1897: six dozen milk-test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at 35 cents ($25.20); twelve dozen 8-percent bottles at 22 cents ($31.68); one acid measure (90 cents); one four-bottle hand tester ($18.00); freight $2.15; total $77.93 (the column sums to 77.93, which defeats the $77.39 of the Chronicle and of Junia Ammon's prose). It went into use on 1 May 1897: Orra Keddie's diary entry of that day, the office file, the test book's "1897, part year," and Bulletin 471's conclusion outrank Vaught's body text and Kyle (1898) and Hazel Brant (1899). Sill's glass went back with one piece chipped. Those pipettes delivered 16.72 c.c., not 17.6. Junia Ammon's reconstruction is a reground mold running the lot consistently short — a fault a spot check of the first, tenth and fiftieth cannot find; the works books burned in 1931. The Chronicle's charge that the lot was sold short knowingly rests only on the firm's 1923 silence, and I do not accept it.

Documents: r02, r03, r07, r12, r14, r15, r20, r22, r24; also r06, r11, r17, r19, r23.

### Story 2. Twenty-five years of the book and the ledger, 1898–1922

Keddie tested the same way every working morning: cans weighed on a state-checked scale, a ten-day composite sample per patron, warmed and mixed, drawn to the pipette's mark, acid, four bottles in the hand tester, fat read in percent with dividers, five columns (date, patron, pounds, test, fat), errors struck and initialed, never overwritten. Emmet Brant weighed every can on his own scale first and the two scales agreed for thirty years: the weighing was never the fault.

From 1898 the station average was 3.61, "or within three hundredths of it every year" (summary leaf of 1922). Larrow Green ran 3.78 (Sill; Falke; Nym's "a little under two tenths better"). The true station figure was 3.80, so the gap against the truth was 0.19; Roy Brant's 0.17 is the gap against Larrow Green and is not wrong. It was 0.19 in July and in February, wet year and dry. Complaints began in 1902.

Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the station took 40,000,000 lb of milk, 1,600,000 a year (Teague's annual sheets, added twice by Loomis; Kyle got the figure out for the arbitration). Teague's 44,000,000 covers "the years the account ran," a longer span; Rundle's 44,000,000 "over the years complained of" is a borrowed error. Butterfat averaged 25 cents (24 cents in 1910 per Brant's book); Sill's and Kepp's 27 cents I set aside as an unsourced round figure.

The excess fat did not vanish. Every year Teague entered: "butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." Sill weighed that cream into his receiving book himself and it "ran heavy" every year. Kyle copied the line for twenty-five years. Selby Vose signed each sheet by the bottom total. Teague says he put it to Vose once in 1916 and was told to let it alone; Kyle says Teague never asked anybody anything — unresolvable. Over the twenty-five years: 1,444,000 lb credited, 1,520,000 lb received, 76,000 lb gain — five and a quarter percent of the credited fat, five percent of the delivered (Teague and Vaught's body both misstate the denominator). Worth $19,000. Because the error was proportional it took most from the richest milk: Brant's 1910, 200,000 lb paid on 3.42, was truly 3.60. Nobody from the office ever stood in the station on a working morning (Loomis). A figure with a name on it "is furniture."

Documents: r02, r03, r05, r07, r09, r14, r23; also r04, r11, r12, r17, r22.

### Story 3. The nine weeks of 1911

On 8 May 1911 the station's pipette broke — Orra Keddie's diary that evening has "A. home cross about a broken measure" — and Keddie wrote "Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." From that morning the station average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and stayed there. On 10 July the station's own measure was back in use and Keddie wrote "Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." The rise and fall showed on every farm on the Bottom; Emmet Brant entered both in his weigh book and could make nothing of it. The interval is 63 days: nine weeks exactly. Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" are wrong against the dated entries. Teague booked only a small replacement charge.

Where the borrowed measure came from is not settled. Ivy Keddie says it came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon; Roy Brant says the state man left his own in June — but Strawn's own book puts his 1911 visit in June, after the 8 May break, and records nothing left behind ("Nothing here to note"), so Roy's version fails on chronology. What can be said is that the borrowed measure gave the same 3.80 as the borrowed Larrow Green glass of 1896 and the new glass after 1923.

Keddie let the question mark stand for fourteen years and would not "put anything in its place that I do not know." His son Duncan, then seventeen, remembers him coming home that summer saying twice that "it was something in the glass," and never daring to write it, since against a suspicion stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. The same year the College published Bulletin 214 on bog hay, and nobody laid the two beside each other. In 1926 Kepp waved the interval away as six summer weeks that "would prove anything you liked." In 1958 Vaught called it the experiment that ran itself — one variable changed, herds, ground, season, acid, bottles and man unchanged — and lay unread for forty-seven years; Falke's 1959 report treats it as decisive.

Documents: r03, r04, r05, r06, r09, r11, r16, r18, r22, r24.

### Story 4. The hay theory, 1902–1926 (withdrawn 1959)

The first men to say aloud, in 1902, that Hollow milk tested under the rest of the valley were laughed at; Emmet Brant was one of them, and Orra Keddie's diary records the talk at the store that March. By the winter of 1907–08 the board could not let it alone, and in January 1908 named a committee of three under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, a man who hauled to the Green and had no stake in the answer. They came up in Grigg's sleigh, went to Emmet Brant's first, visited each of the six farms, drew and sealed their own samples, tried the station scales and the patrons' scales, watched the test made, and inquired into the barn water. They wanted to see barns, not the station. The report — sampling "through January, February and March" — found no dishonesty anywhere, milk whole and scales honest, and gave the opinion that the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream and that the remedy lay with the land. Grigg's own 1919 covering letter claims they sampled every month of 1908; his report, Nym's catalogue and Orra's diary all say three winter months, and the report governs. Emmet read it twice, put it in the drawer, and said the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not.

In 1911 the State Agricultural College tested the claim (Bulletin 214): bog hay as the whole roughage depressed the fat test "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Grigg in 1919 and Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926 both quote it as "two tenths"; four documents quote the printed text and it is 0.05. Kepp's Farm Club paper argued sour, lime-poor ground, sedge hay, cows robbing their frames; he put the county's wet-bottom deficit at a fifth of a point and the Hollow gap at 0.21 — Selby Vose's figure, not the book's 0.19 — and prescribed drains, lime and mineral supplements at 27-cent fat. The theory reached the 1925 board as the third explanation, "blaming nobody in the room," and Falke's 1959 report first allowed it as a partial factor.

It fails on shape and size: the gap was flat across the seasons where a feed effect would vanish on summer pasture; 0.05 is four times too small; the same four herds off the same wet ground tested 3.80 over the ridge; the 1911 interval and the 1923 new glass moved the figures with no change to the land; and Vose's own Jerseys, on Larrow Valley ground, tested 4.56 in 1910. Falke withdrew his concession on reading Bulletin 214.

Documents: r15, r16, r07, r04, r11, r22, r24, r09, r10, r13, r08, r14, r02.
# Answers — Part 2 (Section A, stories 5–8; Section B)

### Story 5. Over the ridge: the Alder Corners Cheese Company, 1913–1930

Jerome Cudd built a cheese factory at Alder Corners, across Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, in 1913 — spring water on the lot, a hard road east; the valley remembers it as "opened in 1914" (Roy Brant, Rundle), which can be reconciled as built one year and running the next. The ridge road, four miles of lumber-road switchback, shuts from November to April; no one hauled over it in winter. From 1915 four of the six Hessel Bottom men hauled their milk over the ridge in the summer flush, about 1 June to mid-September, and back to the Hollow station the rest of the year, for sixteen seasons ending in 1930. Their reasons were price, a factory that buys everything in flush, and a shorter pull while the road was open. The valley called it going over to the enemy. Emmet and Roy Brant, with the biggest herd, stayed home — a decision Roy later thought cost them money.

Cudd tested on his own bench with his own glass and never compared a figure with anybody; the check on Ostrey Hollow was accidental. At the end of 1919 he wrote: "The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." His season averages for the four together run 3.78 to 3.82 every summer from 1915 to 1930, about 3.80, wet years and dry — nineteen hundredths above what the same cows were credited at home in the years of the short glass, and level with the station after 1923. Cheese is also a witness against water: watered milk gives a slack curd the next morning, and in sixteen summers Cudd never docked or complained of those men. (How many seasons of overlap Ivy Keddie counted — she says fourteen — cannot be reconciled with a station book that ends in 1924; I leave it open.)

Jerome Cudd died in March 1941 and his daughter Rosalie — daughter, by her own letter and Duncan Keddie; Ivy Keddie and the Chronicle wrongly say niece — took the factory that spring. In 1958 she copied the figures for Ivy at Farland; the book itself never left Vessey County and went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970.

Documents: r10, r09, r12, r04, r07, r17, r18, r19.

### Story 6. The water circular and the family, 1919 (and 1929, 1934, 1948)

The families first. Orra Brant, born 1866 (her diary and her age at death; Duncan's "1868" fails his own arithmetic), married Ansel Keddie in 1889; their son Duncan was born in 1894 and went to teach at Ashlin in 1911. Orra was full sister to Emmet Brant (1854–1929), the station's largest patron — Roy Brant explicitly rebuts the stepmother story Hazel repeats. The tester was married into the family the station cost most, and the two households ate Sunday dinner together for thirty-six years.

In June 1919 the manager, Selby Vose, sent a printed circular over his own name — Kyle typed it — to the nine patron families of the Hollow (Orra's "eleven" is the sole dissent), warning that added water would be looked for; it named nobody and said the Association "cannot pay for creek water." The state inspector, who had run the lactometer at every visit and never found water, told Vose so. Keddie would not mail the letters or leave them on the platform; he carried them round himself and stood at each door while it was read. Two days later Emmet stood in the Keddie yard, said he had hauled whole milk since the day the station opened and Ansel knew it, and asked why he had carried the paper if he did not believe it. Roy Brant's version — that the man who kept the book should have stood up against the circular and did not — is the same grievance from the other side. The two men never spoke again; Orra walked to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane. Grigg's committee report was reprinted that year to answer the reopened complaint; Sill thought the circular "the only plain thing that office ever did." Vira Toland's father read his twice with his hat on and kept it in a drawer till he died; Hazel Brant's mother put hers in the stove. The two ends of the valley did not speak civilly for six years.

Emmet's name was on the 1925 petition; Orra did not blame him. He died in the spring of 1929 with the ten years unmended. Ansel, dismissed in April 1925, kept a few cows and turned carpenter — "a carpenter at sixty-six" in 1927, a barn door that swung true thirty years — and died at home in February 1934; men who had not spoken to him in nine years shook Orra's hand and said what a hand he was with a door, and no one named the other thing. Orra died in 1948. Neither lived to be cleared.

Documents: r24, r01, r03, r06, r08, r09, r12, r14, r17, r18, r21, r23.

### Story 7. The burette and the board, 1921–1925

In 1921 the state began issuing calibrated burettes to its field men to check the delivery of pipettes; Merle Strawn, on the Ordell circuit since 1907 and kin to nobody in the valley, got his that spring. In 1922 he skipped the check at Ostrey Hollow, "the day being short." On 12 June 1923 he ran the station's pipettes against it: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." The date is his own dated entry; Vose's and Rundle's 12 July is impossible because the Association ordered replacements on 28 June and Ammon & Sons acknowledged on 2 July, declining to say anything of the old lot. The crate went up the ladder into the loft over the intake room. New glass was in use from July; in October it delivered its mark; the 1924 average, Keddie's last season, was 3.80 (test book, read by Strawn; Vose's 3.90 and Falke's 3.85 are wrong). Nobody connected the two things. The burette program was discontinued in 1926.

The patrons petitioned in January 1925. A board of three sat under Alonzo Frick, attorney, the one member who kept no cow: 17 March at the office in Ashlin, then two April sittings in the station's own weigh room, with the crate twenty feet above the board's heads. Four exhibits: the station test book (4 May 1896–31 December 1924, Keddie's hand), the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book from 1 January 1897, and Strawn's 1923 notebook — the entry condemning the glass was in the room, and the crate was never mentioned by anyone (Loomis took every word). Keddie made a nine-sentence statement: he had tested as taught and as the state man had seen him, could not say why the figures were what they were, and had never taken a pound of cream. Three explanations were argued: water (Vose), theft (Teague: "cream is portable," one man for twenty-nine seasons), hay (the 1908 report). The patrons reckoned their loss at 67,000 lb.

On 30 April 1925 the Association dismissed Keddie — "twenty-nine seasons and a letter"; he came home at his usual hour and ate his ham. On 4 May the board found "no cause established" and awarded $3,800, without admission, charged to the station account — a fifth of the $19,000 later computed. Vira Toland's and Cleve Oram's $3,500 are their fathers' memories against the written award. A quick new tester replaced him. Vose's 1926 address called the payment a generous settlement of an unproved claim and blamed water that "stopped when the looking began" — though the tests rose in July 1923, eighteen months before anyone petitioned.

Documents: r06, r07, r20, r03, r02, r13, r05, r08, r23, r21, r24, r18, r22, r12, r14.

### Story 8. The crate in the loft, 1958–1959

Ivy Keddie, born 1931, Duncan's daughter and Ansel's granddaughter, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, climbed the loft ladder in February 1958 and found, under a tarpaulin, a deal crate stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, packed in straw: the condemned glass, untouched since June 1923. Nine pipettes were unbroken (Rundle's footnote and the Chronicle say eleven; her measurement sheet lists nine). On 9 March 1958 she measured them against a calibrated burette, five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — mean 16.72 c.c. against stems blown 17.6, short 0.88 c.c., "95 hundredths of the mark."

For the first time five records were put in one room: the station test book (in the Association's office since 1925), Emmet Brant's weigh book (lent — not left — by Roy), the Alder Corners figures (copied by Rosalie Cudd), Strawn's circuit notebooks (at Farland's library since 1947, unasked-for for eleven years), and the Association's ledgers. Her arithmetic: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80; 40,000,000 lb at 3.61 and 3.80 percent gives 1,444,000 and 1,520,000 lb, 76,000 lb of fat unpaid, $19,000 at 25 cents; the award was a fifth. Bulletin 471, October 1958, under Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie, concluded: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain." Vaught's body text (16.27 c.c., five dozen, 1898, 118,000 lb) contradicts that conclusion and Ivy's sheet; the sheet and the arithmetic govern.

The Ashlin Chronicle of 3 October 1958 ("Old Ordell Wrong Righted") carried it with a day-and-a-half's errors — great-niece, eleven pipettes, 16.27, $19,600, $77.39 — and Ivy's line: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not." In 1959 she read "What the Crate Held" to the Ordell County Historical Society with the crate on the table; Falke's county report the same year recommended no action, all parties being dead. The test book and crate went to the Historical Rooms in 1959; the weigh book passed to Hazel at Roy's death in 1962 and to the Rooms in 1963; Cudd's book to Vessey County in 1970. The station had closed in 1946. Condemned to cause found: thirty-five years; dismissal to clearing: thirty-three; Keddie dead twenty-four.

Documents: r04, r07, r11, r19, r22, r18, r17, r09, r10, r12, r21, r01.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Duncan Keddie (born 1894) states he is Ansel and Orra's son and Ivy (born 1931) his daughter; Ivy opens her 1959 talk with the same chain; Roy Brant calls her "Duncan's girl"; Orra's diary has Duncan at five in 1899. Against this stand the Chronicle's "great-niece" and the first draft of Falke's report — but Falke corrected himself to granddaughter on inquiry, and the Chronicle was assembled in a day and a half from a press release, so the two are one source, and a wrong one.

**B2.** Second cousins. Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie were brother and sister; their children Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie were therefore first cousins; Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are the children of first cousins, which is the definition of second cousins. Roy, Duncan, Hazel and Rundle's 1959 footnote all say so; Vira Toland and Cleve Oram say "first cousin once removed," which would require a generation missing on one side, and both are repeating valley talk. Left open: whether Emmet and Orra were full siblings (Roy insists full; Hazel says half, by the father's second wife — half would make them half second cousins, same degree); the mothers of Hazel and Ivy are never named; Hazel's birth year is not given (she was "small" in 1919, Ivy was born 1931 — a wide gap for the same generation, but not impossible).

**B3.** Gideon Vose, the founding president. Selby's own 1926 address says "my father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law"; Loomis calls him "son of the late president"; Duncan and Kyle say son and call the nephew story nonsense; only Vira Toland and Rundle say nephew. It matters because Gideon wrote Article VII — the station's test governs, with no re-test or appeal — so the son managed for twenty-five years under a rule his father wrote, defended the office and the rule rather than suspect the instrument, and was heard in the Hollow as a family that thought the valley belonged to it; Gideon died in 1917 and could not be asked what the clause meant. Selby's home Jerseys, on valley ground, tested 4.56 in 1910 — evidence against the hay theory he never applied to the station.

**B4.** None that the sources establish. Vira Toland says Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Gideon Vose were first cousins, "everybody knew and nobody wrote down"; Oram says the Keddies and Voses were "cousins of some sort," from his grandmother, and could not draw the line. Duncan Keddie, Orra's son, says there was no connection, that people confuse the Ashlin Voses with Gideon's family, and that his mother, who gave the connection of every headstone, never put a Vose in it. Two hearsay affirmations against the best-placed witness's denial: I hold there was no relationship, while noting the negative cannot be proven from these papers.
# Answers — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
(a) Nine surviving pipettes, blown 17.6 c.c., delivered 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 (mean of five runs each). Sum 150.48 ÷ 9 = 16.72 c.c. Ratio 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95. The bottle neck is ruled for a 17.6 charge, so a 95% charge yields a 95% reading: every test read 95 hundredths of the truth — low by one part in twenty. (Vaught's body 16.27/0.9244 and the Chronicle's 16.27 fail the arithmetic of Ivy's own readings and Vaught's own conclusion "one part in twenty.")
(b) Recorded average 3.61 (summary leaf, 1898 onward). True average 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80. Short 0.19 of a point — 5% of 3.80; proportional, so a 3.42 herd was short 0.18 and a 4.56 herd 0.24.
(c) 40,000,000 lb of milk over the 25 full delivery years 1898–1922. Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb. Delivered: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb. Not credited: 76,000 lb.
(d) 76,000 × $0.25 = $19,000. The patrons received $3,800 = 20%, one-fifth.

**C2.** Emmet Brant, 1910: 200,000 lb paid on 3.42.
(a) 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 lb credited.
(b) True test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60; 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb; owed and not credited 360 lb (5% of 7,200).
(c) Fat was 24 cents in 1910 (Roy Brant's book): 360 × $0.24 = $86.40. (At the period average of 25 cents, $90.00.)

**C3.** Same 25 years, 1898–1922.
(a) 1,444,000 lb credited to patrons (25 × the annual 57,760).
(b) 1,520,000 lb received at Larrow Green in the station's cream (25 × 60,800).
(c) Difference 76,000 lb. As a percentage of the fat *credited* (1,444,000): 5.26% — the ledger's "five and a quarter per cent." As a percentage of the fat *received/delivered* (1,520,000): 5.00% — which is also the glass. Teague and Vaught's body text both say five and a quarter "of the fat actually in the cream," which is the wrong denominator; 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 0.05 exactly.

**C4.**
(a) $77.93 (25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15).
(b) Six dozen = 72 pipettes at 35 cents ($25.20 checks). Each short 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c. — inferred for the lot from the nine survivors (range 16.70–16.74), on Junia Ammon's account of a consistently short lot.

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glass was in use from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923: 26 years, 1 month and 11 days (about 26 years and six weeks), less the nine weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure. The money calculation runs over 25 full delivery years, 1898–1922. They differ because 1897 (May–December) and 1923 (January–June) were partial years on the short glass and the calculation uses only whole years; the 1896 season was on borrowed true glass and has no intake figure anyway.
(b) Cannot be determined. The curator says the 1896 intake (4 May to year-end) is in no ledger and nowhere else; Sill recalls "about nine hundred thousand," Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand," both from memory, and they disagree by 200,000 lb with no document to break the tie.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** 8 May to 10 July 1911, sixty-three days, nine weeks. Variable: the milk pipette — the station's own broken, a borrowed one substituted (probably from Larrow Green; source not settled). Held constant: patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, hand tester, the man at the crank and his habits, the scales, the book. Result: the station average went from 3.61 to 3.80 the morning the substitution began and fell back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned, on every farm. What it establishes that a calculation cannot: the calculation only shows the figures are *consistent* with a 5% short pipette; the interval shows that changing the pipette alone changes the reading, with the men, cows and land untouched — which disposes of water, theft and hay in one stroke and clears the tester by his own hand. What it does not establish: which of the two measures was correct (only that they differed — it takes the burette to prove the station's was short and not the borrowed one long); the exact magnitude over other years; that the error was constant for all twenty-six years (the 1896 and post-1923 figures supply that, not the interval); and it covers only summer, so on its own it does not answer a strictly seasonal objection — the year-round flatness of the gap does.

**D2.** Independent reasons Keddie cannot have been stealing cream:
1. The ledger and receiving book (Teague, Sill, Kyle, Rundle's press note): more fat reached Larrow Green in the station's cream than the station credited, every year, "as usual." Stolen cream would arrive light; it arrived heavy. This rests on the creamery's own weighing at the other end of the road.
2. The 1911 interval (Keddie's book): the same man, alone in the same room, produced 3.80 for nine weeks when only the measure changed, and 3.61 when it came back. A thief does not raise his own figures when a borrowed pipette arrives.
3. Timing (Strawn's 1924 entry; Keddie's letter): the tests rose to 3.80 from July 1923 with the new glass, while Keddie still tested through all of 1924 — two seasons before his dismissal. Vira Toland's "the numbers went right the summer after they put him out" has the order backwards.
4. Shape (Ivy Keddie, Vaught): the loss was a fixed fraction — rich milk lost 0.24, poor milk 0.18 — and exactly even for a quarter century. A man taking cream takes a quantity, not a percentage, and cannot hold it to a hundredth for twenty-six years.
5. Observation (Strawn, Frick): the inspector watched him test in 1907, 1911 and later, found scales true and the man slow and clean; two scales, his and Emmet Brant's, never disagreed; Frick, who watched the test twice, believed him.
They are independent because they rest on different records kept by different hands — the creamery's scales, the tester's own book, the inspector's book, the arithmetic of the ratio, and eyewitnesses — and no one of them borrows from another. Reasons 1, 2, 3 and 5 would all stand if the crate had never been measured in 1958; reason 1 alone, in the Association's own ledger, was enough in 1925. Did Teague ever question his own entries? No. He says evenness "stops him looking" and that a figure at five and a quarter every year "is furniture"; he carried the gain to the general account for forty years; he raised it once with Vose in 1916 as a bookkeeping matter and let it alone; at the hearing he suggested theft without noticing that his own line proved the opposite. Kyle: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years."

**D3.** That the station's tests ran low, yes — beyond doubt, and by his own admission: his 1926 address says that "for a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green," and he sent the 1919 circular precisely because his figures had gone "one way for twenty years." Whether he knew his own ledger showed the missing fat arriving at Larrow Green is less certain. Teague says he laid the sheet before Vose every spring and Vose signed it, and that he put the matter to him in 1916 and was told to let it alone — which, if true, means Vose was told and chose not to look. Kyle, who watched him sign a thousand times, says he read the bottom total and never the lines above. The two are compatible: a man told once in 1916 who reads only totals. What he never knew was the cause; his 1926 speech still blamed water and called the condemnation "housekeeping," and the state inspector had told him in 1919 he had never found water. So: knew the tests were low, was told the gain existed, did not understand what it meant, and did not want to.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The single cause: the lot of milk pipettes Ammon & Sons shipped on the invoice of 14 April 1897 was blown short — probably a reground mold — and delivered 16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6. A test bottle's neck is ruled for the full charge, so every test read exactly 95% of the truth. That fixes the size: 16.72/17.6 = 0.95, one part in twenty, 0.19 off a true 3.80; and the shape: proportional, so herds ranked correctly against each other, the loss was largest on the richest milk, and it was the same in July and February, wet year and dry, because the pipette does not know the season. It began on 1 May 1897, the day the outfit replaced Sill's borrowed (true) glass — the 1896 average had been 3.80 — and it ended on 12 June 1923, when Strawn's burette, issued only in 1921 and first applied in 1923, showed the measures would not deliver their mark; new glass from July 1923 gave 3.80 in 1924. Nothing at the station or on the farms had to change for either, because nothing at the station or on the farms was ever the variable — the 1911 nine weeks prove it by substitution. The error was invisible from inside because the pipette *is* the plant's standard; and the missing fat was never missing — it rode down the valley in the cream and was booked every year as "station gain," 5¼% of the credited fat, 5% of the delivered.

The three competing explanations:
1. Water in the cans — Selby Vose (1919 circular; 1926 address), echoed by Sill. Defeated by Strawn's lactometer at every visit from 1907, never finding water, and telling Vose so in 1919; by Cudd's vats, which would have shown watered milk next morning and never did; by the constancy of the gap where a dodge would vary; by the 1911 interval; and by chronology — the rise came with the new glass in 1923, before the petition, and years after the circular.
2. The tester skimming cream and writing low — Teague at the hearing; the valley's "settled opinion" (Rundle, Vira Toland, Oram's father). Defeated by Teague's own ledger and Sill's receiving book: the cream arrived heavy, not light; by the 1911 rise under the same man; by the rise of 1923–24 while Keddie was still testing; by the proportional shape; by Strawn's and Frick's observation of his work.
3. Wet hay of Hessel Bottom — Grigg's 1908 committee, Kepp 1926, Falke's first draft. Defeated by Bulletin 214 (0.05 in winter, nothing in summer, against 0.19 all year); by the flat seasonal profile; by the same cows testing 3.80 at Alder Corners in summer; by the 1911 interval and the 1923 glass moving the figures with the land unchanged; by Vose's own 4.56 Jerseys on valley ground.

Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware? No. It examined the station's appliances *in use* in 1925 — the new glass of July 1923 — and saw the test made with them; it had Strawn's 1923 notebook as its fourth exhibit, with the condemnation entry in it, and two of its three sittings sat in the weigh room beneath the crate; but the crate was never mentioned by patron, officer or board (Loomis took every word), and "books and appliances of the station" in the award is a form of words.

What the tester believed: not settled. His own statement and letter say he could not tell why the figures were what they were, and he refused to write anything in place of his 1911 question mark. His son remembers him saying twice that summer that "it was something in the glass," never written for want of proof — consistent with the question mark and with his own insistence that he knew what came into the room. Frick says Keddie told him privately in the corridor that he thought the patrons were at fault — which contradicts Keddie's own written words. I weight Keddie's own papers and Duncan's memory: he suspected the glass and would not say so without proof; the corridor remark cannot be reconciled and cannot be checked.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** In 1889 the Larrow Valley farmers formed a cooperative creamery whose by-law paid each patron on the butterfat test of the station where he delivered. Their Ostrey Hollow skimming station opened in May 1896, and in May 1897 got its own testing glass from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet. The pipettes delivered 16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6 — five percent short — so for twenty-six years every test read 95 percent of the truth: 3.61 instead of 3.80. The missing fat reached the creamery anyway and was booked, unread, as "station gain." The valley blamed wet hay, watered cans, and the tester, Ansel Keddie, dismissed in 1925 after an arbitration that found no cause and paid $3,800 of a $19,000 loss. An inspector's burette had condemned the glass in 1923; no one connected it. In 1958 Keddie's granddaughter Ivy found the crate, measured the pipettes, and Bulletin 471 cleared him. The answer lay split across five books nobody had put in one room.
