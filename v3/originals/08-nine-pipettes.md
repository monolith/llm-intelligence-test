# 8. Nine Pipettes

Ivy Keddie was born in 1931, three years before her grandfather died, and she has no memory of
him. What she has instead is the fact that her father Duncan, who taught school at Ashlin for
forty years, could not be got to talk about him, and that when the subject arose at a family
table somebody changed it. She learned the outline of the business at about fourteen, in the
usual way, from a cousin.

She went to the State Agricultural College at Farland and stayed for a master's degree in dairy
husbandry under Leland Vaught, and in the fall of 1957 she asked him whether a graduate problem
could be made out of a creamery case in her own county. Vaught, who had spent his career
telling students that the interesting problems in the field were all in somebody's attic, said
yes.

She began, correctly, with the arithmetic she could not yet do, and with the books she could
get. There were more of them than anyone had supposed, because nothing in Ordell County is ever
thrown away; the difficulty had never been that the records were lost, but that no two of them
had ever been in the same room.

**The station test book.** Twenty-nine seasons in her grandfather's hand, impounded in 1925 and
sent to the county historical rooms in 1959, but available to her at the Association office in
1958. From it: the 1896 average of 3.80 on borrowed glass; 3.61 from 1898 to 1922, flat as a
table; 3.80 again from 1924; and the nine weeks of 1911, with the question mark.

**Emmet Brant's weigh book.** Lent by Roy Brant, who was seventy and still farming. From it:
the station's scale and a patron's own scale agreeing to the pound for forty-eight years, which
kills the watering theory dead on its own; and the 1910 leaf — two hundred thousand pounds even,
paid on a test of 3.42.

**The Alder Corners test book.** Rosalie Cudd copied sixteen seasons out by hand and mailed
them. From it: the same four men, off the same meadow, in the same summers, testing about
nineteen hundredths higher over a ridge in another county.

**Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks.** Twenty-eight of them, in her own college's library since
1947, eighty feet from her carrel. From the 1923 volume, six sentences of the twelfth of June.

**The Association's ledgers.** From them, the line she recognized as an answer before she had
proved it: *station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual.*

That was the winter. By February she could state the shape of the thing precisely — a
proportional shortfall of about one part in twenty, beginning in the year the station got its
own glassware and ending in the year that glassware was condemned, mirrored to the pound in the
Association's own books — and she could not prove it, because the glass was gone.

Except that in Ordell County nothing is ever thrown away.

She drove up to Ostrey Hollow in the last week of February 1958. The station had been closed
since 1946 and was used for storage by a man who kept his hay tools in it. She asked whether
there was anything in the loft. There was: forty years of it, and near the back wall, under a
tarpaulin, a wooden crate stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, with a paper label
still legible on the end.

Six dozen pipettes had gone into that crate in April 1897. Nine came out of it in February
1958, unbroken. She wrapped them in newspaper and drove them ninety miles.

On the ninth of March she took them to the college's laboratory and did the thing that nobody
in the Larrow Valley had been able to do in 1897, because there had not been a burette in the
county. She ran each pipette five times against a calibrated burette and wrote:

> *Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette,
> mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean
> 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of
> the bottle was ruled for.*

Everything else is division.

Sixteen point seven two over seventeen point six is nought point nine five, exactly. Every test
made with that glass read ninety-five percent of the truth. A recorded average of 3.61 is a true
average of 3.80 — 3.61 divided by 0.95 — and the shortfall is 0.19 of a point, which is five
percent of 3.80 and is not a constant but a proportion, which is why the Vose Jerseys lost
0.24 of a point where the Brant Holsteins lost 0.18.

Forty million pounds of milk passed the Ostrey Hollow intake in the twenty-five full years from
1898 to 1922, an average of a million six hundred thousand a year. Nought point nought nought
one nine of forty million pounds is **seventy-six thousand pounds of butterfat**. At the average
price of the period, twenty-five cents, that is **nineteen thousand dollars**. The award of 1925
was three thousand eight hundred, which is a fifth of it.

And the same seventy-six thousand pounds, the identical figure reached from the other end, is
the sum of the station gain entries in the Association's ledgers for those same twenty-five
years: butterfat credited to patrons on the station test, 1,444,000 lb; butterfat received at
Larrow Green in the station's cream, 1,520,000 lb; the difference, written down annually, given
a name, and carried to the general account.

Vaught read the draft and made her strike two adjectives and add the 1911 interval to the front
of the argument, on the ground that a controlled experiment is worth more than a calculation
even when the calculation is exact — nine weeks in which one object in the room was changed and
nothing else was, with the result recorded at the time, in the defendant's own hand, with a
question mark.

Bulletin No. 471 of the State Agricultural College was published in October 1958 under both
their names, and its closing paragraph is the one the county newspaper reprinted:

> *No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May,
> 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because
> its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds
> of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station
> gain.*

Thirty-five years after the glass was condemned. Thirty-three years after the dismissal.
Twenty-four years after the funeral.

The crate and its nine pipettes went to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1959, where they
are shown beside the test book. The label on the case gives the pipettes' marked capacity and
their measured capacity, in that order, and leaves the visitor to do the division.
