# 1. Article Seven

The Larrow Valley is seven miles of bottomland between Ashlin and the head of the creek, and
in 1889 it held forty-one farms that milked. What it did not hold was a buyer. A man with
twelve cows churned what his family could not drink, packed the rest in tubs, and hauled it
fourteen miles down to Ashlin, where a commission man looked at it, made a face, and named a
price that had nothing to do with the butter and everything to do with the hour of the
afternoon. Tub butter from forty-one kitchens is forty-one different articles. Nobody could
build a reputation on it and nobody could get a fair figure for it.

Gideon Vose was forty-four that year and farmed at the Green. He had been down to see a
creamery running in another county and had come home quiet, which those who knew him
understood to mean that he had made up his mind about something. In the fall he got
twenty-six men into the schoolhouse at Larrow Green and put it to them: one churn for the
valley, owned by the men who filled it, the butter sold under one name, and the money divided
by rule instead of by argument. They talked until eleven and organized the Larrow Valley
Cooperative Creamery Association before they went home. Vose was elected president, which
surprised nobody, and held the office for twenty-two years, until 1911.

The creamery itself took until 1891. It was a plain frame building on the creek below the
mill dam, with a boiler, a separator, a churn, and a cold room dug back into the bank. Hobart
Sill came to it as buttermaker in 1893 at twenty-one dollars a month and stayed forty-one
years. From the first season the Association's butter went to the city in fifty-pound tubs
under a stenciled shield, and by the middle nineties it was fetching a cent and a half over
the average quotation, which was the whole argument for the thing, proved.

But the question that made the Association different from a butter factory was not the churn.
It was the question of what a patron was paid *for*.

The easy way — the way most valley creameries began, and the way the Larrow Valley began for
its first season or two — is to pay by weight. A man brings a hundred pounds of milk, he is
credited a hundred pounds, and at the end of the month the pool is divided by the pounds. It
is simple, it is transparent, and it is unjust, and everybody who milked knew it was unjust.
A hundred pounds of milk from a Jersey may carry five pounds of fat. A hundred pounds from a
big raw-boned Holstein cow, fed the same and worked the same, may carry three and a quarter.
Butter is made of fat. Paying by weight pays the man with the watery herd out of the pocket
of the man with the rich one, and it does it silently, month after month.

So Gideon Vose wrote Article VII.

> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of
> pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of
> the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Fifty-eight words. They were adopted at an annual meeting in the early nineties — the printed
by-laws say only "adopted at the annual meeting," with no year on their face, and the minute
book that would have fixed it was lost when the office moved in 1938, so the valley has never
agreed on whether it was 1891, 1892 or 1893. What is not in doubt is the text, and what is
not in doubt is the effect. From the day Article VII took hold, a man's income depended not on
his scales but on a number written in a book by another man looking at a glass tube.

It was the modern and the honest rule. Every serious creamery in the country came to it
within twenty years. It rewarded the man who bred up his herd and it stopped the slow theft
that payment by weight had been committing. Vose was proud of it to the end of his life, and
his obituary in 1917 led with it.

It also did something nobody in the schoolhouse considered, because there was no reason to
consider it. Under payment by weight, a scale that lies costs a man money and a scale is a
thing anyone can check with a known weight in his hand. Under Article VII, the instrument
that decides the money is a glass pipette the size of a pencil, and the way you check a
pipette is against a burette, and there was not a burette in the Larrow Valley.

The rest of the Association's machinery grew up around the rule. Effie Loomis became
secretary in 1901, at twenty-six, and kept the minute book in a hand so regular that
sixty years later a curator could read it across a room; she served until 1940. Wilbur
Teague came in as bookkeeper in 1905 and stayed forty years. He inherited a set of annual
ledgers already carrying, in the station account, a line he did not invent and never
questioned in writing:

> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received
> at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per
> cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*

*As usual.* It had been usual since 1898. Cream weighed and tested at the main creamery kept
arriving with more butterfat in it than the station's books said the Association had bought,
and the difference was written down, given a name, added to the general account, and carried
forward. A separator runs a little to the good, everyone knew; skimming stations gain; it is
in the nature of the trade. Nobody in the office ever set the gain beside the tests and asked
why one station in the valley gained and the other did not.

Gideon Vose died in 1917. His son Selby, who had been managing the Association since 1913 and
who milked Jerseys at the home farm, kept the by-laws framed in the office. He read the
totals of the ledger every year for twenty-five years and signed the sheets. Whether he ever
read the line above the totals is a question the Larrow Valley has argued about since, and
the argument has never had a document in it on either side.
