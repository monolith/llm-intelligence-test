# 4. Hessel Bottom

Hessel Bottom is a hundred and sixty acres of flat wet ground where the creek slows below
Ostrey Hollow, and it is the best grass and the worst reputation in Ordell County. It stays
green through August when the hill pastures burn off. It also floods twice most springs, grows
sedge along the sloughs, and cuts a coarse late hay that a hill farmer will tell you at any
length is not fit to feed a cow you care about. Six farms had rights on it. The largest of
them, sixty-odd acres and thirty-eight cows in a good year, was Emmet Brant's.

Brant was born in 1854 and was a suspicious man in the useful sense: he did not accuse anybody
of anything, and he wrote everything down. From the first of January, 1897 — before the new
glass went into the station, as it happens, though he had no way of knowing that mattered — he
kept his own weigh book. Every can, every day, by his own scale in his own barn, and the
station's figure beside it. Forty-eight years of it eventually, in two hands, his and his
son's.

The two scales agreed. That is the first thing the weigh book proves and it is worth stating
plainly, because it disposes of an argument that occupied the valley for twenty years. Nobody
at Ostrey Hollow was being cheated on weight. Brant's leaf for 1910 reads:

> *Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and
> theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42.*

Two hundred thousand pounds at a test of 3.42 is 6,840 pounds of butterfat, and 6,840 pounds
at the price of that year is what Emmet Brant was paid. What was actually in his cans was 3.60
— his herd was a good sound grade Holstein herd on the best grass in the county — and that is
7,200 pounds. He was three hundred and sixty pounds of butterfat short, that year, which at
twenty-four cents was eighty-six dollars and forty cents, which in 1910 was a hired man for
four months.

The complaints began in 1902 and they were never about weight. They were about the gap. A
Hessel Bottom man who hauled to Ostrey Hollow and a Larrow Green man of the same class of
herd, feeding the same, breeding from the same bulls, got paid on tests two tenths apart, and
they knew it because they met at the store and at church and compared their slips. Two tenths
of a point does not sound like an injury. On a hundred and sixty thousand pounds of milk it is
three hundred pounds of fat, and on the whole of the hamlet's production it was, though nobody
computed it until 1958, about three thousand pounds of butterfat a year going into somebody
else's account.

In 1908 the board sent up a committee under Nyle Grigg, a Larrow Green farmer of standing.
They sampled the Hessel Bottom herds through January, February and March, and reported:

> *We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is
> whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what
> thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station.*

It has to be said for Grigg's committee that it got two of its three findings exactly right,
and that its third was the received agricultural wisdom of the county and sounded like sense.
Wet ground, coarse hay, thin milk: every hill farmer in Ordell believed it and some of them
believe it yet. The report ended the matter officially for eleven years, and it fixed in the
valley's mind the idea that the fault lay in the Bottom itself — an idea that had the useful
property, for the Association, of costing the Association nothing.

Three things were wrong with it, and all three could have been known.

The first is that the report's own method chose its answer. The committee sampled January,
February and March — winter, on stored feed. Three years later the State Agricultural College
put bog hay to a controlled feeding trial and published the result in Bulletin No. 214:

> *Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five
> hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.*

Five hundredths, in winter, at the extreme. The Ostrey Hollow gap was nineteen hundredths, and
it was there in July as surely as in February. A feed effect is seasonal by its nature; cows
eat different things in different months. The station's shortfall was flat across all twelve
months of every year for twenty-six years, which is not how feed behaves and is exactly how a
fixed proportion behaves.

The second is that four of the Hessel Bottom men could have proved it, and did in effect prove
it, from 1915 onward, by hauling over Redlow Ridge in the summer flush and selling to a cheese
factory that tested them at 3.80 off the same grass.

The third is arithmetic that anyone in the office could have done. The other explanation that
the valley favored — and that Selby Vose put in writing in 1919 — was that the Hessel Bottom
men were adding water. A dilution large enough to carry 3.80 down to 3.61 is a dilution of one
part in twenty. Five percent of the station's intake was eighty thousand pounds a year. If
nine families had been pouring eighty thousand pounds of creek water into their cans annually,
the station's intake would have jumped by that amount in 1897 and dropped by it in 1923, and
Emmet Brant's own weigh book — his scale and the station's agreeing to the pound, for forty-eight
years — would have shown it in the first month. It shows nothing of the kind. The intake rose
and fell with the size of the herds and with nothing else.

What the accusation cost is not in any ledger. Nine families at the head of a small valley were
told on Association paper, over the manager's signature, that they were thieves; and the man
who wrote down the figures that made them look like thieves was married to the sister of the
biggest of them. Emmet Brant and Ansel Keddie were brothers-in-law and by all accounts they
had been friendly, and after 1919 they were not. Brant died in 1929 and Keddie in 1934, five
years apart, in a hamlet of nine houses, without settling it.

Roy Brant kept his father's book on after 1929 in the same ruled columns, and lent it in 1958
to a young woman from the college who had asked, very politely, whether anybody in the Hollow
had happened to keep their own weights.
