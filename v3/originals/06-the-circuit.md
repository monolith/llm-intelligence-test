# 6. The Circuit

The state put dairy inspectors into the field in the middle of the first decade of the century,
and Merle Strawn took the Ordell circuit in 1908 and held it for twenty-eight years, until
1936. He was thirty-nine when he started, a former creamery man from downstate, and he was
that rare and unglamorous thing, an official who did the job as written.

The circuit was fifty-odd plants in four counties. Strawn drove it with a horse until 1919 and
after that in a series of bad cars, and at every plant he did the same short list. He checked
the scales against sealed weights. He ran the lactometer on random samples for added water. He
looked at the boiler and the cold room. He watched the tester make a test through, from the
warming of the composite to the reading of the neck, and he wrote down whether the man knew
what he was doing. Then he wrote three or four lines in a pocket notebook and drove to the
next one.

He kept a notebook a year, twenty-eight of them, in a hand that got no larger and no smaller.
They are not literature. They are the most valuable single record in this history, because
Merle Strawn had nothing whatever at stake in the Larrow Valley. He was not a patron, not an
officer, not a neighbor, not kin to anybody in it, and he was not trying to prove anything. He
went to Ostrey Hollow between one and three times a year for fifteen years and wrote down what
he found, and what he found, year on year, was a clean station run by a competent man.

That is the first thing his notebooks establish, and it is the one nobody in the valley used.
From 1908 onward, Strawn ran the lactometer at Ostrey Hollow on every visit — that is thirty
or more separate occasions across fifteen years, unannounced, on random cans — and he never
once found added water. He was still finding no added water in 1919, the year Selby Vose put
his accusation on Association paper, and he found none in 1920, 1921 or 1922 either. The state
of Ordell County's most persistent theory about Hessel Bottom was being disproved, in writing,
by a state officer, twice a year, in a notebook in his coat pocket, throughout the entire
period in which the theory was believed.

The second thing they establish is the date.

On the twelfth of June, 1923, Strawn came up to Ostrey Hollow on his spring round. He had with
him, as he had for two seasons, a burette — the department had begun a program of checking
creamery glassware after a run of complaints in another part of the state, and had issued
burettes and a circular of instructions to its field men. It was a small initiative and it was
discontinued in 1926.

He set the station's pipettes against the burette one after another and wrote:

> *Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk
> pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot,
> the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not
> weigh what it has cost them.*

Six sentences, and every one of them is true, and the last one is the saddest sentence in the
record. He knew, in the ordinary technical sense, exactly what he had found. He did not know —
and had no way of knowing, and no duty to find out — that the lot he had just condemned had
been in daily use in that room since the first of May, 1897; that the entire difference between
Ostrey Hollow and every other station in his circuit was contained in it; or that nine families
at the head of the valley had spent a quarter of a century being called thieves on account of
it. He wrote no figure for the deficiency. He made no report beyond the condemnation itself.
He drove on to the next plant.

The crate — the original crate from Tarnet, with what was left of six dozen pipettes in it —
was carried up the stair and set in the loft over the intake room, because in a country
creamery nothing that cost money is ever thrown away. It sat there for thirty-five years.

The replacement order went to Tarnet within a fortnight, on the twenty-eighth of June. Ammon &
Sons answered on the second of July:

> *Your order of the 28th ultimo for six dozen milk pipettes and six dozen test bottles is
> entered and will go forward this month. We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are
> not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name.*

That letter is also, incidentally, the thing that fixes the date of the condemnation for anyone
who later gets it wrong. A replacement lot ordered on the twenty-eighth of June cannot have been
ordered in response to a condemnation in July. Whatever bridging arrangement carried the station
through the few weeks between the condemnation and the new glass is not recorded.

In 1924, the first full year on the new glass, the Ostrey Hollow station's average test was
**3.80**.

It had been 3.61 for twenty-five years and it was 3.80 now and it stayed at 3.80. Every patron
at the head of the valley saw his slips rise by about a nineteenth in the same season, and
every one of them drew the obvious and wrong conclusion: that something had been going on and
had now stopped. Nobody at all connected it with a line in a state inspector's pocket notebook
about glassware. Strawn himself did not connect it; he was not shown the station's averages and
would not have thought to ask for them. He came back through in 1924 and 1925 and wrote
"scales true, no added water, tests improved," and drove on.

This is the shape of the thing that makes the Ostrey Hollow case worth telling. The cure
arrived in June 1923 by pure administrative accident, from a program that ran three years and
was cancelled, in the hands of a man who was looking for nothing and found the answer and did
not know it was an answer. The diagnosis arrived thirty-five years later. In between sat an
arbitration that destroyed a man's name, a settlement of thirty-eight hundred dollars, and two
funerals.

Strawn retired in 1936 and died in 1946. His notebooks went to the state dairy office with the
rest of his effects, and in 1947, in a general clearing-out, the office sent twenty-eight
years of Ordell circuit notebooks to the library of the State Agricultural College at Farland,
where they were accessioned, shelved, and not opened by anybody for eleven years.
