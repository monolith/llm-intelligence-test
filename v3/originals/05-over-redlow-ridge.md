# 5. Over Redlow Ridge

Redlow Ridge runs north and south behind Ostrey Hollow and puts eleven hundred feet and a
county line between the head of the Larrow Valley and the hamlet of Alder Corners. The road
over it is four miles of switchback, impassable from November to April and merely unpleasant
the rest of the year. In every way that mattered to the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
Association, Alder Corners was a foreign country. It was in Vessey County. It read a different
newspaper. Its farmers were not members and never had been.

In 1914 Jerome Cudd built a cheese factory there.

Cudd was forty-eight, he had made cheese in three states, and he had picked Alder Corners
because it had grass, no buyer, and a railroad siding four miles down the far side. A cheese
factory wants milk in the summer flush, when there is more of it than any butter creamery can
take at a decent price, and it wants that milk whole. Cudd paid by test, like everybody, and
he tested his own milk with his own glassware on his own bench, because that is what a
cheesemaker does.

From the summer of 1915, four of the six Hessel Bottom men began hauling over the ridge in the
flush.

The arrangement was not a defection and nobody in the Association treated it as one. In June
and July a good Hessel Bottom herd made more milk than the family could haul profitably to a
station that was paying a butter price in a glutted market. Cudd paid a cheese price, which in
the flush was better. So from about the first of June to about the middle of September, four
men turned left at the top of the Hollow instead of right, and the rest of the year they
hauled to Ostrey Hollow as before. This went on for sixteen seasons, through 1930.

Jerome Cudd kept a test book, as any factory must. His entry for the season of 1919 reads:

> *The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground,
> whatever they say over the ridge.*

That last clause is the only sign in sixteen years that Cudd had any idea he was recording
anything remarkable. He had heard the Ordell County talk about Hessel Bottom hay — everyone
within twenty miles had heard it — and he thought it was nonsense, and he said so in his book
the way a man says a thing he considers settled and unimportant. He was not comparing his
figures with anybody's. He had no reason to. He did not know what those four men tested at
Ostrey Hollow in October, and he would not have cared: they were his summer patrons and his
figures were his own.

But the figures are these. Across sixteen overlapping seasons, 1915 through 1930, the same
four men, hauling the same milk off the same hundred and sixty acres of the same wet meadow,
tested at Alder Corners **about nineteen hundredths of a point higher** than they tested at
Ostrey Hollow. Not in one year. In every year. Season after season, in the two books, in two
hands, in two counties, kept by two men who never met and who had no business with one
another.

Consider what that comparison disposes of, if anyone had made it.

It disposes of the hay, completely and at a stroke. It is the same hay. It is more than the
same hay — it is the same *pasture*, in the same months, in the flush, which is precisely the
season in which the college's feeding trial found no effect from bog roughage at all. If the
Bottom thinned the cream, it would have thinned it on both sides of the ridge.

It disposes of the water, just as completely. Cudd was a cheesemaker, and a cheesemaker who
takes watered milk finds out within a day: the curd is soft, the yield per hundredweight
falls, and the vat tells him before any test does. Cudd took those four men's milk for sixteen
summers and never made a complaint. Watered milk that tests 3.61 in one building does not test
3.80 four miles away.

It disposes of the theft. Whatever else may be said about cream vanishing at Ostrey Hollow, no
account of it explains why the same men's milk read differently over a ridge in a factory
Ansel Keddie had never entered.

And it disposes of every version of the story in which the Hessel Bottom herds were simply
poor herds. They were not poor herds. They were 3.80 herds, and there was a book in Vessey
County that said so, every summer, for sixteen years.

The comparison was never made. There is no mystery about why. Nobody held both books. The
station's test book was Association property and lived at Ostrey Hollow; after 1925 it lived in
a safe at Ashlin. Cudd's book lived on a shelf in Vessey County. The four men who hauled both
ways certainly knew that they were paid better in the summer, but they were paid better in the
summer for four separate reasons — a cheese price, a flush market, a shorter haul in good
weather, and a better test — and only one of those four was a scandal, and it was the one no
farmer would think to isolate. A man does not compare the *test* on his June slip from one
buyer against the *test* on his November slip from another. He compares the money, and the
money was different for a dozen honest reasons.

So the most decisive single piece of evidence in the whole history sat in a shelf in the wrong
county for forty-three years, in the handwriting of a man who thought he was writing down
nothing at all.

Jerome Cudd died in 1941. His daughter Rosalie, who had grown up in the factory and had been
making its cheese since she was twenty-five, ran it from 1941 until it closed in 1962. In the
spring of 1958 she got a letter from a graduate student at the State Agricultural College
asking whether the factory had kept its test books from the years before 1930, and if so
whether it had bought milk from any Ordell County farmers, and if so which ones and what they
tested.

Rosalie Cudd copied out sixteen seasons of figures in a fair hand and mailed them, and added a
line at the bottom saying that she remembered the Hessel Bottom men coming over the ridge when
she was a girl, and that her father had always said the talk about that meadow was nonsense.
