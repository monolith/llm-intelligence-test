# 2. The Crate from Tarnet

Ostrey Hollow is seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, and in the nineties that was a
two-hour haul each way with a loaded wagon over a road that went to grease every March. The
nine farms at the head of the valley had joined the Association at its founding and had been
paying for their membership in horseflesh ever since. By 1895 they had made the argument
often enough that the board gave way: the Association would build them a skimming station.

A skimming station is a creamery with the interesting part left out. Milk is hauled in, weighed
at the intake, sampled, tested, and run through a separator. The cream — a tenth of the bulk,
carrying nearly all of the value — goes down the valley to the main creamery to be churned.
The skim goes back into the patron's own cans to feed his calves and his hogs. What the station
does that matters is the weighing and the testing, because under Article VII the test of the
station where a man delivers is the test that governs his pay.

The building went up over the winter of 1895–96: one room for the intake and the scale, one
for the separator and the boiler, a loft above for storage, and a lean-to for the wagons. The
board engaged Ansel Keddie as tester and separator man at thirty-five dollars a month. He was
thirty-four, he had farmed and failed at it, and he had spent two winters at a butter school
in another state, which in Ordell County in 1896 made him the most technically educated man
within seven miles.

The station opened on the fourth of May, 1896. The Association had not yet bought its glass,
so Keddie began with a Babcock outfit borrowed off the main creamery's bench — pipettes,
bottles and a hand tester that Hobart Sill could spare between churnings. On the first leaf of
a new ruled book Keddie wrote:

> *Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until
> our own comes. A. Keddie.*

He tested through that first season on Larrow Green's glass. The station's average test for
1896, all patrons together, was **3.80**.

The station's own outfit was ordered that winter from Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet,
a house that supplied half the creameries in the state out of a four-story building by the
railroad yard. The invoice is dated the fourteenth of April, 1897:

> *Sold to the Larrow Valley Co-op. Creamery Assn., for the Ostrey Hollow station:
> 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20
> 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68
> 1 acid measure — .90
> 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00
> Freight — 2.15
> Total — 77.93*

Six dozen pipettes is seventy-two of them, which sounds like a great many until you have
watched a man work a milk bench. They are thin-walled glass, they are handled a hundred times
a week with wet fingers, and they break. Seventy-two would see the station most of a working
lifetime, and in fact it very nearly did.

The crate came up the valley on a wagon, was unpacked in the intake room, and the new glass
went into use on the first of May, 1897. The borrowed outfit went back down to Larrow Green
with the next cream wagon. There is no drama in any of this and nobody recorded it as an
occasion; the invoice and a line in Keddie's book are the whole of the evidence that it
happened at all.

Here is what a milk pipette does, and it is worth being exact about it, because everything
that follows for the next twenty-six years is contained in it.

The Babcock test measures the fat in milk by volume, and it does so by a trick of design. A
fixed charge of milk is drawn into the pipette to the mark on its stem and blown into a test
bottle. Sulfuric acid is added, which digests everything in the milk except the fat. The
bottle is spun in the tester, and the freed fat rises into the graduated neck. The neck is not
graduated in cubic centimeters. It is graduated **in percent** — and it can be, because the
charge is fixed. Every mark on that neck means "this much fat, out of the standard charge."
The pipette is the constant on which the whole scale rests. Get the charge right and the neck
reads truth directly. Get the charge wrong and the neck reads a proportion of the truth, with
perfect precision, forever, and with nothing anywhere to indicate that it is doing so.

The pipettes in the crate from Tarnet had **17.6 c.c.** blown into the stem, as the invoice
says and as the standard requires. They delivered **16.72**.

Nobody in the Larrow Valley discovered this, and nobody could have. There was no burette in
the county, and no reason to want one. A pipette is not an instrument a working creamery
checks; it is a *definition*. Checking it would be like checking a foot rule against another
foot rule, except that the man in the Ostrey Hollow intake room did not own another foot rule.
The scale was checked twice a year against the county's sealed weights, and it was true. The
acid was bought by strength and tested by the state man. The tester was run to time and speed.
Everything in that room was verified against a standard except the one object that *was* the
standard.

The first full delivery year on the new glass was 1898. The station's average test, all
patrons together, was **3.61**.

It stayed there. From 1898 to 1922, twenty-five full years, the Ostrey Hollow average never
departed from 3.61 by more than three hundredths of a point, through good seasons and bad,
through drought and wet, through a generation of breeding-up that lifted the herds at the head
of the valley as much as it lifted any herds in the county — and every one of those
improvements showed up in the book at ninety-five percent of its size, which is to say that
they showed up, faithfully proportioned, and nobody could see that the whole picture had been
shrunk.

Down at Larrow Green, from that same year 1898, the cream from Ostrey Hollow began arriving
with more butterfat in it than the station's books said the Association had bought. Wilbur
Teague's predecessor wrote the difference into the ledger, called it station gain, and carried
it to the general account. It came to about five and a quarter percent, and it did that every
year for twenty-five years.

Two facts, in two books, in two buildings seven miles apart, saying the same thing in
different units, from the same spring, for a quarter of a century.
