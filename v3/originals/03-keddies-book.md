# 3. Keddie's Book

Ansel Keddie was born in 1861 and married Orra Brant in 1889, which is a fact of no apparent
importance and turns out to be one of the two or three most important facts in this history.
Orra was Emmet Brant's sister — the younger by a dozen years — and Emmet Brant farmed the
largest place on Hessel Bottom and was, from the day the Ostrey Hollow station opened, its
largest patron. Whether Orra was Emmet's full sister or a half sister by their father's second
wife is a thing the valley has said both ways and the Ostrey Hollow church register, which
would have settled it, burned in 1912.

Keddie went to the station in May of 1896 and kept its books for twenty-nine seasons, 1896
through 1924. He was a careful, unhurried, unsociable man who did the same work in the same
order every morning of his life. The intake began at half past five. Cans came off the wagons
onto the scale; the weight was called and written; a dipper of each patron's milk went into
his composite jar, which stood in the cool room and was made up over ten days. On testing days
Keddie took the jars to the bench in the corner by the window, warmed and mixed each one,
drew the charge into the pipette to the mark, blew it into the numbered test bottle, added the
acid from the measure, set the four bottles in the hand tester, and turned the crank at the
speed the school had taught him. Then he read the columns of fat off the necks with a pair of
dividers and wrote the figures in the book.

The book is a ruled ledger, foolscap, half-bound, and it is a beautiful object. Date, patron,
pounds, test, fat, running total. Twenty-nine seasons in one hand, with the hand getting
smaller and more crabbed toward the end and never once getting careless. There are corrections
in it — Keddie crossed a figure and initialed the correction rather than write over it, which
is what the butter school had taught him and which almost nobody in the trade actually did.
Sixty years later this habit is the reason anyone can trust the book at all.

At the back he kept a summary leaf, made up finally in 1922:

> *Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61
> or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted
> in their place, when it stood at 3.80.*

The nine weeks of 1911 are the strangest and finest thing in the whole record, and Keddie is
the only person who noticed them for forty-seven years.

On the eighth of May, 1911, he broke the station pipette. It happened at the bench, at the
start of a testing day, and he got a measure from somewhere — the book says only "a borrowed
measure," and the valley has told it two ways ever since, one that it came up from Larrow
Green with the cream wagon and one that the state inspector left his own. He wrote:

> *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*

For the next sixty-three days, nine weeks exactly, the station's tests ran at **3.80**. Every
patron, every jar, every testing day. The same cows were milked by the same men off the same
Hessel Bottom grass in the same warm weather; the same scale weighed them; the same acid
digested them; the same hand turned the same crank at the same speed; the same eyes read the
same necks. One object in that room had changed.

On the tenth of July a new pipette came up from Tarnet — from the same crate of 1897; there
were still five dozen of them in the loft — and Keddie unpacked it and used it, and the tests
came back to 3.61 that day and stayed there. He wrote:

> *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*

That question mark is the only thing in twenty-nine seasons that could be called an editorial
comment. Whatever he made of it, he put it nowhere else, and he never wrote about the cause of
anything again. The valley's two accounts of what he privately thought — his son's, that he
came home that summer saying it was something in the glass and never dared put it in the book,
and the arbitration clerk's, that Keddie told him he had always believed the patrons were at
fault — are single recollections of private speech, from interested men, thirty and forty
years after, and they contradict each other. The only thing that survives in Keddie's own hand
is the question mark.

What the years did to him is the ordinary thing. From 1902 there were complaints, first
grumbling and then formal, that Ostrey Hollow milk tested below Larrow Green milk. In 1908 a
committee came up and sampled the herds through January, February and March and reported that
the wet hay of Hessel Bottom was what thinned the cream, which satisfied the board and
satisfied nobody at the head of the valley. In 1919 the manager, Selby Vose, circulated a
warning about added water to the Ostrey Hollow patrons, over his own signature, on Association
paper — a letter that accused nine families of adulteration on no evidence at all and that
several of them kept for the rest of their lives. Keddie's position after 1919 was that of a
man whose neighbors believed he was either a fool or a party to something, with his wife's
brother among the accused.

He was called before the board of arbitration on the seventeenth of March, 1925, and made a
statement of nine sentences, of which two are remembered:

> *I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for
> twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are. I have never
> taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I
> have when he is by himself.*

He was dismissed on the thirtieth of April, 1925, without a finding against him and without
one for him. He kept a few cows and did some carpentry. Orra outlived him by fourteen years.
He died in 1934, and the imputation went into the ground with him and stayed there for
twenty-four years, until his granddaughter took it out.

The book was impounded by the board in March 1925 and never given back. It sat in the
Association's office at Ashlin for thirty-four years, and in 1959 it went to the county
historical rooms, where it is catalogued, correctly, in the name of the man who wrote it.
