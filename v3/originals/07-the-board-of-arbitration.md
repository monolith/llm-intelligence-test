# 7. The Board of Arbitration

The trouble did not begin with an accusation. It began with a raise nobody had asked for.

In the spring of 1924 the Ostrey Hollow patrons opened their pay slips and found their tests
up by about a nineteenth. A man who had been credited 3.61 for a quarter of a century was
credited 3.80. Nothing on his farm had changed. He had not bought a bull, or changed his feed,
or dried off a poor cow. The station had simply begun, in the middle of the previous summer,
to say a different thing about the same milk.

Farmers are not fools about this. Within a month the head of the valley had reached the
conclusion that any sensible person would reach: that something had been wrong before, that it
had now been put right, that nobody had told them, and that they had been paid short for
twenty-five years by somebody who knew it. In January 1925 nine families petitioned the
Association. The board, to its credit, did not stonewall; it agreed to a board of arbitration,
three members, sitting under Alonzo Frick, an Ashlin attorney of good local reputation and no
dairy knowledge whatever.

The board sat from the seventeenth of March to the fourth of May, 1925, in three sessions. The
first was at the Association office at Ashlin. The second and third were held at Ostrey Hollow
itself, in the station's weigh room, so that the members could see the plant. Above their heads,
through a plank ceiling and up a stair in the corner, sat the crate.

Three explanations were put to the board, and the board could not choose among them, and that
is the whole of what went wrong.

**The first was watering.** Selby Vose put it, as he had been putting it since his circular of
1919. His case was that the Hessel Bottom men had been adding water; that the practice had
stopped when it became clear the Association was looking; and that the 1924 rise was the
measure of it. It had a motive in it, it accounted for the numbers, and it had the great
practical advantage of making the Association the injured party. Against it there stood, unconsulted, fifteen years of a state inspector's lactometer
readings in a notebook in a state office; sixteen summers of a Vessey County cheese book;
Emmet Brant's weigh book, which the board did have; and a simple sum that nobody did — that a
dilution of one part in twenty means eighty thousand extra pounds of milk arriving at that
intake every year, appearing in 1897 and vanishing in 1923, which the receiving books flatly do
not show.

**The second was theft.** Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, put it — not as an accusation in
terms, but in the way a careful accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it. He
told the board that a station's figures reflect the man who keeps them; that Ostrey Hollow had
been in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons; and that cream is portable.
The board understood him. Ansel Keddie was called on the seventeenth of March and said his nine
sentences, of which two are remembered, and it is worth noticing that his own denial —
*there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself* — was accurate, and
did him no good at all.

The refutation of the theft theory was, at that moment, sitting in Wilbur Teague's own ledgers
in Teague's own hand, and Teague had written it out twenty times: *station gain, five and a
quarter percent, as usual.* A man stealing cream at a skimming station makes the cream arrive
at the main creamery **light**. That is what theft looks like in a ledger; there is no other
shape it can take. Ostrey Hollow cream arrived **heavy**, every year, by the same proportion,
for twenty-five years. The books did not merely fail to support the accusation. They contained
its exact and quantified contradiction, filed under a comforting name, by the man making the
accusation.

**The third was the hay.** Somebody produced the Grigg report of 1908, and it was read into the
record, and it did what it had done in 1908: it gave everyone in the room a way of believing
that the fault lay in the ground. It has to be said that in 1925 this was the theory with the
least evidence against it in the room and the most evidence against it in the world — Bulletin
No. 214 had been in print for fourteen years, in a library ninety miles away, saying five
hundredths in winter and nothing in summer.

The board heard the patrons and the officers. It had before it, by the minutes, the station
test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and Merle Strawn's notebook for 1923
— which contains the answer in six sentences and was, as far as anyone can now tell, read for
its scale and lactometer lines and not for its third. Whether anyone fetched the crate down
from the loft is not recorded and cannot now be established; the award recites only that the
board had before it "the books and appliances of the station," a phrase that will bear either
reading.

On the fourth of May, 1925, Effie Loomis recorded the finding:

> *The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and
> appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow
> patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged
> to the station account.*

Ansel Keddie had been dismissed four days earlier, on the thirtieth of April, without a finding
against him. The two facts are not connected in any document and were connected by everybody.

Two things about the award deserve to be said plainly, and neither was said in 1925.

The first is the size of it. The Ostrey Hollow patrons had been short **seventy-six thousand
pounds of butterfat** across the twenty-five full years from 1898 to 1922, worth about
**nineteen thousand dollars** at the average price of the period. They were awarded three
thousand eight hundred. That is **one fifth**. Nobody computed the true figure, because
computing it required a number — the ratio 16.72 to 17.6 — that did not exist in the world
until 1958.

The second is where the money came from. The award was "charged to the station account," which
is to say, to the account into which the station gain had been flowing since 1898. The
Association paid the Ostrey Hollow patrons a fifth of their own butterfat back to them out of a
fund that consisted entirely of their own butterfat, and recorded the transaction as a
generous settlement of a claim it did not admit.

Frick wrote a short memoir of the arbitration in 1946 in which he said the board had examined
every physical appliance of the station and satisfied itself. He also reproduced the exhibit
list, which names four books and no appliance at all.
