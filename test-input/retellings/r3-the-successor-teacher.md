# Retelling 3 — As told by the teacher who succeeded Aron Keel at the Marrow Bend school

I taught under Mr. Keel for six years and took the school when he retired in 1965 — he died the year after, and I'll say plainly there was no better-kept register in the county and no better-kept mind either.

The bell was his life's puzzle and he went at it the only way he knew, which was the way his grandmother had taught him — "count what you hear, write it down," he'd say, that was the old lady's saying, she'd been something at the weir herself in the building days, or so he once let slip. The tolling had been heard since the flooding itself — from 1907, the very first winters the water stood over Aldercote — and everyone said it was the drowned bell ringing for the dead. Mr. Keel kept his log seventeen winters and let the columns speak.

And what the columns said was this. Forty-three nights of tolling, and on every single one, two things true: the wind in the east, off the moor, and the water on the spillway gauge below fourteen feet. Never once otherwise. But — and he was scrupulous about the but — six nights had the east wind and the low water both and no tolling at all, so the wind and the water were needed but they were not the whole of it. Something more had to be true that he never found.

As for ringing for the dead: nine deaths in the village across his log, and just two of them within a day of a tolling night. Chance, pure chance, he'd say — you'd get the same from dice. The village never thanked him for that sum.

The wager, now, I had from his own mouth. First of November 1930, the gauge stood sixteen foot six, and he knew the winter draw took it down three inches a week. Anyone can do that sum — he named a day in January when the water would go under fourteen foot, and named it right, and bet the publican the bell would beat Candlemas. The east wind kept him waiting past his date, but on the twenty-first of January it came, and the bell with it, and he heard it himself that night for the first time — a slow rolled note, he said, like a bell remembering rather than rung. He took the guinea. The best winter for it — worst, I should say — was '33 into '34: seven nights.

Then the company men came in the spring of '46 and did their work on the gates, and it never rang again. He showed me the last page once: four nights the winter after with the wind and the water both agreeing, and nothing. "I have subtracted every wrong answer," he'd written, "the right one has not surfaced." It bothered him to the end, I believe, though he never reopened the book.

There was some sequel after he died — a museum woman from Harden came, the drought year, when the lake went right down and the old village stood out of the water, and did some digging about in Aldercote and took things away for the museum, and I'm told she made something of his log at last. What, exactly, I never heard rightly. I wish he'd lived for that.
