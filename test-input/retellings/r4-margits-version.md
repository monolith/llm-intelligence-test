# Retelling 4 — As told by Margit Brandt, née Voss

People get our family wrong in one telling out of two, so let me set it down straight while I still can.

My father was Tomas Voss, the carpenter — it was he who built the timber shutters for all five gates of the weir, whatever he thought of the thing, and what he thought of it he kept behind his teeth. The orchard at Aldercote was ours, the Voss orchard, back past anyone's counting; my father was born in it in 1877 and his sister Ilsa in 1880, and the two of them were the last children raised under those trees.

Ilsa — my aunt Ilsa — went to work for the very company that drowned us, and my father was years forgiving her, though he took their wage himself soon enough. She said someone had to watch them honestly, and the truth is she watched them so honestly they dismissed her for it. She was the one with the figures. The lake filled in a hundred and twenty days, just as the company promised — that part at least they got right — and my aunt kept the accounts of all of it in a green book she was never parted from.

She married the schoolmaster at Marrow Bend, Henrik Keel, in 1908, and Aron was born the year after, 1909 — her boy, her only one, and as like her in his ways as a son can be. When she died in '49 it was Aron who got her deed-box, the locked one, with the green book in it and the company's letter turning her out. He told me once, at her funeral, that he'd understood his mother about twenty years too late. He put the box up in the school attic with a label on it in that beautiful hand of his.

And then it came round to my Vera — my daughter, born in '41, the botanist. The summer the lake showed its floor — two years after Aron died, that summer — she went out onto the lakebed to the old orchard. Our orchard. The stumps were all standing, grey like glass, and she cut eleven of them for the rings, and the rings went back before Waterloo, which is just what my grandmother always claimed and nobody believed her.

Vera climbed the church tower too, and I'll tell you what she found, because it puts an end to sixty years of nonsense: nothing. No bell. The wheel and the timbers and no bell in it, because the bell had been sold out of the tower before ever the water came — sold for forty-six pounds to pay for moving the graves, it's in Aunt Ilsa's book. All those winters of the village crossing themselves over the drowned bell ringing, and the bell was forty miles away melted into I don't know what. My aunt knew it the whole time. She wrote it down and said nothing. That was her to the life.

Vera pulled some old iron out of the mud at the weir as well, off the bad gate, and got very quiet and very pleased about it, the way Ilsa used to. It's all in the museum now, the wood and the books and the iron together. Three of them it took — my aunt, then Aron, then my girl — and none of them lived to see more than their own piece of it, except Vera, who lived to see the whole.
