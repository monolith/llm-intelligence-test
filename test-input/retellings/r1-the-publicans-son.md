# Retelling 1 — As told by the publican's son at the Lamb

You want the bell? Everyone wants the bell. My father kept this house forty years and told it every winter, so I'll tell it as he told it, and he was there, mind.

When they drowned Aldercote — before my time, before my father's nearly — they left the church standing under the water, bell and all. Too heavy to shift, or unlucky to shift, one or the other. And that bell rings yet. Rang, I should say. On the hard winters, when the wind came off the moor — the east wind, the dry one — you'd hear it of a night, slow, like a funeral with no hurry on it. A hundred nights if it was one, over the years. It rang for the dead of the old village, that's what was always said, and I'll not laugh at it, because I heard it myself as a boy and it doesn't sound like anything a man would laugh at.

The weir itself, there was always something not right with it. One of the gates was known to be a bad one — the second gate, my father always said, jammed or hung crooked from the start, some botch in the building of it. There was a woman mixed up in the building too, kept the company's figures, an Aldercote woman at that, which they say is why the compensating money came through honest when it might not have.

The schoolteacher, though — Keel — that's the part my father never liked telling, because it cost him a guinea. Young Keel got it into his head the bell was no ghost, all figures and wind-vanes, and one winter — the '31 it was, January — he stood in this very room and bet my father the bell would ring before the month was out. Watched the water like a heron all winter, that man. And ring it did, the twenty-first, and my father paid up in front of the whole bar and said figures or no figures, it rang for whoever it pleased. They neither of them budged an inch till the day they died.

It stopped, of course. After the last war some company men came and did work at the weir, and the bell never rang again after. My father said the hammering disturbed it. The dead don't like hammering, he said. Make of that what you like.
