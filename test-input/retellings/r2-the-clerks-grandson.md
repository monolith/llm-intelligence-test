# Retelling 2 — As told by the grandson of a Selde Weir Company clerk

My grandfather clerked for the Selde Weir Company from the first survey to his retirement, and what I know I have from him, and he was a man who did not embroider.

The weir was five gates, each of twelve foot, and the lake behind it was reckoned at one thousand four hundred and forty million gallons. Now the famous story in the office was the filling sum. Corven — the chief engineer, Edmund Corven — had promised the shareholders the lake would fill in a hundred and twenty days. The company had a computer, a Miss Voss, a local woman, very exact, and she worked it properly: the river gave twelve million gallons a day in a wet winter, but two million of that was owed by law past the weir to the mills, so ten million a day was all you could keep, and ten into one thousand four hundred and forty is a hundred and forty-four days, not a hundred and twenty. She was right, near enough — it took a hundred and forty-seven in the event, my grandfather said, and not a man in the office dared say either number where Corven could hear.

There was a second matter with her, about the ironwork. When the trunnion castings came up, she gauged them all — that was her way, she gauged everything — and found the pin for the third gate wanting, a quarter of an inch under the specified size, and flawed in the casting besides. She took it to Corven and Corven overruled her; recasting meant six weeks and the six weeks weren't there. In the pin went. My grandfather always said the company got forty years' luck out of that pin and didn't deserve ten.

She was dismissed in 1906. The way my grandfather told it, it was the filling sum that did it — Corven couldn't forgive being out-arithmeticked by his own computer in front of the shareholders, and found a pretext by the autumn. She kept some book of her own, which the company called irregular, and perhaps it was, but here's the joke of it: in 1921 the office burned, every record the company had, gone in a night. After that, whatever that woman had carried out under her arm was the only true account of the Selde Weir in existence. My grandfather used to say the company spent fifteen years calling that book irregular and the next forty wishing they knew where it was.

Of the bell nonsense I know nothing and my grandfather would hear none of it. Office men, we were.
