# Retelling 5 — As told by the retired water bailiff

I had the bailiff's post from 1952, and my predecessor had it back into the twenties, and between what he told me and what the work itself teaches you, I'll give you the weir's side of the story, which is the side nobody asks for.

First: there was never anything ghostly about that noise, and the company men never thought there was. The old hands had a name for it — the weir singing. A gate knuckle knocking loose in an empty throat, that was my predecessor's phrase for it. You must understand how the thing is built: each gate sits over a culvert, and when the lake is drawn down past the culvert lip the water leaves it and the air comes in, and an empty culvert is a speaking-trumpet thirty feet long. Get a gate that's slack on its pins, give it a steady wind to rock it, and the knocking comes out the far end as a toll you'd swear was bronze. The lip of the low culvert sat at fourteen foot on the gauge — that's why the singing only ever came with low water. The old hands always reckoned it was the first gate, the one nearest the moor, that did the singing, it being the one that took the wind.

Second: people will tell you the noise was heard from the first flooding. Not so. There was no singing before the war-time draws — first complaints came the winter of '18, and the reason is no mystery to a bailiff: it wasn't until the mills' war demand that the winter draw-down ever took the gauge below fourteen foot at all. Before that the culverts never emptied, and a drowned culvert says nothing.

Third: the refit. March of 1946, the company re-pinned all five gates — the old trunnion pins out, new ones in, a fortnight's work. And here is the detail I'd want written down, because it settles the whole business: of the five old pins that came out or were accounted for, the only one worn oval was off No. 3. The others were round as the day they were cast. Number three's was under-size from the start, the story in the company went — three and seven-eighths against a four-inch specification, some foundry botch passed in the building because the schedule was sacred — and forty years of rocking had worn it past oval into something you'd not hang a farm gate on. The singing stopped with the refit. Of course it did. You'd taken away the slack.

The woman engineer — Elsa, the name was, an Aldercote woman who kept the measurements in the building days — she'd flagged that very pin when it was delivered, so the company story went, and been overruled for the schedule's sake. Whatever else is fog in this business, the bailiffs always believed that part, because it is exactly how companies are.

Last thing. The drought — '66, that was — when the lake went to its lowest ever chalked, nine foot four, and the old village stood out in the air. A young woman came and went over the drained works, and it was she who fished the old No. 3 pin out of the culvert mud, which by rights should have been my find, but I'll allow she knew what she was holding better than I would have.
