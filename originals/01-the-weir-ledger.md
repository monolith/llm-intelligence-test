# The Weir Ledger

Ilsa Voss was twenty-four when the Selde Weir Company came to drown the village she was born in, and she asked them for a job.

Her brother Tomas thought her mad, or worse, disloyal. The Voss family had kept the orchard at Aldercote for as long as anyone could count backwards — their grandmother said the oldest tree had gone in around 1818, and nobody had ever contradicted a woman who could name the year of every frost since — and now the company meant to put ninety feet of water over the lot. But Ilsa had her reasons and gave them plainly. Someone would build the weir whether or not a Voss watched. If a Voss watched, it would at least be built honestly, and the people of Aldercote would be paid honestly for what went under.

The company took her because she was cheap and because she was exact. Her title, when anyone bothered with it, was measurer. She counted wagonloads, gauged castings, and kept the figures for Mr. Edmund Corven, the chief engineer, a man who was courteous the way a closed door is courteous. She kept the company's books in the company's office, and she kept a second book of her own: a fat green canvas ledger, bought with her own shilling, in which she entered everything twice. Into its front pages, before the works even began, she copied thirty years of the millers' flow books — the Selde's moods from 1876 to 1904 — because the downstream mills were owed water by law, and she meant the debt to be written where she could see it. Two years in that record stood out like scars: 1893 and 1902, when the river had run thin and the mills had gone hungry.

Those flow books gave her the first quarrel of her employment. Corven told the shareholders the new lake would fill in a single winter — one hundred and twenty days, he said, a number with a confident shine on it. Ilsa did the sum in the margin of her ledger. The lake would hold one thousand four hundred and forty million gallons. A wet winter brought the Selde down at twelve million gallons a day, but two million of every day's twelve were owed past the weir to the mills and could not lawfully be kept. Ten million a day, into one thousand four hundred and forty: one hundred and forty-four days, not one hundred and twenty. She said so. Corven thanked her in the tone men use for clocks that chime during sermons, and did not correct the prospectus. She wrote both numbers in the green ledger and let them wait.

The second quarrel was worse, and she dated it carefully: the fourteenth of April, 1905, the day the trunnion castings came up from the foundry. The weir was to have five gates, each twelve feet wide, and each gate swung on trunnion pins specified at four inches in the diameter. Ilsa gauged every pin as it came off the wagon, because gauging things was what she was for. Four were true. The pin for Gate No. 3 measured three and seven-eighths — an eighth of an inch under — and when she turned it to the light she found a blowhole in the collar, a little pocket where the casting had breathed when it should have set solid.

She took it to Corven. An eighth of an inch, Miss Voss, he said. Recasting meant six weeks, and six weeks meant the gates would not be hung by summer, and the summer was promised. The pin went in. Ilsa went back to the office and wrote, on page forty-seven of the green ledger: *No. 3 trunnion pin gauged 3⅞ in. against 4 in. Blowhole at collar. Flagged to E.C. Overruled.* She signed it, as she signed everything in that book, *I.V.*

There was one more entry she made that later mattered, though it seemed only sad at the time. On the eleventh of March, 1906, the church at Aldercote was stripped before the water came, and its bell — the bell that had rung over the orchard every Sunday of her childhood — was sold for sixty-four pounds to the foundry at Harden, the money going to the fund for moving the graves to dry ground. Tomas, who had taken the company's wage himself by then and built the timber shutters for all five gates, would not come to watch it lowered. Ilsa watched, and wrote the price.

That summer Aldercote emptied. In September, Corven discovered the green ledger. He called the keeping of private duplicates irregular, and he was not wrong that it was irregular; it was also hers, her paper and her ink and her shilling, and she said so and was dismissed. She walked out with the book under her arm. In 1921, when the company's office burned with all its records in it, her irregular book became the only true account of the Selde Weir ever made — but that is running ahead.

On the first of November, 1906, they closed the valley and the filling began. Ilsa had painted the spillway gauge herself that autumn, white figures on tarred board, the full mark at twenty-two feet — and she had noted in the ledger, because she noted everything, that the lip of Gate No. 3's culvert sat level with the fourteen-foot mark. She counted the filling from the shore like a woman keeping vigil. One hundred and forty-seven days: three more than her number, twenty-seven more than Corven's, and no one at the company said either figure aloud.

She stood at the new waterline that spring where the orchard rows went down into the dark, and she did not cry, because crying was not counting.

In 1908 she married Henrik Keel, the schoolmaster at Marrow Bend, a kind man who thought her exactness was a form of beauty, in which he was correct. The green ledger went onto her shelf, and stayed. When anyone asked her why she kept it, she said what she always said, the whole of her creed in seven words: count what you hear, write it down.

The water asked its question later, and slowly. The ledger waited for it.
