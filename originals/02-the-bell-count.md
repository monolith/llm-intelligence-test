# The Bell Count

Everyone in Marrow Bend knew what the sound was. That was the trouble with it.

On certain winter nights — never summer, never still nights — a slow tolling came up the valley from the direction of the lake. Not the Marrow Bend church; wrong direction, and its ringers slept like anybody. The lore was settled before anyone thought to question it: it was the bell of Aldercote, the drowned village, ringing under the water. It rang for the dead, people said. It rang when the lake remembered. The first reports had come in the winter of 1918, and by the time Aron Keel was grown, the story had the smooth, worn feel of a thing handled by everyone and examined by no one.

Aron was born in 1909, the only child of Henrik Keel the schoolmaster and of Ilsa Keel, who had been Ilsa Voss of Aldercote, and who did not speak of the weir. When his father died in 1928, Aron came home from his training and took over the school himself in 1929 — the same room, the same globe, his father's initials still inked inside the register cupboard. He was twenty, rational to the point of stubbornness, and constitutionally unable to leave a settled story unexamined. When he told his mother he meant to get to the bottom of the bell, she gave him neither encouragement nor warning, only the sentence he had grown up under: count what you hear, write it down.

So he did, for seventeen winters.

The log he kept was a plain exercise book, and its discipline never varied. For every report of tolling — his own ears or a neighbour's — he entered the date, the wind, and the water. The wind he took from the schoolhouse vane. The water he took from the water bailiff's board at the weir, where the spillway gauge reading was chalked up weekly. And out of that plain discipline, two facts surfaced that no one in five generations of firelight retelling had noticed.

Every tolling night, without exception, the wind was in the east — the dry, sustained wind off the moor. And every tolling night, without exception, the spillway gauge stood below fourteen feet. Forty-three tolling nights he logged across those seventeen winters, and the two conditions held on all forty-three. They were not, he noted scrupulously, the whole answer: six other nights had east wind and low water both, and passed in silence. Whatever rang required the wind and the low water, and something more that he could not name.

The lore fared worse in his columns. Rings for the dead: he set the parish burial register against his log. Nine deaths in Marrow Bend across the period; exactly two fell within a day of a tolling night, which is about what a thrown pair of dice would give you. He read the figures out at the Lamb one evening, and the publican told him figures were all very well but the bell had rung for old Sorrel, everyone knew that. You could not subtract a story from a village with arithmetic. He wrote that down too.

His one public triumph came by wager. On the first of November, 1930, the gauge stood at sixteen feet six inches, and Aron knew from the bailiff's board that the winter draw for the mills took the lake down about three inches a week. Thirty inches to fall; ten weeks; the water would pass below fourteen feet around the tenth of January. Before witnesses at the Lamb, the publican bet him a guinea the bell would not ring before Candlemas; Aron, privately doing the sum, said it would ring before January was out. The gauge crossed on schedule. The east wind was the only laggard — it arrived on the twenty-first of January, 1931, and that night Aron heard the tolling himself for the first time: a slow, rolling, metal note, arriving in gusts, less like a bell rung than a bell remembering how. He collected his guinea and was never quite forgiven for it.

The great winter was 1933–34, dry and bitter: seven tolling nights, his record. Then the war years, thinner entries but the pattern unbroken. Then, in March 1946, the company sent men to refit the weir — new trunnion pins for the old gates, a fortnight's clatter — and the pattern did not so much break as stop. The following winter the gauge went below fourteen feet as usual, and the east wind blew on four nights that qualified in every column, and nothing rang. Nor the winter after. The lake had gone silent, and no one in Marrow Bend celebrated, because a village will forgive a ghost anything except leaving.

Aron's final entry in the exercise book, dated at the end of that first silent winter, reads: *Conditions met four times, tolling nil. Something has changed at the weir that I cannot see. I have subtracted every wrong answer; the right one has not surfaced. Perhaps it waits for lower water.*

He never reopened the log. In 1949 his mother died, and among her effects was a locked deed-box he had never seen opened. Inside were two things: a letter of dismissal from the Selde Weir Company dated September 1906, and a fat green canvas ledger, dense with gauge figures and flow tables in her upright hand. Aron was a letters man; the shorthand of inverts and trunnions was not his language, and he was three years out of the habit of the mystery. He read enough to see that his mother had counted things all her life exactly as she had taught him to, felt the specific loneliness of understanding a parent too late, and carried the box up to the school attic, where he shelved it beside his own exercise book under a label in his careful teacher's hand: *Mother — weir papers.*

The two books stood spine to spine in the dark for seventeen years, saying between them everything anyone would have needed to know.
