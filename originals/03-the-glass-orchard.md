# The Glass Orchard

The drought of 1968 took the lake down to nine feet four inches on the spillway gauge — the lowest figure ever chalked on the bailiff's board — and by September you could walk into Aldercote for the first time in sixty-one years.

Vera Brandt came with a coring drill, a folding saw, and her grandmother's directions. She was twenty-seven, a botanist by training and a measurer by temperament, and the drowned village was, in a sense she did not advertise to the county council that funded her, family property. Her grandfather Tomas Voss had been the last of the orchard Vosses; her mother Margit had grown up on his stories of it; and Vera had grown up on Margit's, which is how family stories survive, thinning a little at each pouring.

What she found on the lakebed was better than the stories. The mud had kept out the air, and the air is what rots things. The orchard stumps stood in their old rows, grey and hard and shining wet — the villagers who walked out to stare called it the glass orchard within a week, and the name was exact, which Vera approved of. Stone walls stood to the waist. The church tower stood almost whole.

She worked the orchard first, because the orchard was the science. Nine stumps were sound enough to section. The rings were as clean as the day the water closed over them: the outermost ring of every stump was 1906, the year the saws and then the lake had come, and the oldest stump carried eighty-eight rings — a tree whose first full season was 1819, which agreed within a year with what her great-great-grandmother was supposed to have said about the planting. Family legend, confirmed by dendrochronology: Vera allowed herself one cigarette over that.

But rings alone are a diary without dates until you cross-date them, and two rings in her oldest sections were conspicuously starved — pinched years, drought years. Counting inward from 1906 she placed them at 1893 and 1902. What she needed now was an independent record of the Selde's flow to set against the wood, and the county had nothing: the weir company's records, she was told, had burned in 1921.

It was the parish, not the county, that had what she needed. The old schoolmaster, Aron Keel, had died in 1966, and his effects had gone into the parish chest. The vicar, apologising for the dust, brought her a plain exercise book and, beside it, a fat green canvas ledger with a paper label in a careful teacher's hand: *Mother — weir papers.*

Vera knew the family arithmetic before she opened either book. Aron Keel was her mother's first cousin — his mother, Ilsa, had been Tomas's sister. The upright hand filling the green ledger was her own great-aunt's.

The front pages of the ledger held copied flow tables for the Selde from 1876 to 1904, and there were her two starved years, 1893 and 1902, running thin in the millers' figures exactly as they ran thin in the wood. The trees and the ledger agreed like two witnesses who had never met. That alone was worth the summer. It was not the half of it.

She read the ledger through that night, and the exercise book after it, and what had been botany became something else. Page forty-seven, dated the fourteenth of April 1905, in her great-aunt's hand: *No. 3 trunnion pin gauged 3⅞ in. against 4 in. Blowhole at collar. Flagged to E.C. Overruled. — I.V.* Further on, a note that the lip — the invert — of Gate No. 3's culvert sat at the fourteen-foot mark on a gauge Ilsa had painted herself. And in the exercise book, in the cousin's hand, seventeen winters of tolling nights, forty-three of them, every one with an east wind, every one with the gauge below fourteen feet, the whole record ending in bafflement in the first silent winter after the company's men refitted the gates in March 1946 with new pins.

There was one more thing the lore said, and the lakebed was available for questioning. The next morning Vera climbed the Aldercote church tower — the stair was mud to the eighth step and bare stone above — and stood in the bell chamber. The headstock was there. The wheel was there, grey as the orchard. There was no bell. There had been no bell, she found when she went back to the ledger's spring of 1906, for sixty-two years: sold on the eleventh of March that year, sixty-four pounds, to the foundry at Harden, the money to move the graves. The bell that all of Marrow Bend had heard tolling from 1918 to 1946 had spent those years as somebody's dinner service.

She walked out to the weir itself on her last day, along the drained face where the culverts stood open like empty throats. At Gate No. 3 the trunnions wore the bright pins of 1946. In the mud of the culvert below, half sunk, she found the old pin — three and seven-eighths by her calipers where it was not worn, and worn well past that, worn visibly oval, by forty years of a loose gate rocking on it. She stood in the culvert mouth with the pin in both hands and put the whole of it together, the way you can only when three people have done the counting for you: a gate hung an eighth of an inch slack in 1905; a lake drawn below the culvert lip every hard winter after 1918, letting the air in; an east wind off the moor, steady enough to rock twelve feet of gate on a worn oval pin; and an empty culvert to give the knocking its long bronze voice.

Sixty-three years from the flagging of the pin to the finding of it, and every year of the distance covered by somebody in her family writing down what they heard. She deposited the stump sections at the Harden museum, and the two books with them, and at the top of her survey report — the county got its money's worth — she wrote the only sentence of it anyone in Marrow Bend ever quoted:

*The lake never had a ghost. It had a hinge.*
