# Story Three — Delivered on Her Own Wheels

Bly County is two hundred miles north of Pell County and has nothing to do with it. It has
timber, and it had, from 1884 until the last cut in 1936, the Tolliver Lumber Company,
which Silas Tolliver founded at thirty-five with a mill, a horse, and an unshakable
conviction that the horse was temporary.

The horse lasted eleven years. In 1895 Tolliver laid the first four miles of what the
company always called the Sixmile tram, after the creek and not after its length, which
eventually ran to nineteen miles of thirty-pound rail up three drainages, with grades that
would have frightened a common carrier and switchbacks that would have appalled one. He
ran it at first with a geared engine that could climb anything and pull almost nothing.
What he wanted was a road engine — something with big wheels and a long wheelbase that
could take a made-up train of loads down the main stem to the mill at a speed better than
walking.

In the spring of 1901 his daughter Pearl, who was twenty and had been keeping the
company's books for two years because she was better at it than anybody her father could
hire, found the advertisement. A railroad in Pell County was selling a twelve-year-old
American-type engine. Silas went down on the cars to look at her, came back, and wrote a
figure on the back of an envelope, and Pearl entered the transaction in the stock book on
the nineteenth of June, copying the seller's bill of sale word for word because that was
how she had been taught:

> *Locomotive No. 9, 4-4-0, Rowan Works 1889, works no. 1142, sold this day to the
> Tolliver Lumber Company, Bly County, for two thousand one hundred and fifty dollars
> ($2,150.00), delivered on her own wheels.*

She had cost her first owners eight thousand six hundred dollars new, a figure Pearl also
recorded, in the margin, with a small note that they had bought her for a quarter of her
price. Silas Tolliver was proud of that arithmetic for the rest of his life and repeated
it at every opportunity, generally rounding it in his own favor.

*Delivered on her own wheels* meant she came north under steam, which took eleven days and
four railroads. She arrived at the mill in July of 1901 with a Pell County crew who turned
around and went home on the cars, and the Tolliver shop painted out the 9 and painted on a
4, because Tolliver numbered its engines in the order it bought them and she was the
fourth.

She was, by every account the company kept, a disappointment for exactly one season and an
institution for twenty-six after that. The trouble at first was that a four-coupled engine
built for a level branch does not care for a five percent grade, and the Sixmile tram had
several. What she was for was the main stem — nine miles of two percent and easy curves
from the foot of the switchbacks down to the pond — and once the shop stopped asking her to
climb and started asking her to haul, she settled into the work and did it for
twenty-seven years.

Alma Nace, Pearl's daughter, was born in 1907 and grew up inside the sound of her. In her
memoir she wrote that the engine had a bell with a flat note and a habit of slipping on the
third bridge, and that her grandfather would not let anyone but two men run her, and that
the men were both named Coe and were not related.

What the books show is duller and more useful. She ran the main stem from 1901 to 1928.
There is a boiler retube in 1909, new tires in 1913, a cracked frame welded in 1919 and
welded again in 1922, and a shopping in 1924 that cost more than a fifth of what she had
been bought for. From 1925 the entries get shorter. In September 1928 the company took
delivery of a second-hand consolidation with eight driving wheels and the strength to do
her work and half again, and No. 4 was set out behind the shop.

She sat there through the winter and into the spring, and in May of 1929 the company cut
her up, and Pearl Nace made the last entry any book anywhere would ever make about the
engine:

> *No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile this month. Scrap to Ravel Brothers, $470.*

Four hundred and seventy dollars. She had cost eight thousand six hundred new in 1889 and
brought two thousand one hundred and fifty second-hand in 1901, and forty years after she
left the Rowan shops she went out of the world for four hundred and seventy dollars and a
line in a ledger. Pearl noted, because she noted everything, that Ravel Brothers took the
tires and the brass separately and that the boiler shell went into the mill as a stationary
tank, where it stayed until the mill closed.

Silas Tolliver had died in 1922. The cut ran out in 1936, and the company wound up over
the next two years, and in 1938 Pearl Nace — who was then fifty-seven and had kept books
for that company for thirty-nine years — did the thing that made this story recoverable at
all. She did not burn the records. She put the stock book, the shop book, the timber
ledgers, and eleven boxes of correspondence into the back of a truck and drove them to the
Bly County Historical Rooms, which accessioned them, catalogued them accurately, shelved
them, and did not have a single inquiry about them for twenty-four years.

She is not on record as saying why. Alma's memoir says only that her mother "did not care
to have a thing end twice."

That is the whole of it, and it is a story about a lumber company two hundred miles from
anywhere relevant, in a county that had never heard of Sallow Creek. It has no ghosts in
it. Nobody in Bly County ever wondered where the engine came from beyond the word "Pell"
on a bill of sale, and nobody in Pell County ever wondered where she went, because Pell
County had already decided.

The two facts that matter are small and dry and were written down by a twenty-year-old
bookkeeper and a fifty-year-old one: that on the nineteenth of June, 1901, a locomotive
numbered 9 left Pell County alive and under her own steam; and that in May of 1929 she was
cut up at a lumber camp two hundred miles north of the gorge she was supposed to be lying
in.

Between those two dates, for sixteen of the years she spent hauling logs in Bly County, a
valley she had left was hearing her run.
