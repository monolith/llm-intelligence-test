# Story One — The Two-Inch Mark

The Cadder Valley Railroad ran thirty-one miles, and every mile of it was an argument. It
left the main line at Ninestone, which the company called milepost 0 and everyone else
called the county seat, and it climbed the valley past the mills to Cadder Falls at
milepost 23, and went on climbing to the Ashby quarry at milepost 31. Between the quarry
and the Falls the line fell two and two-tenths in the hundred, nine miles of it, which is
a grade that makes brakemen religious. And a mile below the Falls, at milepost 22, it had
to get across Sallow Creek.

The gorge there is ninety-seven feet deep and narrow at the lip. The company's answer was
three spans — a hundred and twenty feet of deck girder on the near side, a hundred and
twenty on the far, and between them a pin-connected truss three hundred feet long. Five
hundred and forty feet of iron in the air. They began it in April of 1896.

Adela Rennick was twenty-two that spring and had been with the engineering party a year.
Her title on the payroll was *computer*, which meant she did the arithmetic, held the rod,
kept the level book, and copied the resident engineer's figures into fair form for the
office at Ninestone. She had learned the work from a surveyor's manual and from her
brother Emil, eight years older, who was the falsework foreman on the viaduct. Emil put up
the staging the truss was assembled on. Adela wrote down what the truss did afterward.

She kept two books. One belonged to the company. The other was hers — black oilcloth, her
own shilling, her own hand — and into it went everything the company's book got and a
good deal it didn't. In the front of her own book, on the first page, she had copied the
rule the drawings gave for the long span, because she expected to need it often:

> *Rule for the long span: one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees.*

Steel is not a fixed thing. It lengthens in July and shortens in January, and a
three-hundred-foot span moves an inch for every forty degrees the thermometer travels. So
one end of the truss was pinned solid to the north pier, and the other end — the south
end — was left free to slide, resting on a nest of rollers in a cast pedestal that the
drawings called the south shoe. The rollers let the bridge breathe. Sheet 11 of the
contract drawings said how much breathing room to give them: three inches of travel, each
way, from the seated position.

On the twelfth of September, 1897, at twenty minutes to four in the afternoon, they closed
the span. The thermometer on the north pier read sixty-six degrees. Adela was on the deck
with her book because somebody had to write down the temperature at closing, and the
temperature at closing is the whole of the arithmetic afterward: from that number you
count in both directions.

She looked at the roller seat, and then she looked at it again, and then she got down on
the deck plates and put her thumb on the chisel mark the erection gang had cut into the
cast iron to show where to bring the rollers up to. The mark was at two inches.

She reported it to the resident engineer, Warren Tice, who was forty and unhurried and
had brought the job in under time. He heard her out. He said that the pedestal could not
be recast before the fall haul, that the quarry contract began on the first of November,
and that two inches was more than this valley would ever ask for. He was not unkind about
it. He was, as far as anybody afterward could establish, entirely sincere.

She went back to her own book that night and wrote page sixty-two:

> *Sept. 12, 1897. Span closed 3:40 p.m., thermometer 66°. Rollers set at the two-inch
> mark. Sheet 11 calls for three. Reported to W.T., who answers that the pedestal cannot
> be recast before the fall haul and that two inches is more than the valley will ever ask
> for. Entered here so that it is somewhere. — A.R.*

*Entered here so that it is somewhere.* It is the sentence the whole of the rest of this
depends on, and it was written by a woman who had no expectation of ever being read.

She never recorded who had cut the mark. The gang was fourteen men and she knew all their
names, and she did not write one of them down for the reason people usually do not: she
was reporting a condition, not accusing a person. The company's erection time books, which
would have had the answer, burned in the Ninestone roundhouse fire of the fourth of April,
1911, with everything else the CVR had ever written about building that bridge. After
1911, Adela's black book was the only account of the span's erection that existed anywhere
in the world, and nobody knew it, including her.

The line opened to Cadder Falls on the fourteenth of November, 1897, and for two years
nothing happened that anyone thought about twice.

Then, at twenty minutes past seven on the morning of the sixth of March, 1898, a work
train came down off the quarry grade without brakes. The engine was Number 9 — a
four-coupled Rowan machine built in 1889, works number 1142, which the company had bought
new for eight thousand six hundred dollars and which was the best thing on the property.
She ran nine miles gathering speed and went off the iron at the north approach of the new
viaduct, and she did not go into the gorge, and she did not take anybody with her. Ott
Lidell the brakeman broke his arm jumping. Sherrod the fireman was scalded across the
back at the deck. That was the whole of it.

> *March 6, 1898. No. 9 off the iron at the north approach, 7:20 a.m., running away from
> the quarry. Nobody killed. Lidell's arm broke, Sherrod scalded at the deck. Nine days to
> rerail her.*

The *Ninestone Sentinel* of the eighth said the same thing in longer words: *No lives were
lost. Two of the gang are hurt, and the engine is on her side at the north end of the new
viaduct, where she will be got up within the fortnight.*

Nine days later they had her on the rails again. She worked another three years on the
valley, and then, on the nineteenth of June, 1901, the company sold her. There is a line
for it in the stock book at Ninestone, in the chief clerk's hand:

> *Locomotive No. 9, 4-4-0, Rowan Works 1889, works no. 1142, sold this day to the
> Tolliver Lumber Company, Bly County, for two thousand one hundred and fifty dollars
> ($2,150.00), delivered on her own wheels.*

Two thousand one hundred and fifty dollars is exactly one quarter of eight thousand six
hundred, which is the arithmetic of a twelve-year-old locomotive and of no interest to
anyone at the time. She went north on her own wheels in June, with her own crew as far as
the junction, and the valley did not think about her again for sixty years.

Five weeks before she went, Adela had a letter:

> *The engineering party is reduced entire on the 15th May next, and your time is paid to
> that day. Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card.*

The party was reduced entire. That is a fact, and it is on paper. Whether the entry on
page sixty-two had anything to do with the fact is not on paper anywhere, and the people
who could have said were the two people least likely to: a man who thought he had been
reasonable and a woman who did not complain. She was twenty-seven. She married Josiah
Frayne, a mill clerk at the Falls, the following year, and had a son in 1904, and kept the
black book on a shelf for fifty-eight years, and never worked for a railroad again.

The bridge stood in the cold air with two inches where three had been drawn, and waited.
