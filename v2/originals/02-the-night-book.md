# Story Two — The Night Book

Judd Rennick was born in 1888, the only child of Emil Rennick the carpenter, and he went
on nights at the Cadder Falls depot in 1909, at twenty-one, because the job was open and
because his father had done work for the railroad and the railroad remembered. His aunt
Adela — his father's younger sister — had worked for the same company before he was born
and never once talked about it. He was made station agent in 1915 and stayed thirty-nine
years.

The first thing he ever wrote down about the bridge he wrote three years late, from the
depot's weather slate, because in August of 1909 he had no reason to think it mattered.
The bridge gang was at Sallow Creek three weeks that summer. They put down eighty-five
pound steel where sixty had been, and new ties under it, and while they were at it they
jacked the span at both ends and set the shoes again. The note he eventually copied into
the front of his book read:

> *Bridge gang here three weeks, August 1909. New 85-lb. steel, new ties, both shoes
> jacked and set again to the mark. Thermometer on the north pier read 86 at four o'clock
> when they seated the south nest. Mr. Coyle says the deck is forty ton heavier than it
> was.*

*Set again to the mark.* The gang did exactly what the bridge had been built to. They
brought the rollers up to the chisel mark on the seat, which is what a careful crew does,
and the mark was where it had been since 1897, and not one man on that gang had ever heard
of Sheet 11. The only difference between them and the erection gang was the afternoon.
Twelve years earlier the span had been closed at sixty-six degrees. Coyle's men seated it
at eighty-six. Twenty degrees of steel — half an inch on a three-hundred-foot span — went
quietly out of the bridge's account that August, and everyone went home pleased with the
job.

Two winters went by, both of them mild. Then, at two in the morning on the seventeenth of
January, 1912, Judd Rennick was alone in the office with the stove and the sounder when
the whole building spoke.

He described it the same way for fifty years and never improved on it: one deep report,
not a crack and not a roll, like a heavy door shut in a cellar three towns off; and under
it, running up the rails and into the floor and into the sounder on the table, a shiver
that arrived before the sound did. He went out on the platform in his shirtsleeves. There
was nothing on the line. There was nothing anywhere. The thermometer by the door said
twelve below.

By that evening the Falls had already decided what it was. In 1898 a runaway had gone off
the iron at the north end of the viaduct, and in fourteen years of retelling the runaway
had acquired a gorge, a crew, and a death toll, none of which it ever had. The town said
Number 9 was down in Sallow Creek with her men, and that when the cold got deep enough she
worked the grade again. It was a good story. It was better than the weather.

Judd started the book the next morning. He ruled five columns — the date, the day's high,
the night's low, whether she spoke, and the hour — and he kept them for forty-three
winters, 1911–12 through 1953–54, and he never once wrote down an opinion in it.

What the columns showed took him about a decade to see, and it was this. She never spoke
unless the night's low went below six degrees. And she never spoke unless the day that
preceded the night had been at least thirty degrees warmer than the night that followed
it. Both, every time, sixty-one times.

Both conditions, and never one without the other, and yet not enough by themselves: five
nights in forty-three winters answered both conditions and gave nothing at all. Judd
worked out why by watching, not by theory. She would not speak twice inside the same
freeze. If she had spoken on the Tuesday, then Thursday could go to fifteen below with a
forty-degree fall behind it and the valley would hear nothing, unless in between there had
come a thaw — a real one, up above forty in the daytime. After a thaw she was ready again.

Sixty-one nights when she spoke. Five that answered and gave nothing. Sixty-six nights in
all that answered the conditions. Thirty-four winters when she spoke at least once, and
nine winters in the forty-three when she never spoke at all. The heaviest winter was
1917–18, five nights, and the January of that winter carried the coldest reading anybody
in the valley ever recorded — eleven below.

There was also the matter of the deaths, which Judd handled the way he handled everything.
The lore said she rang for the dead. So he counted. Between 1912 and 1954 there were a
hundred and ninety deaths in Cadder Falls, and seven of them fell within a week after a
night when she spoke. That leaves fifty-four nights she spoke and nobody died, and a
hundred and eighty-three people who died with the bridge silent. He wrote the four numbers
in the back of the book without comment and never mentioned them in the store, because he
had to live there.

The store was Dorsey Tice's. Dorsey was born in 1893, the son of Warren Tice, who had been
the resident engineer when the viaduct was built and was by then the CVR's chief engineer
at Ninestone, and Dorsey did not think about the bridge from one year to the next except as
the thing the town would not stop talking about. On the afternoon of the fourteenth of
January, 1936, Judd came in for kerosene and said she would speak that night.

Dorsey asked how he could possibly know.

Judd said: it made forty-one at noon. It is thirty-eight now, at four o'clock, and it has
been coming down four degrees an hour since one. Thirty-eight to six is thirty-two
degrees; at four an hour that is eight hours; she will be under six about midnight. And
there has been no thaw and no speaking since the second week of December.

Dorsey Tice bet him five dollars.

The low that night was three below — a fall of forty-four degrees from the day's high —
and she spoke at ten minutes past one in the morning, and Judd Rennick collected five
dollars from the son of the man who had overruled his aunt, and neither of the two men in
that store had the faintest idea that was what had just happened.

She spoke for the last time on the ninth of February, 1954.

In March of that year the railroad rebuilt the bridge for diesel power. Among a great many
other things, the gang took out the old roller nests and put in rocker bearings with four
inches of travel each way, and threw the cast pedestals over the abutment into the creek,
which is what you do with scrap iron in a gorge. Nobody told the station agent, because
there was nothing to tell. Judd retired that June.

He kept listening. The winter of 1955–56 gave three nights that answered both conditions
perfectly, and the valley slept through all three. On the second of March, 1956, he opened
the book for the last time and wrote:

> *Second winter since the rockers went in. Three nights under six degrees, three falls of
> better than thirty, and not a sound out of her either winter. I have kept this book
> forty-three winters and I do not know now what it was I was listening to.*

He was sixty-eight, and he had, in five ruled columns, every fact necessary to the answer,
and not one of the three other facts required to read them.
