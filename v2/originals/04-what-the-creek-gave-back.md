# Story Four — What the Creek Gave Back

The state's flood-control cut took Sallow Creek out of its bed in the late summer of 1962.
For eleven weeks the water went around by a diversion channel, and the gorge under the
viaduct was dry to the gravel for the first time since the ice.

Ruth Frayne was twenty-eight and three years into the Pell County highway department, and
the bridge was hers only in the sense that everything with a deck in that county was hers.
Her assignment was an inspection: measure, sound, photograph, file. She was Wendell Frayne's daughter and Adela Rennick's granddaughter, and
she had been told the ghost story by her own grandmother, who told it the way you tell a
story you have decided not to have an opinion about.

She went down into the gorge on the fourth of September with a tape and a camera, and
found four things.

The first thing she found was nothing. There is no locomotive in Sallow Creek — no frame,
no boiler shell, no driving wheel, no scatter of firebox stays. She walked eight hundred
feet of dry bed above and below the viaduct with the whole floor of it exposed, and
photographed all of it, and the largest piece of iron in Sallow Creek was a rail.

The second thing she found was the pedestal.

It was forty feet below the abutment on the south side, half in the gravel, cast iron,
thrown there by the 1954 gang the way any gang throws scrap into a hole it is standing
over. She cleaned it with a wire brush. It was the old south shoe, the seat that had
carried the roller nest of the center span from 1897 to 1954, and cut into it, plain as a
date on a stone, was the chisel mark the erection gang had brought the rollers up to.

She measured it with a steel tape three times and then went up top and measured the new
rocker bearings the railroad had put in eight years before, and wrote both numbers down.
The mark on the old seat was at two inches. The new rockers had four inches of travel each
way.

The third thing she found was in her father's house. Adela had died in 1959 at
eighty-five, and the black oilcloth book had gone to Wendell with the rest of it and sat
on a shelf being a keepsake. Ruth took it back to the office and read it in one evening,
and in the front matter she found the rule — *one inch in three hundred feet for forty
degrees* — and on page sixty-two she found her grandmother at twenty-three, at twenty
minutes to four on a September afternoon in 1897, writing down a thermometer reading of
sixty-six degrees and a chisel mark at two inches where Sheet 11 called for three, and
finishing with the flattest sentence in the county's history: *Entered here so that it is
somewhere.*

The fourth thing she found she had to go and ask for. Judd Rennick was seventy-four and
living three streets from the depot he had run for thirty-nine years, and when she
explained what she was doing he went and got the night book and put it on the table and
did not say anything while she read it, which took two hours.

Five ruled columns and forty-three winters. Sixty-one nights when the bridge spoke. Every
one of them below six degrees, every one of them at the bottom of a fall of thirty degrees
or better. Five nights that answered both conditions and gave nothing. And in the front of
the book, written out from a depot slate, a gang in August of 1909 jacking both shoes and
setting them *again to the mark* at eighty-six degrees on the north pier.

Ruth Frayne did the arithmetic that night on the back of the inspection form, and it is
the whole of the answer.

A three-hundred-foot span moves an inch for every forty degrees. Sheet 11 gave the south
nest three inches of cold travel from the closing position, and the span was closed at
sixty-six degrees. Three inches is a hundred and twenty degrees. Built as drawn, the
bridge would have run out of travel at fifty-four below zero, which is a temperature this
valley has never seen and never will.

It was not built as drawn. It was built to two inches, which is eighty degrees, which put
the binding point at fourteen below. Also a temperature the valley does not reach — the
coldest reading in sixty-eight years of record is eleven below — and that is why, for
twelve years, a wrong bridge behaved like a right one.

Then in August of 1909 a careful crew jacked the span on a hot afternoon and brought the
rollers up to the same two-inch mark with the steel standing at eighty-six degrees instead
of sixty-six. Two inches of travel measured from eighty-six is eighty degrees down from
eighty-six. Six above zero.

Six above zero is where a station agent's five ruled columns say the bridge spoke, sixty-one
times in forty-three winters, and never once above.

She wanted a witness who had no stake in it, and she found one three miles up the valley.
The Wexlers of Kettle Bench had kept a volunteer weather record — high, low, and rain,
every day — from the first of January, 1894, and Lettie Wexler, who was seventy-three and
had been keeping it herself since 1926, lent Ruth sixty-nine years of mornings in four
cardboard boxes. The Wexlers had never written down a sound in their lives. They wrote
down weather, and their weather agreed with Judd Rennick's book on every single one of the
sixty-one nights, including the first: *Jan. 17. High 34. Low −2 at daylight. Clear,
still.*

And the weather book did something Judd's book could not, because it started eighteen
years earlier. Between the opening of the line in November 1897 and the re-decking in
August 1909, the Wexler record shows seven nights below six degrees, four of them at the
bottom of falls of thirty degrees or more — and the valley heard nothing on any of them,
and no living person at the Falls in 1962 remembered anything before 1912. The coldest of
those twelve years was nine below, on the fourth of February, 1899: five degrees short of
what the bridge as built would have needed to bind. The two winters immediately after the
re-decking, 1909–10 and 1910–11, never got below nine *above*. The first night below six
degrees after August 1909 fell on the seventeenth of January, 1912.

The silence of those seven nights is not an absence of data. It is the proof.

The locomotive took a letter and eleven days. The Bly County Historical Rooms held the
Tolliver Lumber Company's books, deposited in 1938 by a bookkeeper named Pearl Nace and
consulted by nobody since, and they sent photostats of two pages: a stock book entry of
the nineteenth of June, 1901, and a shop book entry of May 1929 recording that No. 4, *ex-
Cadder Valley 9*, had been cut up at Sixmile and her scrap sold for four hundred and
seventy dollars.

Ruth Frayne's report went to the county in September 1962 and ended:

> *The south pedestal was recovered from the creek bed forty feet below the abutment,
> where the 1954 gang threw it. The chisel mark on the roller seat is at two inches. Sheet
> 11 calls for three. There is no locomotive in this creek and there never was one. What
> the valley heard for forty-three winters was a bridge letting go of itself, one inch
> late.*

The field book went to the Pell County Historical Society in 1963, the weather book in
1967 after Lettie Wexler died, the night book in 1969 after Judd did. The pedestal is in
the entrance hall with a card on it. Ruth read her paper to the society in the spring of
1963 and said, in the discussion afterward, the sentence the *Sentinel* did not print: that her grandmother had measured the bridge
in 1897 and she had measured it in 1962, and both of them had got two inches, and the only
difference between them was that the second one was believed.
