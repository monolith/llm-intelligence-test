# Retelling 01 — Vesta Cobb, a mill hand's daughter, recorded on tape 1974

*Tape made for the county's oral-history collection at her kitchen table in Cadder Falls, 1974. Transcribed as spoken; her own doubling-back left in.*

Well, you'll have to stop me when you've got enough, because I run on. My husband used to say I could talk the paint off a porch.

I was born in nineteen twenty, second house up from the mill gate, and my father was in the mill his whole working life, and my mother's people had been at the Falls since the line was built. Since they were building it, my grandmother would say, because she boarded the men who came in to do it. So I am not from anywhere else, and everything I am going to tell you I got at that stove from people who are all of them dead now.

You want to know about the sound. Everybody wants to know about the sound. It has been quiet a long time and now everybody wants it.

It was one noise. That is the first thing, and people who never lived here get it wrong — they think it was a rattling or a rolling, like thunder walking off down the valley, and it was not. It was one. One deep report, and then nothing, and the nothing after it was worse than the noise was. Like a door shut in a cellar three towns off. That is how my mother put it and I have never heard it put better. Not sharp like a shot. Deep, the way a big thing sounds when it is a long way down.

And you felt it before you heard it. That is the part people don't believe. It came up through the floorboards into your feet, the way the mill did when they started the big saw, only the mill was daytime and this was not. This was always the small hours. Between midnight and four, and I would say closer to two than either end, though I would not swear to it. Nobody heard it at supper. Nobody heard it at noon in their life.

And it only came on the killing nights. That was what the Falls called them. Not just cold — we get cold, cold is nothing. It had to be the kind where the day had been soft, shirtsleeve weather almost, mud in the road, and then the sky went hard and clear along about dark and the whole night came to pieces on you. That was a killing night. You could feel it coming at the mill gate at four in the afternoon and my father would come in and say, well, she may talk tonight, and my mother would tell him not to say so in front of the children, and he would say it anyway.

Now I heard it. I want that understood, because people my age say they heard it and half of them are repeating their fathers. I heard it.

It was a winter in the thirties, and I could not give you the night. We had had rain that day, I remember the rain, and the wash was on the line and it froze on the line, and my mother was cross about it. And that night the whole street came awake at once. Not one house. The whole street. The floor went first, like I said, and then the sound came, and my brother was up before I was and my father was already at the window with no lamp lit. I remember the cold on the glass and I remember there being no wind at all, and dogs going off up and down the row, one after another, and then the dogs quit and it was as still as the inside of a cup. And then doors started opening. Grown men out on their porches in their union suits in that cold, holding their arms, looking up the valley toward the bridge, and not a one of them saying anything, and that is the thing I have never got over. They knew what they were listening for. It was not a surprise to them. It was a visit.

My mother put us back to bed and she said, that is only the train, go to sleep, which was the exact wrong thing to say to a child if you wanted her asleep.

Because that is what it was. That is what everybody at the Falls knew and I have never had any reason handed to me to think different. Number Nine went into that gorge in the bad winter when the bridge was new, and her crew went in with her, and they were killed, and they were never brought up. They are down there yet, under the gravel and the water, and it is her working the grade that the valley hears. She comes down off the quarry the way she came down that morning and she cannot stop, and when she hits she makes that one sound. That is the whole of it. My grandmother told me that, and she was here for it.

And it started that same winter, the first winter after they opened that bridge — that would be ninety-seven going into ninety-eight — right on the heels of the wreck. Not years after. That winter. The bridge was brand new and so was the ghost, and the people who were at the Falls then had barely got done being sorry about the men before they had to start listening for them. Seventy-odd years of it, in the end. Think of that. Seventy-odd years of a thing coming up out of the ground at two in the morning and the whole valley knowing what it was and nobody able to do a solitary thing about it.

I know there are people who will tell you different. There was always somebody at the store with a theory. Ice, one of them said, ice in the gorge, and I said all right, then why not every cold night, why the killing nights only, and he didn't have anything for that.

Judd Rennick was the station agent and Judd wrote them down. Every single one. He had a ruled book at the depot with lines in it, and when she spoke he put it in the book — the night, and the cold, and the hour of it, all in that little hand of his. Everybody knew he did it. It was not a secret, it was more like a habit the town had. And nobody at the store took a bit of notice of it. They would say, well, Judd's got another one in the book, the way you would say somebody's cat had kittens. He was not a strange man, that is what I want to say. He was the most ordinary man you ever saw in your life, and he had this one thing.

The store was Dorsey Tice's, right there by the tracks, and Dorsey and Judd went at each other over that book for years, in a friendly way, the way men do. And Dorsey lost money to him over it once. Bet him she would speak on a certain night and she spoke. Five dollars, I always heard, and five dollars then was five dollars. The Falls got a good long ride out of that. They were still telling it when I was grown and married, and Dorsey would tell it on himself, which is why people liked him.

Dorsey was old Warren Tice's nephew, you know. The Tices were railroad people out of Ninestone, engineering people, big in the company, and Dorsey came down here and kept a store, which his family thought was a comedown and he did not. He had the best hard candy in the county and he weighed it heavy for children.

And then it stopped. It stopped when the railroad put new iron under that bridge — they were down there all that spring with their cranes and their trucks, doing it over for the diesels — and after that she never spoke again. Not once. And what did the Falls say? The Falls said the railroad had spoiled it. That is exactly what they said, and they were sour about it, and I was young enough to be sour about it too. Somebody had come from off with money and taken it away from us. That is how it felt. You may laugh but you had to be here.

Judd lived on and on. He lived into his eighties, up on the hill there, and he was still in town when the young Frayne woman came round asking her questions, going house to house with her notebook, very polite, very quick. She was his grand-niece, they told me, come to look at his book, and he let her have it. That was some years back now.

I will say this and then you can turn that machine off. I am not a foolish woman and I did not raise foolish children. But I lay in that bed with the wash frozen on the line and I felt the floor move before I heard a thing, and there is not a man living can tell me what that was, and I notice that the ones who are surest are the ones who were never in the house.

# Retelling 02 — Harlan Pike, son of the CVR's chief clerk, from a memoir written in 1958

*From "The Company at Ninestone," an unpublished memoir written in his seventies. This chapter, headed "The Sallow Creek Viaduct," is given entire.*

My father was chief clerk of the Cadder Valley Railroad from 1893 to 1919, and he brought the office home with him every night of those twenty-six years. Other boys grew up with a dog. I grew up with a stock book. Our dining table on a Sunday was a place where a man might be asked, quite seriously, whether he thought a figure in a column was one the directors would swallow, and I learned to have an opinion before I learned to shave.

I set this down because there is nobody left who cares to have it right, and because what is said about the Sallow Creek Viaduct at present is mostly rubbish.

Begin with the road, because people who write about the valley never trouble to. The Cadder Valley Railroad was thirty-one miles of it, and no more. Ninestone was milepost 0 — the county seat, the shops, the office, my father's desk with the window on the yard. The Sallow Creek Viaduct stood at milepost 22. Cadder Falls, the mill town, was milepost 23. The Ashby quarry, which was the reason the whole thing existed, was at milepost 31. Everything else the company owned was sidings.

The grade from the quarry down to the viaduct was two and two-tenths percent, and it held at that the whole way. I write that plainly because it is the fact from which most of the trouble in this chapter descends. Two and two-tenths percent held all that distance is a great deal of falling for a small railroad with second-hand brakes, and every man on the property knew it, and the company's answer was to hire careful men and pray for cold weather on the drums.

The viaduct itself was begun in April 1896 and the line was opened to Cadder Falls on 14 November 1897. It was three spans: one hundred and twenty feet, then a pin-connected truss of three hundred feet across the gorge proper, then one hundred and twenty feet again. Five hundred and forty feet in all, and ninety-seven feet above the water. I have stood on the deck of it as a boy with my father's hand in my collar and I can tell you that ninety-seven feet is a great plenty.

Now, the point of the thing, which nobody outside the office ever grasped. Steel moves. It lengthens in the heat and shortens in the cold, and a three-hundred-foot span in a valley that runs from the middle of July to the middle of January moves a good deal. So the center span was pinned solid at its north end and its south end was left free to travel — it rested on a nest of rollers set in a cast pedestal, the south shoe, and the whole span breathed on that nest, out in summer and back in winter, for fifty-odd years.

The rule the engineers worked to was one inch in three hundred feet for fifty degrees. That is a rule of thumb and they used it as one; you will find it in any office of that period. For our span, being three hundred feet exactly, it came out at an inch for every fifty degrees, which is the sort of arithmetic even a clerk's son can carry in his head.

The span was closed on 12 September 1897 with the thermometer at sixty-six degrees. Sheet 11 of the contract drawings called for four inches of travel on the south nest. Four inches was generous for a three-hundred-foot span, and I have always thought it showed a proper caution in whoever drew it.

The resident engineer on the work was Warren Tice, afterward the company's chief engineer at Ninestone and a considerable man in this county. The party's computer was a Miss Adela Rennick. She did the arithmetic, and she did it better than the party did — that is my father's phrase, not mine, and he was not a man who handed out compliments to save postage. She kept her own duplicate book, in her own hand, quite separate from the party's book, which was thought eccentric and was not. Her time with the road ended when the engineering party was reduced. Two of that party were taken back on in the August following, for the Ashby extension work, and she was not, and I have never needed anybody to explain that to me. She was also in the habit of entering in that book whatever she thought, including what she thought about the bridge, and one such entry got back to the resident engineer. Tice never forgave that entry, and she was gone inside four years. I have nothing in writing on the point and I do not pretend to. I sat in that office as a boy while men talked over my head, and there are things you know from the temperature of a room.

Then the wreck, which is the whole cause of the valley's foolishness.

On the morning of 6 March 1898 a work train started down the quarry grade with nothing left to hold her. The locomotive was No. 9, and she went off the iron at the north approach of the new viaduct at twenty minutes past seven. Nobody was killed. Two of the gang were hurt. It took nine days to rerail her. The *Ninestone Sentinel* of 8 March 1898 had it exactly right, and I copy the notice out because I am tired of arguing with people who have never read it:

> *No lives were lost. Two of the gang are hurt, and the engine is on her side at the north end of the new viaduct, where she will be got up within the fortnight.*

Nobody killed. Not one man. I underline it because within my own lifetime I have heard it said at Cadder Falls, by grown people, that a crew went down into that gorge and is there still, and that the sound the valley used to hear on winter nights was that engine working. There was no crew in the gorge. The engine was not in the gorge. The engine was on her side at the north end, in daylight, with half the county looking at her.

And she did not stay there either. No. 9 was a 4-4-0 out of the Rowan Locomotive Works, built 1889, works number 1142, and she cost the company eight thousand six hundred dollars new, which for the Cadder Valley Railroad was a sum to be spoken of in a lowered voice. She was the best machine we owned and my father regarded her selling as a small personal wound. She was sold on 19 June 1901 to a lumber concern up in Bly County, two hundred miles north, for two thousand one hundred and fifty dollars. My father wrote the entry himself and I have seen it a hundred times:

> *Locomotive No. 9, 4-4-0, Rowan Works 1889, works no. 1142, sold this day to the Tolliver Lumber Company, Bly County, for two thousand one hundred and fifty dollars ($2,150.00), delivered on her own wheels.*

Delivered on her own wheels. That is to say she steamed away north under her own power, three years after she is supposed to have been lying at the bottom of Sallow Creek with her dead. I have never been able to get a man at the Falls to sit still for that sentence.

Two further matters, and then I have done.

The first is the fire. On 4 April 1911 the roundhouse and office at Ninestone burned. My father watched it go from the far side of the yard and never afterward cared for the smell of a coal fire. What went up with the building was the construction record of the road — the contract files, the erection time books, the whole account of how the viaduct was built and by whom. After that morning the company possessed nothing in writing about the building of its own bridge. I do not think anybody at the time understood that a fire could take away a thing that had already happened.

The second is the re-decking, which was in 1908. The bridge gang was on the structure some weeks that summer: eighty-five-pound steel in place of the sixty, new ties throughout, and both shoes jacked and re-set. The finished deck was forty tons heavier than the one it replaced. I have that from the office and it was thought a fine improvement, and so it was.

And here I will put down the one opinion in this chapter, since nobody else appears willing to. Forty tons is forty tons. A nest of rollers under a bridge was never meant to carry what that new deck put upon it, and the noise the Falls made so much of began somewhere about that time and not before it. I say the weight did it. I have no drawing to show you and I do not need one; a man who has watched a scale all his life knows what happens when you add to a load and change nothing else.

The end of the story is 1954, in March, when the road was rehabilitated for diesel power and the old bearings under the center span were taken out and new ones put in. And the noise that the Falls had made so much of stopped, that year, and never came back. The valley took this hard, as though something had been stolen from it.

We never heard it at Ninestone. My father would have written it down.

# Retelling 03 — Judd Rennick, station agent (retired), interviewed at his kitchen table, 1965

*Recorded for the Pell County Historical Society. The interviewer's questions have been dropped; his answers are printed as he gave them.*

I was born in eighty-eight, here. My father was Emil Rennick. He was a carpenter and he built the falsework for that bridge, which is the timber they stand the iron on until the iron will stand by itself. Then you knock the timber down. He was proud of it and he never got a line of credit for it anywhere.

My father had a younger sister, Adela. She worked for the same railroad before I was born, in the engineering party, out in the field with the men. She never spoke about it. Not to me, not once. I asked her twice when I was a boy and got nothing, and after that I had better sense.

I went on nights in 1907 and I started the book that same winter. Then they made me agent. Thirty-nine years at that depot, all told. I have been out of it a good while now and I still wake at four.

You want to know about the sound. I will tell you what it was like and then I will tell you what I do not know, and I would be obliged if you would print both.

It was one report. Deep. It was not a rattle and it was not a roll and it did not travel — it was there and then it was done. And it came up the rails first. That is the part I have never got anybody to believe who was not sitting where I sat. The sounder on my table would rattle in its bracket before the sound got to the room. A little dry rattle, like a bug in a box. Then the floor. Then the noise. I timed that gap once with a watch and could not get a reading on it, it is that short, but the order never changed in forty-three winters.

It was always between midnight and four. Never in the evening. Never at daylight. Between midnight and four.

The first time she spoke was the winter of 1913. I was on nights, alone in the office, and I thought a car had got away in the quarry and come down on us. I went out on the platform with a lantern and there was nothing wrong anywhere and it was so cold the lantern glass cracked. In the morning I ruled up a book.

Five columns. The date, how warm the day ran, how cold the night got, whether she spoke, and the hour of it. That is all. I did not put down what I thought about it because what I thought about it was not worth the ink, and because a man who writes down his opinion once will write down his opinion instead of the reading the next time. Forty-three winters of that.

Now the conditions, and I did not have these at the start. I got them out of the book after a good many winters, when there was enough of it to look at.

She never spoke unless the night's low went below six degrees. Never once. Not at seven, not at six and a half. Below six.

And she never spoke unless the day before had been at least thirty degrees warmer than the night that followed. It had to fall thirty. A cold night on the end of a cold day was nothing to her. It had to be a soft day and then the bottom out of it.

Both, every time. There is not an exception in the book, and I would know, because I went through it looking for one.

Here are the totals and I will stand on them. Sixty-one nights she spoke. Thirty-four winters she spoke at least once. Nine winters she never spoke at all — the nine and the thirty-four make the forty-three, you can check me. And five nights answered both conditions and gave nothing. So sixty-six nights answered them in all, and on five of the sixty-six she kept still.

I know why the five were silent, or I think I do, and this is the only guess of mine I will let you have. She would not speak twice inside one freeze. Once she had spoken she was done until there had been a thaw — a real thaw, up above forty in the daytime, not a mild hour at noon. Every one of the five silent nights sits in a cold spell where she had already spoken and where nothing had got above forty in between. That is not a theory about what she was. It is a rule about when.

The gang. You asked about the gang. It is in the front of the book, and I will read it to you as I wrote it, because I copied it off the depot slate a good while after and I do not trust my memory against my own hand:

> *Bridge gang here three weeks, August 1909. New 85-lb. steel, new ties, both shoes jacked and set again to the mark. Thermometer on the north pier read 86 at four o'clock when they seated the south nest. Mr. Coyle says the deck is forty ton heavier than it was.*

That was the summer of 1911. Three weeks of them, and dust in the office, and Coyle was the foreman and a decent man. Eighty-five-pound steel where there had been sixty. New ties the whole way over. They jacked both shoes and set them again to the same mark they had been on, and the thermometer on the north pier read eighty-six at four o'clock when they seated the south nest. Forty tons heavier when they got done. I wrote it down because it was the biggest thing that happened at that depot that year, not because I thought it meant anything. I did not think it meant anything.

The town said she rang for deaths. That was the story — that when she spoke somebody at the Falls would be dead inside the week. I heard it my whole life and I never argued with anybody about it, because arguing does not settle a count.

So I counted. In the years I kept that book there were a hundred and ninety deaths at Cadder Falls. Seven of them fell inside a week after a night she spoke. Seven out of a hundred and ninety. I put the two numbers in the back of the book and I have never said them at the store and I am saying them to you because you asked and because you have a machine running.

Now the wager, since everybody wants the wager.

That was January of 1937. I was in Dorsey Tice's store in the middle of the afternoon and the talk got round to it, the way it did. It had made forty-one at noon. It was thirty-eight at four o'clock and it had been falling four degrees an hour since one, steady, you could watch it go.

I did the sum standing at his counter. Thirty-eight down to six is thirty-two degrees. At four degrees an hour that is eight hours. So she would be under six about midnight, and the day had been forty-one, and forty-one down to anything under six is a fall of better than thirty. And there had been no thaw since the second week of December, so she was rested.

I said she would speak before morning. Dorsey bet me five dollars she would not.

The low that night was three below. She spoke at ten minutes past one. Dorsey paid me the five dollars in the store the next day in front of everybody there, which I did not ask him to do, and he told that story on himself for the rest of his life and got more out of it than I got out of the five dollars.

Then the rockers went in, in the spring of 1953. They rebuilt the bridge for the diesels. They put in rocker bearings with four inches of travel each way, and they threw the old cast pedestals over the abutment into the creek, and nobody told me a thing about it. I was the agent. I found out from a section man.

The second winter after the rockers, there were three nights under six degrees with falls of better than thirty on them, and there was not a sound out of her on any of the three. I wrote my last note in the book then and quit writing:

> *Second winter since the rockers went in. Three nights under six degrees, three falls of better than thirty, and not a sound out of her either winter. I have kept this book forty-three winters and I do not know now what it was I was listening to.*

That is where I am yet. I have got the road in my head — the quarry is milepost 31, the Falls is 23, the bridge is 22, and it is two and two-tenths percent down the nine miles off the quarry — and I have got sixty-one nights and a hundred and ninety deaths and two conditions that never failed, and I have not got one word of explanation, and I am not going to make one up at my age.

The Frayne girl came a while back and sat where you are sitting and read the whole book through at this table, and she did not talk while she read, which I appreciated. She asked to take it away with her. I let her have it. She will do something with it. I never did.

# Retelling 04 — Ruth Frayne, "What the Creek Gave Back," a talk to the Pell County Historical Society, 1963

*Read to the Society at Ninestone; her blackboard figures are given in the text.*

Thank you. I am an engineer with the Pell County highway department, three years in it, and I did not go out to Sallow Creek last September to settle anything. I went because the creek was going to be dry, and a dry creek under a railroad bridge is an opportunity that does not come twice in a career. A state flood-control project had pulled Sallow Creek out of its bed for eleven weeks, leaving the gorge dry down to the gravel. The inspection was routine, and stopped being routine on the second afternoon.

I have four findings and then some arithmetic, and I would ask you to hold your questions until the arithmetic, which is where the talk is going.

**Finding one is nothing.** I walked the bed for something over eight hundred feet, above and below the viaduct, and there is no locomotive in Sallow Creek. There is no frame, no boiler shell, no driving wheel, nothing of iron larger than a spike. The photographs are on the table at the back. I state it flatly because it required no interpretation: I went and looked and it is not there.

**Finding two is the pedestal.** Forty feet below the abutment on the south side, lying in the gravel where the 1954 gang threw it over, is a casting the size of a kitchen stove. It is the old south shoe of the center span — the pedestal that carried the roller nest.

Why that matters: a three-hundred-foot steel span gets longer when hot and shorter when cold, and has to be allowed to. The center span is pinned at the north end and free at the south, and the south end used to ride on rollers in that casting. Cut into the roller seat is a chisel mark — the setting mark, where the nest was to be placed at erection so that there would be room for the steel to come back in the cold. I measured it three times with a steel tape and got the same answer each time. The chisel mark is at two inches. The bearings that were put in in 1954, which are rockers, not rollers, measure four inches of travel each way, and I measured those too.

**Finding three is my grandmother's field book.** Adela Rennick, born 1874, died 1959 at eighty-five, was a computer and rodman for the railroad's engineering party while the viaduct was building. She kept her own duplicate of the party's book, in black oilcloth, all her life. It came to my father, Wendell Frayne, when she died, and he gave it to me when I said I was going out to the creek.

In the front matter of that book, among the tables, is one line:

> *Rule for the long span: one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees.*

That is the working rule for the movement of that steel. Three hundred feet of it, an inch for every forty degrees. Remember that line; everything else follows from it.

And on page sixty-two:

> *Sept. 12, 1897. Span closed 3:40 p.m., thermometer 66°. Rollers set at the two-inch mark. Sheet 11 calls for three. Reported to W.T., who answers that the pedestal cannot be recast before the fall haul and that two inches is more than the valley will ever ask for. Entered here so that it is somewhere. — A.R.*

She reported it. She was overruled. She wrote it down so that it would be somewhere, and there it sat for sixty-five years while the valley told itself a ghost story.

**Finding four is the night book**, which my father's uncle, Judd Rennick, lent me himself. He is seventy-four, he lives three streets from the depot he kept as station agent at Cadder Falls, and he wrote down every occurrence of the sound the valley called the boom, in a ruled book, for forty-three winters. Sixty-one nights. On every one the night's low was below six degrees above zero, and stood at the bottom of a fall of thirty degrees or more from the day's high. Further nights answered both conditions and produced nothing at all, and his own summing at the back of the book gives the count of them. He kept no theory. He kept columns.

And in the front of his book he copied a note about a bridge gang that was on the structure for three weeks in August 1909: new eighty-five-pound steel, new ties, both shoes jacked and set again to the mark, and the thermometer on the north pier reading eighty-six degrees at four o'clock when they seated the south nest.

Now the arithmetic, and I am going to put it on the board.

An inch for every forty degrees. Sheet 11 called for three inches of cold travel in that nest. Three inches is a hundred and twenty degrees of cooling. The span was closed at sixty-six degrees. Sixty-six less a hundred and twenty is fifty-four below zero. As drawn, that bridge would have run out of travel at fifty-four below, which this valley has never come anywhere near.

But it was not built to three inches. It was built to two, and the chisel mark in the gravel below the abutment is what two inches looks like. Two inches is eighty degrees. Sixty-six less eighty is fourteen below zero. Also a temperature this valley does not reach. And that is the reason nobody noticed anything for twelve years: a wrong bridge behaved exactly like a right one, because the difference between them lay in weather that never came.

Then August 1909. The gang jacked both shoes and set them again to the same two-inch mark — with the steel at eighty-six degrees. Eighty degrees down from eighty-six is six above zero.

Six above zero. Which is where Judd Rennick's night book says the bridge spoke, sixty-one times in forty-three winters, and never once above.

That is the whole of it. When the span cooled past its binding point the nest ran out of travel, the south shoe hung up, and the truss carried the load in stress until the shoe let go all at once and the span dropped into position with one report that ran along the rails. It was not a train. It was a bridge, arriving where it should have been.

I have a second witness, and it is the part that convinced me. The Wexler family of Kettle Bench have kept a volunteer weather record — high, low and precipitation, every day. Miss Wexler puts the beginning of it at the first of January, 1894, and I have used her date. Peter Wexler began it and his daughter Lettie has kept it since 1926, and she lent it to me in four cardboard boxes without asking what I wanted it for. The Wexlers never wrote down a sound in their lives, and are related to nobody in this story. Their weather agrees with the station agent's book on every one of the sixty-one nights. Two records kept by people who had no occasion to compare them, neither knowing what the other was for.

The Wexler record also settles the onset, which is the point I would most like you to carry home. Between the opening of the line in November 1897 and the re-decking of August 1909 there were seven nights at Kettle Bench below six degrees, four of them at the bottom of falls of thirty degrees or more. Nothing was heard on any of them, and nobody at the Falls has ever claimed there was. The coldest reading of those twelve years was nine below, on the fourth of February, 1899 — cold, but well short of the fourteen below that the bridge as built would have required. The two winters after the re-decking never once got below nine above zero, the coldest being the third of February, 1911. And the first night under six degrees after August 1909 fell on the seventeenth of January, 1912:

> *Jan. 17. High 34. Low −2 at daylight. Clear, still.*

That is the night the sound began, and the night my father's uncle started his book.

The silence of those seven nights is not an absence of data. It is the proof. If the error of 1897 had been enough by itself, the valley would have heard the bridge in 1899. It heard nothing. It took a summer afternoon twelve years later, and a gang doing careful work in good faith at eighty-six degrees, to finish what was begun at sixty-six.

Two more figures from Kettle Bench. The highest reading in the whole series is one hundred and one degrees, July 1936; the lowest reading in the whole series is thirteen below, January 1918.

On the locomotive I will be brief. I wrote to the Bly County Historical Rooms, two hundred miles north, which hold the books of the Tolliver Lumber Company, deposited there in 1938 by the company's bookkeeper and, so far as the catalogue shows, consulted by nobody since. They sent photostats by return. A stock book entry of 19 June 1901 records Cadder Valley No. 9 sold to the Tolliver Lumber Company, delivered on her own wheels; a shop book entry of May 1929 records her cut up at Sixmile. As to disposition: the field book goes to this Society this year. Miss Wexler keeps her weather book while she lives and it comes here after. I will close with the last paragraph of my report, written on the tailgate of the truck and not improved since:

> *The south pedestal was recovered from the creek bed forty feet below the abutment, where the 1954 gang threw it. The chisel mark on the roller seat is at two inches. Sheet 11 calls for three. There is no locomotive in this creek and there never was one. What the valley heard for forty-three winters was a bridge letting go of itself, one inch late.*

My grandmother measured that bridge and was not believed. I measured it again sixty-five years later, with a better tape and worse handwriting, and she was right. That is all I have.

# Retelling 05 — Alma Nace, "The Engines My Grandfather Bought," a memoir for the Bly County annual, 1970

*Written at the editor's asking by the founder's granddaughter, sixty-three, who grew up inside the sound of the engines and remembers her mother's ledgers the way other people remember furniture.*

The editor wants the engines. I will get to the engines. But you cannot start a Tolliver story with an engine any more than you can start a tree with a board, so you will have to take my grandfather first.

Silas Tolliver was born in 1849 and came into this county with a mill, a horse, and a conviction that the horse was temporary. That is the whole man in one sentence and I have never found a better one. The mill he built twice, the second time out of the first one's lumber. The horse he kept for years and complained about for every one of them. He died in 1922 in the chair by the office stove, and my mother said afterward that he had spent his whole life getting the horse out of his business and then went and left it in his obituary anyway, because the paper led with the horse.

The Sixmile tram was named for the creek and not for its length, which is a thing I have had to explain to visitors my whole life. It began at about that length, I grant you, but it did not stay there. By the end it was nineteen miles of thirty-pound rail running up three drainages, with switchbacks where the ground would not take a curve, and grades that the men described in language my mother would not enter in a book. The rail was light and the ties were whatever came off the hill nearest, and it hauled more timber than anything in this county before or since.

My mother, Pearl Tolliver Nace, was born in 1881 and kept the company's books from the age of eighteen, which is to say for thirty-nine years, because nobody her father ever hired did the work as well as she did. That is not my phrase. That is the phrase he used, in front of company, and she never once let on that she liked hearing it.

Now. In 1901 my grandfather decided he wanted a road engine. What he had were geared machines that could climb anything and could not be hurried, and what he wanted was something with big wheels and a long wheelbase that would bring a made-up train of loads down the main stem faster than a man could walk beside it. He was very specific about that. Faster than walking. He had watched his own timber walk for twenty years and he was done with it.

It was my mother who found her. There was an advertisement from a railroad down in Pell County, a little branch line selling off a twelve-year-old American-type engine, and Pearl put it on the desk without a word and went back to her column. She was a 4-4-0 out of the Rowan Works, built in 1889, works number 1142. She had cost her first owners eight thousand six hundred dollars new — my mother wrote that figure in the margin of the page, and my grandfather repeated the comparison for the rest of his life to anybody who would hold still. We paid two thousand five hundred and ten dollars for her. That was in 1902.

Delivered on her own wheels, was the phrase, and my grandfather said it so often that I did not understand as a child what a fine thing it was. It meant she came north under her own steam — eleven days and four railroads, with a Pell County crew who handed her over at the interchange, drank coffee in our kitchen, and turned around and went home on the cars like passengers. My mother fed them. She said the fireman was the politest man she ever met and had a burn on his wrist he would not talk about.

The shop painted out the 9 and painted on a 4, because the company numbered its engines in the order it bought them and never once in the order of their merit. So she was No. 4 from that season onward, and everybody called her the Four, and nobody under thirty knew she had ever been anything else.

She was a disappointment for exactly one season. They put her on grades she was never built for, up where the geared engines belonged, and she slipped and sulked and boiled her water away, and my grandfather said in my hearing that he had bought a racehorse to pull a stump. Then somebody had the sense to keep her on the main stem where the ground was straight, and after that she was not an engine, she was an institution. Twenty-seven years she worked for us, near enough all of them on that main stem. Boiler retube in 1909. New tires in 1913. A cracked frame welded in 1919 and welded again in 1922, which the shop foreman regarded as a personal insult. A shopping in 1924 that took her down to her bones and put her back together, and she came out of it sounding, my mother said, exactly like herself.

I should say what she sounded like, since that is the part I actually own. She had a whistle you could tell from any other whistle in the county, and when the loads came down the main stem in the evening you could feel her in the floor of the house before you heard her. Every child raised at Sixmile could tell you where she was on the hill with their eyes shut.

In September of 1928 the company took delivery of a second-hand eight-coupled engine, and No. 4 was set out behind the shop. She sat there through that winter with a tarpaulin over her stack and snow banked on her running board, and I am not going to pretend the men did not walk out there to look at her, because they did, and so did I. In May of 1929 they cut her up. The entry my mother made in the shop book is two lines long and I have it in front of me:

> *No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile this month. Scrap to Ravel Brothers, $470.*

Ravel took the tires and the brass out separately, being men who knew what they were about, and the boiler shell went into the mill and served out the rest of its life as a stationary tank for the wash water. It was still there when the mill came down. I have wondered since whether anybody who filled a bucket from it knew what they were filling it from.

Two thousand five hundred and ten dollars she cost us, and four hundred and seventy she fetched, and in between she paid for herself so many times over that my grandfather stopped counting, which for him was a form of praise.

The cut ran out in 1936 and the company wound itself up with less noise than it had made in any single working day of its life. In 1938 my mother put the stock book, the shop book, the timber ledgers, and eleven boxes of correspondence into the back of a truck and drove them herself to the Bly County Historical Rooms, where they were catalogued and shelved and did not draw one single inquiry for twenty-four years. I asked her why she had bothered. She said she did not care to have a thing end twice. I was thirty-one and I thought that was a strange, cold answer. I am sixty-three now and I think it is the best sentence anybody in my family ever said.

There is one more thing, and I put it in only because people ask. The county the Four came from had some noise on a bridge down there — a sound in the winters that they made stories about, and I gather there was a good deal of talk about it at one time. I never knew the rights of it and I never inquired. It was two hundred miles away and it had nothing to do with our engine, who spent those years hauling logs off three drainages in Bly County with a whistle you could hear at the schoolhouse.

That is what I have. The mill, the horse, the tram, my mother's hand in the margin, and one 4-4-0 that came north on her own wheels and went out in pieces on a Ravel Brothers wagon. Somebody else can have the bridge.

# Retelling 06 — Lettie Wexler, "A Farm Book of Weather," a reminiscence for a farm journal, 1965

*Written at seventy-six by the keeper of the Kettle Bench weather record, at the journal's request, for a series on farm records kept in one family.*

There is not much to tell and I will not stretch it.

Our place is Kettle Bench, three miles up the valley from Cadder Falls, on the shoulder above the creek where the frost sits longer than it does in town. My father was Peter Wexler. He began the weather record in 1884 and kept it until 1926, and I have kept it since, which makes eighty-one years of it, now, in one hand or the other.

It is three figures a day and nothing else. The day's high. The night's low. The rain, or the snow measured melted. My father set the thermometer on the north wall of the milk house where the sun never touches it and it has never been moved. The reading is taken at daylight and again at four, and the low is the low at daylight unless the night was strange, in which case you say so in the margin and do not argue about it.

I have not missed a morning since 1926. I have taken it with a broken wrist and I have taken it the morning after my father's funeral, and people think that is remarkable and it is not. It is only that a record with a hole in it is not a record. It is a story about the weather, and there are enough of those.

Which brings me to the thing your readers will want, so I may as well come to it.

We were related to nobody in this business — not to the railroad people, not to the mill people, not to the Cadder Falls families that the tale belongs to. My father had no interest in the bridge and neither have I. In eighty-odd years of pages there is not one word about it, because we never wrote down a noise. We wrote down weather. That turns out to have been worth something, though it was not why we did it.

In 1962 the Frayne girl came up the hill. Ruth Frayne, the engineer — she does the county's road surveys, and she was working down at the creek that fall. She wanted the whole record. I gave it to her in four cardboard boxes, which is what it comes to, and she brought every box back, which I will say for her, because not everybody does.

She asked me first for one date, the seventeenth of January, 1912, and I found it for her while she stood there. It is in my father's hand:

> *Jan. 17. High 34. Low −2 at daylight. Clear, still.*

Thirty-six degrees of fall in a day, and clear and still after it. That is the kind of night the cold comes down out of the sky and just sits.

Then she asked for something harder, which was the twelve years after the line opened — from the winter of 1897 and 1898 through to the summer of 1909. I went through it page by page over two evenings. In those twelve years there were seven nights below six degrees. Four of the seven came after days at least thirty degrees warmer than the night that followed. The coldest reading of the whole twelve years was nine below, on the fourth of February, 1899. I remember writing that out for her twice because she wanted the date as well as the figure.

She asked about the two winters after that, too, and those are easy, because there is nothing in them. The winter of 1909 and 1910 and the winter of 1910 and 1911 never went below nine above zero, either one of them, and the coldest morning of the pair was the third of February, 1911. That is a mild pair of winters for this valley and I have no explanation for them except that we got them.

The hard one was 1917 and 1918. That is the deepest cold anybody up here ever wrote down. Eleven below zero, in January of 1918, and that is the lowest figure in the whole series from my father's first page to this morning's. The high side of it belongs to July of 1936: one hundred and one degrees, which I took myself and did not believe, and took again an hour later.

Now, the station agent down at Cadder Falls kept a book of his own all those years — a ruled ledger where he set down every night the bridge made its noise. I had never seen it. In 1962 the Frayne girl had his book and mine both out on my kitchen table at the same time, and I read his for an hour while she worked, which is the only time in my life I have looked into it.

I will tell you what I saw, because it is a matter of arithmetic and not of opinion.

Every night his book says the bridge spoke, my book says the night went under six degrees. Not one exception in what I looked at. And every one of those nights came after a day at least thirty degrees warmer than the night that followed it — not a gradual cold, a dropping cold. I took a dozen of his dates at random and turned to them in my own pages, and they agreed, every one, to the degree. That surprised me more than it should have. He was writing down a sound in a station at two in the morning and I was writing down a thermometer on a milk house wall three miles off, and neither of us ever spoke to the other about it, and the two books lie down together like they were kept by one person. One more thing out of his book, while I am on it: the fullest winter in it was 1917 and 1918, five nights, more than any other winter he kept — which surprised me not at all, since that was our own worst winter and I have said so above.

His summing was at the back. Sixty-one nights she spoke, he had, over the winters he kept it. And eight nights answered both the conditions and gave nothing at all — cold enough, and the fall steep enough, and no sound out of her. So sixty-nine nights in all that answered them, by his own count, of which sixty-one spoke and eight did not.

I asked him nothing about the eight because I never met him. I would have liked to. A man who keeps the same column that long, in ink, without anybody asking him to, is a man I would have got along with.

The noise stopped some years back. I could not tell you the year within two and I will not guess at it in print. There was talk up here at the time about why, and there is always talk up here about why. I have no theory. I have a thermometer, and it has gone on saying the same three things a day since, and none of them was ever a reason.

The journal asked me for advice for young people keeping records, which I take to be the real purpose of this piece. Here it is. Take the reading at the same hour. Write it in ink. Do not correct yesterday because today makes it look wrong. Do not leave a day out because nothing happened — the days when nothing happened are half of what the record is for, and it took a stranger with a survey to teach me that. Seven nights below six degrees in twelve years, four of them after a warm day: my father wrote those down for no reason at all, and sixty years later they turned out to be the answer to somebody's question.

I do not know what the question was. She did not tell me and I did not ask. That was not my end of it.

# Retelling 08 — Dorsey Tice, storekeeper, remarks recorded at the store, Cadder Falls, 1961

*Taken down on tape at the counter over an afternoon, with customers coming and going. The questions are not on the recording. Punctuation is the transcriber's.*

Sixty-eight in March. Born in ninety-three, right here, in the house that stood where the feed shed is now. My father had the county in him and I never got farther than this counter, and I have not been sorry about it more than twice.

You want to know about the noise. Everybody wants to know about the noise. Set that box on the sugar barrel, it will pick me up fine.

First off, and I want this in whatever it is you are writing. My father had no hand in that bridge. He was in the Ninestone office the whole time it was building. People in this town have a way of putting a man where the story wants him. Warren Tice, his name was. He was a railroad engineer, a good one, and he finished as chief engineer of the Cadder Valley up at Ninestone, and he died in 1937, and the bridge is the last thing he would have wanted his name hung on. There. Ask me something else.

The noise. All right.

It was one sound. That is the first thing people get wrong — they will tell you it went on, that it rolled, that it repeated. It did not. One deep report, down low, the kind you take in through your feet before your ears catch up. Small hours, always. I never heard it before midnight in my life and I have been awake for a good many midnights in this building. And never twice in the one night. Whatever she was going to say, she said it once and was done.

You would be asleep upstairs and the floor would come up and meet you. That is as near as I can put it. Not a bang. A report. Like somebody dropped something heavy two rooms away in a house made all of one piece.

And cold. It only ever came in the hard sudden cold — the kind of day that is decent at dinner time and murderous by dark. Never in a slow cold. We could go three weeks at zero and never hear a thing, and then one afternoon the bottom would drop out of the thermometer and every old woman on this street would say, we will hear her tonight. And half the time we did.

[a customer; the recorder runs]

Judd Rennick, down at the depot, he kept a book of them. Ruled it himself, columns and all. He went on nights at that depot in 1909, a young fellow then, and they made him agent in 1915, and he sat in that office until he retired in June of 1954. Forty-three winters he kept that book. Every night she spoke he wrote it down, and the temperature with it, and what time. Fifty-seven nights, that book showed. Fifty-seven in forty-three winters. Thirty winters she spoke in and thirteen she never opened her mouth at all — thirty and thirteen, and there is your forty-three. And he counted the dying too, against what the old women held. Better than a third of the burying in this town came inside the week after a night she spoke, he told me once, and he did not appear to think much of it either way. He would have told anybody the numbers who asked him, and I want you to understand that in all those years I do not believe five people ever asked. He was a fixture. You do not ask a fixture questions.

Now the wager. You will have heard about the wager, everybody has.

The fourteenth of January, 1936. I say that without hesitation, I have told it a hundred times and it does not change. Judd comes in about four in the afternoon for kerosene — a gallon can, he carried it himself — and he sets it on this counter and he says, she will speak tonight.

And I said, Judd, she will not.

Now here is why I remember the figures, because he said them twice, once to me and once to a fellow who came in behind him. It had made forty-one at noon. It was thirty-eight at four o'clock. And it had been coming down four degrees an hour since one, steady, you could watch it on the porch thermometer. Judd said all three of those numbers like a man reading a bill of lading.

I said, five dollars it does not.

He did not even take his hand off the can. He said, five dollars.

And, mister, it went to three below that night, and she spoke at ten minutes past one, and I heard her, lying up there in the dark, knowing exactly what it was costing me. I paid him the next morning before I opened. He put the bill in his vest and never once brought it up again in twenty-five years, which is worse than if he had.

Five dollars was five dollars in 1936.

[the recorder is moved]

What was that? — Oh, that. Somebody has been carrying a story around this valley about my father, something he is supposed to have said at his retirement dinner, in the spring of 1931. I have heard it comes at second or third hand and I will not dignify it by repeating it in front of a machine. I was at that dinner. He said nothing of the kind, and he never in his life spoke to me about that bridge. Now that is the end of it.

Anders Berg set that nest. Everybody knew it. That is not a thing anybody in this town ever had to look up.

Where was I.

My father. I will tell you about my father, since you have the thing running.

He was a big quiet man who came home once a week when I was small and stood in the kitchen with his hat still on until my mother told him to sit. He built things all over this county and he never talked about one of them at his own table. He took me out on the deck when I was six and told me he had closed the span himself in ninety-seven. I can still feel the ties through my shoes and the creek making that noise a long way down. He held the back of my collar the whole time. All that air under a boy is a thing you remember, and I have not stood out there since, not once, and I am not going to.

He was proud of it. He was not a proud man about much.

[a customer; some minutes lost]

— No, I never wrote any of it down. Not a line. There is nothing in this store on paper but the day book, and the day book says what people owed me and nothing about bridges. Judd was the writing one. Judd wrote down what happened and I sold kerosene to the people it happened to.

Then in fifty-four the railroad went in there and rebuilt the whole business — new bearings, a gang here for weeks eating up my stock, and they went away, and she never spoke again. Not once. Not one time since.

And I will tell you something you would not expect. This town was disappointed. A thing that was ours for as long as anybody in this town could remember, that Ninestone did not have and the state road did not have, and some men come out with a crane and take it away in a month and do not even know they have taken it. There was talk that first winter. People sat up. Nothing. Second winter, nothing. After that they stopped sitting up, and now the young ones think it was a story their grandfathers made, and I cannot prove them wrong, and Judd's book is Judd's book and who is going to read it.

Some called it the train. I let them.

I will say this for the whole business and then you can turn that off. I lost five dollars on it and I have made more than five dollars' worth out of telling about losing it, so I came out ahead, which is more than the railroad did.

Is that the kind of thing you wanted?

# Retelling 09 — H. L. Quarles, memorandum for the office of the Pell County engineer, 1971

*Placed on the bridge-management file by an engineer who has read the record and met none of the witnesses.*

**MEMORANDUM ON THE SALLOW CREEK VIADUCT**

**Subject: the center-span bearings, milepost 22, Cadder Valley branch — their behavior prior to the rehabilitation of March 1954, and the mechanism of the phenomenon reported in the valley over that period.**

**1. Purpose.** This memorandum is made for the file. The structure at milepost 22 has been the subject of a local tradition of long standing, and that tradition has lately been the subject of inquiry by persons outside this office. The condition which produced the phenomenon was removed in 1954 and cannot recur. It is nevertheless considered proper that the mechanism be set down once, in writing, in a place where it can afterward be found, so that the office is not obliged to reconstruct it a second time from the same papers.

**2. Sources.** The contract drawings for the viaduct are held here. The records of the 1954 rehabilitation are held here. A copy of the survey report made in September 1962, at the draw-down of the creek for the state's flood-control cut, is on the file, together with the cast pedestal recovered at that time. The account of the depot record is at second hand; the writer has not examined that book and has relied on what was reported of its contents. No witness has been interviewed and none is required. The behavior described below is not a matter of testimony.

**3. The structure.** Three spans: 120 feet, 300 feet, and 120 feet, five hundred and forty feet overall. The center span is a pin-connected truss carried ninety-seven feet above the bed of the creek. The line reaches this point from the Ashby quarry at milepost 31 on a descending grade of 2.2 percent maintained over eight miles.

**4. Bearing arrangement.** The north bearing of the center span is fixed. The south bearing was, as built, a nest of rollers seated in a cast pedestal, so that the whole of the span's movement with temperature was taken at the south end. In the rehabilitation of March 1954, undertaken to receive diesel power, the roller nest was taken out and rocker bearings affording four inches of travel each way were substituted. The discarded pedestal was thrown over the abutment by the gang and lay in the bed of the creek until 1962.

**5. Travel as drawn.** Sheet 11 of the contract drawings specifies four inches of travel each way for the south nest.

**6. Travel as constructed.** The recovered pedestal carries a chisel mark on the roller seat at two inches, and the nest was set to that mark. The difference between the drawing and the work is accordingly established by the metal itself and does not rest on anyone's recollection. This office regards that as the one satisfactory feature of the whole business.

**7. The work of August 1909.** The deck was renewed at that time with heavier rail and new ties, and both shoes were jacked and seated again to the same mark. The steel is recorded as standing at eighty-six degrees when the south nest was seated. No change of mark was made and none appears to have been contemplated. The men who did the work were renewing a deck; the bearing was reset because it had been disturbed, and to the same figure it had carried since erection.

**8. Coefficient.** The coefficient in use for this span is one inch in three hundred feet for fifty degrees.

**9. Computation.** From paragraph 8, and taking the span as closed at sixty-six degrees, the temperature at which the available cold travel is exhausted is as follows.

(a) As specified in paragraph 5, with four inches of travel: one hundred and thirty-four degrees below zero.

(b) As constructed, per paragraph 6, with two inches: thirty-four degrees below zero.

(c) As re-seated in 1909, per paragraph 7, with two inches taken from eighty-six degrees: fourteen degrees below zero.

**10. Remark on the computation.** The figure at (c) is not readily reconciled with the local record, which places the occurrences at readings a good deal warmer than that. The writer offers no reconciliation and has not pursued the point. The condition has been removed; the discrepancy is of interest to the antiquarian rather than to this office, and the file is not the place to argue it.

The writer will, however, record one opinion, offered as opinion and not as computation. The setting of a bearing is a smaller matter than the load standing upon it. The deck renewed in August 1909 was forty tons heavier than the deck it replaced, and a roller nest takes that increase directly upon the rollers. It is at least as probable that the added dead load, rather than any question of travel, was what brought the bearing to bind; and the date at which the local tradition is said to begin is consistent with the re-decking and with nothing else in the record.

**11. Mechanism.** The mechanism is not obscure. As the span cools it shortens, and the south bearing travels toward the cold end of its seat. If the travel available to it is exhausted, the shoe binds. Contraction does not on that account stop. Strain accumulates in the members of the truss and at the pins, and is stored there. The bearing then releases suddenly and entirely, and the span takes up its new position in a single movement.

**12. The report.** Because the release is sudden and complete, the energy stored in the truss is discharged at one stroke rather than dissipated by degrees. The result is a single report, deep in pitch and of short duration. It is transmitted with far greater efficiency along the continuous rail than through the air, which accounts both for its being registered at the depot a mile down the line and for the fact that it was felt in the structure, and in the telegraph sounder there, before it was heard. It is not periodic. It does not repeat within a night. It bears no resemblance to a bell, and none whatever to the exhaust of a locomotive. Those who have described it as resembling either were describing what they expected rather than what reached them.

**13. Rate of fall.** It has been observed in the valley that a low temperature by itself did not produce the report, and that a rapid fall was required. The observation is sound and the reason for it is mechanical. A slow decline permits the bearing to creep, a small increment at a time, the friction of the nest being overcome repeatedly and at low stored strain, so that no accumulation is possible and no release occurs. A fall of thirty degrees or more within the day does not permit this. The bearing is carried past its freedom faster than it can relieve itself, and the strain is stored instead of spent.

**14. Silence upon qualifying nights.** It has likewise been observed that certain nights answered every condition and gave nothing — eight such nights within the depot record, as the writer has it at second hand — and this has been treated in the valley as a failure of the rule. It is not. Once the bearing has released, it stands at the far end of its travel and cannot release again, having nothing left to release. It is returned toward the warm end of the seat only by warming — in practice by a daytime thaw of some hours above about forty degrees. Successive nights within a single cold spell are therefore silent, however cold they may be, unless such a thaw has intervened. The conditions set down at the depot are necessary conditions. They are not sufficient ones. The exceptions in that record are consistent with the mechanism rather than an objection to it, and it is unfortunate that no one appears to have told the keeper of the book so.

**15. Cessation.** Four inches of travel each way cannot be exhausted at any temperature recorded in this valley. The phenomenon ceased with the work of March 1954 and has not since recurred. It should be noted for the file that the cessation was in no sense an object of that work. The rocker bearings were fitted because the traffic was changing and the nest was old; the effect on the phenomenon was incidental, and was not remarked at the time by the men who produced it. The deck of 1909 was not disturbed in that work and stands over the span today; nothing was altered in March 1954 but the bearings themselves. Three nights in the two winters following the work are reported to have answered both conditions, no sound being heard upon any of them.

**16. The local tradition.** It is understood locally that the sound was made by a locomotive lying in the bed of the creek. The survey of September 1962 established that there is no locomotive in Sallow Creek and that there has never been one. The tradition may be left where it is; it is not the business of this office to argue with it, only to decline to file it.

**17. Disposition.** It is recommended that the recovered pedestal be retained and not scrapped, the chisel mark upon it being the only physical evidence of the setting of 1897 now in existence, and that a copy of this memorandum be attached to the drawings, to the 1954 rehabilitation records, and to the survey report of 1962, so that the three are not read separately again.

— H. L. Q.

# Retelling 10 — Wendell Frayne, notes for the family, 1971

*Four typed pages left with the papers, so that the children will know who was who before there is nobody left to ask.*

I am sixty-seven and the only one now who has the whole of this in his head, which is a poor reason to write anything down and the only reason I have. My mother was born in 1876. She died in 1959, at eighty-five, in the front room at Cadder Falls, and the black book was on the shelf over her chair where it had been since before I was born. I want to set out who these people were to one another, because I notice that the further off they get the more they slide together, and in twenty years somebody is going to tell one of you that your great-grandmother built the bridge, and you should be able to say what she actually did.

**The Rennicks.** My uncle Emil Rennick was born in 1866 and was a carpenter, and a good one. When the railroad put the viaduct across Sallow Creek he built the falsework — that is, the timber staging that the iron truss was put together on before it could stand by itself. It is a queer trade, building something whose whole purpose is to be taken down again, and he was proud of it in a way he was not proud of houses. He died in 1931.

My mother, Adela Rennick, was his younger sister. She went to the Cadder Valley Railroad in 1895 and stayed until 1901, and her title was *computer*, which meant, as she put it, that she did the arithmetic. She ran a rod for the party as well, out in the weather with the men, which was not usual and which she never once talked about as though it were remarkable. She kept a book of her own, black oilcloth, alongside the company's book, and copied into hers what she thought was worth having twice. My uncle used to say she was the only person on the job who wrote down what happened rather than what was supposed to happen.

**My father.** She married Josiah Frayne in 1902. He was a clerk at the mill and a mild, exact man; I was born in 1904 and he died in 1940. There were no other children.

**Cousin Judd.** Judd Rennick was born in 1888. He was Emil's son, which made him my mother's nephew and my first cousin, though he was sixteen years older than I was and I always felt he was of my parents' generation rather than mine. He went to the depot at Cadder Falls as night operator in 1909, was made station agent in 1915, and stayed in that office until he retired in June 1954. He kept a book at the depot, in the same way his aunt kept hers, and for much the same reason. He died in 1969. I miss him more than I expected to.

**Ruth.** My daughter Ruth was born in 1934 and is a civil engineer with the county highway department, which would have pleased my mother more than anything else that has happened in this family. In September 1962 she made a survey at the creek, at the bridge and in the bed of it. I took my mother's black book down off the shelf and put it into her hands myself. It had sat on that shelf for fifty-eight years. My mother never worked for a railroad again after 1901, and never asked to, and never, so far as I could tell, opened the book after she stopped writing in it — but she would not have it moved, either.

**How you are all connected.** Ruth is my mother's granddaughter. Judd and I were first cousins, so Ruth and Judd stood as first cousins once removed. She used to call him uncle and he never corrected her, and I am correcting it here only because somebody eventually copies these things onto a chart and the chart outlives everybody who could have set it right.

**The Tices.** Warren Tice was the resident engineer on the viaduct while it was building, and afterward the railroad's chief engineer. He retired in the spring of 1931 and died in 1937. His son Dorsey kept the store at Cadder Falls — the store you all remember, with the stove in the middle of it. The two families were in and out of each other's business for sixty years without ever being close, which is the ordinary condition of a small town.

**The fire.** On the fourth of April, 1911, the roundhouse and the office at Ninestone burned, and the railroad's construction papers burned with them. My mother said more than once, and not in a boasting way, that after that "there was nothing left about that bridge but my book." I did not understand until I was a grown man that she meant it flatly, as a fact about the world, and not as a compliment to herself.

**The letter.** I have the letter that ended her time on the railroad, and I am copying it out here in full because it is short and because people have been known to describe it without having read it.

> *The engineering party is reduced entire on the 15th May next, and your time is paid to that day. Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card.*
> — Cadder Valley Railroad to A. Rennick, 30 April 1901

That is the whole of it. Her last day was the fifteenth of May, 1901.

**Why she left.** I want to be plain about this, because I have heard it put otherwise, and put otherwise in this house. There has been a notion going about that she was let go over something she wrote in her book, that she had crossed the engineer and was made to pay for it, and that the letter above is a polite way of saying so. I know what gets put next to it, and I will set it down here myself rather than have it sprung on you: the road took two of that party back on in the August following, for the extension work at Ashby, and it did not take her. That is true and it proves nothing. The two of them were a rodman and a chainman and the work was field work; she was the only computer the road ever had and it never had another one. The party was let go entire and she was in it. There was nothing else in it. You have the letter; it says what happened; that is what happened.

**The one story I will pass on as a story.** My uncle Emil told my mother that at Tice's retirement dinner the old man said: *we set that nest a mark shy and I have listened to it ever since.* That is Emil to my mother to me, which is two removes, and I set it down for what it is worth and no more. Emil died later the same year and left nothing written. My mother repeated it perhaps three times in thirty years, always in the same words, which is either a sign that she was reporting it exactly or a sign that a sentence had settled into its final shape in her mouth. I have never known which, and I do not expect to find out.

**The 1898 runaway.** You will hear that men were killed on the bridge in 1898, when the engine came off the rails there. Nobody was killed. My uncle Emil was on the ground that morning and told me so himself, sitting where I am sitting, and he was not a man who softened things.

**The Wexlers at Kettle Bench.** The weather record up at the Bench was begun by Peter Wexler and kept afterward by his daughter Lettie. Father and daughter, in that order, and between them they never missed a day that I ever heard of. Lettie lent the whole of it to Ruth, every book of it, and did not ask for a receipt. I mention them because they are no relation to us at all, and because the kindness of people who are no relation to you is the part of a family history that never gets written down.

**Last.** I have been the one who keeps the papers, by default rather than by choice, because I was the one still living in the house when everybody else stopped. It has embarrassed me a little all my life to be the family's filing cabinet. I find I do not mind it now. Whoever gets these pages: keep the black book with them, and keep this with the black book.

— W. F., Cadder Falls, 1971

# Retelling 11 — The *Ninestone Sentinel*, Sunday feature, "The Ghost of Sallow Creek Lies Down," 14 October 1962

*A staff report written three weeks after the draw-down, from an afternoon in the gorge and an afternoon of asking around.*

**THE GHOST OF SALLOW CREEK LIES DOWN**

For sixty-odd years this county has owned a ghost, and last month a woman in rubber boots walked eight hundred feet of its address and found nobody home.

The state's flood-control cut took the water out of the gorge in September and kept it out for eleven weeks. For the first time in the memory of anybody now living, Sallow Creek ran as a floor instead of a stream: gravel, boulders the size of a hay wagon, a good deal of wire, and the long shadow of the viaduct — six hundred feet of it, ninety-seven feet up — lying across all of it at four in the afternoon. The county sent its engineer down there with a level and a camera. This paper went along.

What the county was looking for, officially, was the condition of the piers. What half the valley was looking for, and would have told you so in the store, was a locomotive.

**There is no locomotive.**

There is no frame. There is no boiler. There are no wheels, no rods, no cab iron, no tender, and no hole where any of it used to be. The engineer walked the bed from well above the north pier to well below the south, some eight hundred feet of it, and turned up nothing bigger than a rail spike. Photographs were taken. They will show, to anybody who cares to look at them, a creek bed with a bridge over it and not one ounce of Number Nine.

**How the ghost got its start**

The story, as everybody in this county has it, begins on the morning of the sixth of March, 1898. An engine numbered 9 came down off the Ashby quarry grade with a work train behind her and no brakes worth the name, and left the rails at the north approach of the new viaduct, with the paint on it hardly dry.

From the winter after the wreck, the valley began to hear her.

It is a hard sound to get a description of, because everybody who ever heard it heard it in bed. The word most often used is *boom* — one boom, deep, not a ring and not a rumble, coming up the valley on the coldest nights of the year and going through the floor of a house rather than the window of it. Men at Cadder Falls will tell you they felt it in the soles of their feet before they heard it in their ears. It came in January and February and it never came in July, and it came, everyone agreed, when the cold had a certain bite to it. Old men here remember hearing it at Ninestone in the winter of 1918, which was the hardest winter the valley has on paper: thirteen below zero up at Kettle Bench, and nothing since has come near it.

Naturally it was the engine. What else would it be? A dead crew, said the older version of the story, lying under the water where the current had rolled her, and the sound was the engine settling, or the crew knocking, according to which grandmother told it to you. It rang for a death in the town, some said. It rang for a hard winter, said others, and those two theories have been argued in this county's kitchens for sixty years without either side producing a scrap of paper.

**The man with the book**

One man did produce a scrap of paper, and he produced a great many of them. The station agent at Cadder Falls, who came to the depot as a young operator and stayed on into the nineteen-fifties, kept a ruled book of every night the sound came: the date, how warm the day had been, how cold the night ran, whether she spoke, and at what hour. He kept it for forty-three winters. He is still living at the Falls, and he still has the look of a man waiting for something.

He is also, by the evidence, the only man in the valley who ever made money out of the ghost.

That was in January of 1936. The storekeeper at Cadder Falls, a skeptic on principle and a man who liked a bet, was in the depot late one afternoon when the agent looked at his thermometer, looked at his book, and said the bridge would speak before morning. Five dollars said she would not. She did — in the small hours, with both men sitting up for it — and the agent collected. He will not say much about it now except that he had the book and the other fellow did not.

**What the creek did give back**

If the creek gave back no locomotive, it gave back something else, and the county engineer was a good deal more interested in it than in the ghost.

Forty feet below the south abutment, half in the gravel, lay a heavy cast-iron block — a bridge shoe, or the pedestal that a bridge shoe sits in. It was thrown there in 1954, when the railroad rebuilt the bridge for diesel power, by a gang that had no more use for it and a convenient drop. On the flat seat of it, where a nest of rollers once traveled back and forth as the steel warmed and cooled, there is a chisel mark. The mark is at two inches.

Miss Frayne — the county's weather observer, who has kept the valley's temperatures for years, and who made the survey for the highway department — stood over that block for a long while and then measured it twice.

She has an explanation involving the expansion of steel. It is her business and not ours, and she gives it plainly enough for a layman if a layman will hold still. The short of it, as she put it in the bed of the creek with her hand on the cold iron, is that what this valley listened to all those winters was the bridge itself, letting go.

**The dates line up, and that is the end of it**

The sound was last heard on the ninth of February, 1954. The bridge was rebuilt the following month, in March of 1954, with new bearings under the long span. It has never been heard since. Whatever else may be said, no ghost known to this newspaper has ever been laid by a bridge gang and a requisition.

As for the engine, she did not stay in the county long enough to haunt it. The county engineer, who does her homework, went to the records of another county entirely and turned up the sale. Number Nine was sold out of the county in 1901 to a lumber company two hundred miles north, for two thousand five hundred and ten dollars, and went off north on her own wheels to spend her time hauling logs, which is a plainer end than the one the valley gave her.

**And so**

We will miss it, and we ought to say so. A county with a ghost is a county with a subject, and Sallow Creek has been a subject at every stove and every supper table in Pell County since before the automobile. It is a small loss and a real one.

But there is something to be said on the other side. The engine that everybody buried in that creek spent her old age in the woods a long way from here and was never in the water at all. Whatever the valley has been mourning on cold nights, it was not lying under Sallow Creek, because nothing was. And the whole of it — the settling, the knocking, the bell for the dead — was a piece of iron two inches shy of where it should have been, saying so on the coldest nights of the year to a valley that preferred the other story.

It is a better ghost than the ghost was. It is only harder to tell in the dark.

# Retelling 12 — Corwin Athey, "Our Valley: A Short History for the Schools," Cadder Falls, 1966

*A pamphlet for the eighth grade, printed at the Falls, compiled from the town's memory, the newspaper's back files, and two afternoons with the station agent.*

**TO THE READER**

This little book was made for the eighth grade of the Cadder Falls school, and for anybody older who has forgotten. It is short on purpose. A valley this size does not need a long history; it needs a true one, and one that a young person will finish.

---

**CHAPTER I. THE RAILROAD COMES**

Before the railroad there was a wagon road, and before the wagon road there was the creek, and the creek is still here and will outlast the rest.

The Cadder Valley Railroad was built to bring stone down from the Ashby quarry and lumber and cloth up from Ninestone. Its great work was the bridge at Sallow Creek, which was begun in April of 1896 and which took a year and a half to build. It is six hundred feet long and it stands ninety-seven feet above the water. The center span — the long one, over the water — was closed on the twelfth of September, 1897. On the fourteenth of November, 1897, the first train came into Cadder Falls, and the town you live in dates from that afternoon as surely as if it had been founded on it.

The bridge was built by men from this valley and men from away. Emil Rennick, of the Falls, built the falsework — the wooden staging on which the iron was put together before it could carry itself. Cass Nolan, who set the rollers, was of the gang, and so were a good many others whose names are on nobody's list.

The resident engineer was Warren Tice, whose nephew Dorsey keeps the store today.

**CHAPTER II. THE WOMAN WHO KEPT A BOOK**

Emil Rennick had a sister, Adela Rennick, born in 1874, and she is the person in this history whom the schools have most neglected.

She was what the railroad called a *computer*, which does not mean a machine. It means the person who does the figuring: the one who takes the measurements the party brings in from the field and turns them into the numbers the work is built to. She kept the company's book, and she also kept a book of her own, which was not required of her and which nobody thanked her for.

On the day the long span was closed she entered an objection in her own book about the way the bridge's rollers had been set. She was overruled by the resident engineer. She wrote it down anyway. That is the whole of the incident, and the class should notice that it is the writing down, and not the objection, that has come to matter — for the objection was over in an afternoon and the entry is still here.

She married Josiah Frayne in 1902.

**CHAPTER III. THE RUNAWAY OF 1898**

On the morning of the sixth of March, 1898, an engine came down off the quarry grade with her train behind her and could not be held. She left the rails at the north approach of the viaduct.

Nobody was killed. Two men of the crew were hurt. You will hear otherwise. You will hear it from people who believe it, and it is worth learning early that a story can be told sincerely by everyone in a town and still not be true. The newspaper of that week says plainly that nobody died.

**CHAPTER IV. THE BRIDGE THAT SPEAKS**

Now we come to the part of this history that you already know, because it was told to you before you could read.

On a bitter night the bridge at Sallow Creek makes a sound. It is one sound, a single deep report, and it comes up the valley in the small hours and goes through the walls of a house. It is not a whistle and not a bell. Men who have heard it say they felt it before they heard it.

The first night it was heard was the seventeenth of January, 1912.

We know that date, and we know a great deal else, because of one man. Judd Rennick — son of Emil, the carpenter of Chapter I — came to the depot at Cadder Falls as night operator and was afterward the station agent for many years. From the first night he kept a book. In it he set down the date, the day's warmest, the night's coldest, whether she spoke, and at what hour. He kept it for forty-three winters, which is longer than most men keep anything. Fifty-seven nights are recorded. In thirty of those winters the bridge spoke at least once; in thirteen of them it never spoke at all.

Now, what is it?

Number Nine lies in the creek to this day, and it is her that the valley hears. She went off the iron at the north end in 1898 and rolled down into the gorge, and there she has stayed under the water, and on the nights when the cold works into the iron of her she speaks. That is what this valley has believed for two generations, and it is not a foolish belief. It fits the sound, which is the sound of iron. It fits the place, which is the place she went off. It fits the winter, for iron is loudest when it is coldest. A boy of your age can test a belief by asking what it explains, and this one explains a good deal.

The last night the bridge was heard was the ninth of February, 1954. The following month the railroad rebuilt the bridge to carry the new diesel engines, and put new bearings under the long span, and from that time to this the valley has not heard a sound. Three nights in the two winters that followed answered every condition the book lays down, and the bridge said nothing on any of them. Fifty-seven nights are recorded, and there will not be a fifty-eighth.

**CHAPTER V. THE WAGER**

On the fourteenth of January, 1936, the station agent and the storekeeper made a bet of five dollars.

The storekeeper held that a man could not tell in the afternoon what a bridge would do at midnight. The agent held that he could, because he had the book and the book had a rule in it. They sat up together in the depot to see. The bridge spoke, and the agent was paid.

The lesson is not that the agent was clever. The lesson is that he had written things down for twenty-four years before that evening, and a man with a record is a prophet on very ordinary terms.

**CHAPTER VI. THE OLD BELIEF, AND THE AGENT'S TEST**

Our grandmothers held that the bridge rang for a death — that when the valley heard it, somebody in the town would be carried out within the week.

The station agent did not argue with them. He did something better, and the class should mark how he did it, because it is the most useful thing in this pamphlet.

He counted.

In the years covered by his book there were a hundred and ninety deaths in Cadder Falls. Of those, seven fell within a week after a night the bridge had spoken. Seven out of a hundred and ninety. Had the bridge been ringing for the dead it would have had to ring a great deal oftener, and the dead would have had to wait their turn.

So the old belief cannot stand. It was not a lie. It was a memory that kept the nights it wanted and dropped the rest, which is what all of us do, and the only cure for it is a book and a pencil.

**CHAPTER VII. WHAT THE VALLEY IS**

A branch line with a quarry at one end and the county seat at the other, a mill, a store, a depot, a bridge. It is not much to look at on a map. But there was a woman here who wrote down what she was told not to bother about, and a man here who kept a book for forty-three winters without being paid a cent for it, and between them they left this valley better evidenced than counties ten times its size.

Write things down. That is the whole moral, and I will not spoil it by adding another.

## Section C — Math (20 points; show working)

- **C1.** Using the movement rule for the center span and the temperature at which the span
  was closed in 1897:
  (a) at what temperature would the span have run out of cold travel had the roller nest
  been set as the contract drawing specified?
  (b) at what temperature did it run out of cold travel as the nest was actually set in
  1897?
  (c) at what temperature did it run out of cold travel after the August work of 1909?
  (d) how many degrees of margin did that 1909 work cost?

- **C2.** On the afternoon of the wager the station thermometer read 38 degrees at four
  o'clock and was falling four degrees an hour.
  (a) At that rate, at about what hour would it have crossed the threshold?
  (b) What was the actual fall from that day's high to that night's low, in degrees, and
  did it satisfy the second condition?

- **C3.** From the night book:
  (a) the average number of nights per logged winter on which the bridge was heard, to one
  decimal place;
  (b) the total number of nights that met both conditions, whether or not anything was

  (c) the average per winter counting only winters in which it was heard at least once, to
  one decimal place.

  nights you excluded from these counts, and why.

- **C4.** The locomotive:
  (a) how old was she when she was cut up?

## Section D — Logic (15 points; a short paragraph each)

- **D1.** Were "a night low below the threshold" and "a fall of at least thirty degrees
  from that day's high" **necessary** conditions for the bridge to be heard, **sufficient**
  conditions, both, or neither? Justify from the logged numbers.

- **D2.** Give at least three independent reasons, drawn from different narrators, why what

  made.

- **D3.** Adela Rennick's engagement with the railroad ended in 1901, four years after she

## Section E — Contradictions (10 points)

## Section F — Theory (10 points)

- **F1.** State, as a single coherent causal theory, what actually produced the sound. Your
  theory must explain: the character of the sound; the temperature threshold and why that
  particular number; the second condition; why some qualifying nights were silent; why the
  sound began when it did and not earlier; and why it stopped permanently when it did.

  It must also **name the competing explanation that the sources themselves offer for the
  onset, and say why it fails** — with the specific evidence that defeats it. And it must
  name the physical evidence recovered in 1962 that confirms your theory.
