# Retelling 02 — Harlan Pike, son of the CVR's chief clerk, from a memoir written in 1958

*From "The Company at Ninestone," an unpublished memoir written in his seventies. This chapter, headed "The Sallow Creek Viaduct," is given entire.*

My father was chief clerk of the Cadder Valley Railroad from 1893 to 1919, and he brought the office home with him every night of those twenty-six years. Other boys grew up with a dog. I grew up with a stock book. Our dining table on a Sunday was a place where a man might be asked, quite seriously, whether he thought a figure in a column was one the directors would swallow, and I learned to have an opinion before I learned to shave.

I set this down because there is nobody left who cares to have it right, and because what is said about the Sallow Creek Viaduct at present is mostly rubbish.

Begin with the road, because people who write about the valley never trouble to. The Cadder Valley Railroad was thirty-one miles of it, and no more. Ninestone was milepost 0 — the county seat, the shops, the office, my father's desk with the window on the yard. The Sallow Creek Viaduct stood at milepost 22. Cadder Falls, the mill town, was milepost 23. The Ashby quarry, which was the reason the whole thing existed, was at milepost 31. Everything else the company owned was sidings.

The grade from the quarry down to the viaduct was two and two-tenths percent, and there were nine miles of it. I write that plainly because it is the fact from which most of the trouble in this chapter descends. Two and two-tenths percent for nine miles is a great deal of falling for a small railroad with second-hand brakes, and every man on the property knew it, and the company's answer was to hire careful men and pray for cold weather on the drums.

The viaduct itself was begun in April 1896 and the line was opened to Cadder Falls on 14 November 1897. It was three spans: one hundred and twenty feet, then a pin-connected truss of three hundred feet across the gorge proper, then one hundred and twenty feet again. Five hundred and forty feet in all, and ninety-seven feet above the water. I have stood on the deck of it as a boy with my father's hand in my collar and I can tell you that ninety-seven feet is a great plenty.

Now, the point of the thing, which nobody outside the office ever grasped. Steel moves. It lengthens in the heat and shortens in the cold, and a three-hundred-foot span in a valley that runs from the middle of July to the middle of January moves a good deal. So the center span was pinned solid at its north end and its south end was left free to travel — it rested on a nest of rollers set in a cast pedestal, the south shoe, and the whole span breathed on that nest, out in summer and back in winter, for fifty-odd years.

The rule the engineers worked to was one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees. That is a rule of thumb and they used it as one; you will find it in any office of that period. For our span, being three hundred feet exactly, it came out at an inch for every forty degrees, which is the sort of arithmetic even a clerk's son can carry in his head.

The span was closed on 12 September 1897 with the thermometer at sixty-six degrees. Sheet 11 of the contract drawings called for four inches of travel on the south nest. Four inches was generous for a three-hundred-foot span, and I have always thought it showed a proper caution in whoever drew it.

The resident engineer on the work was Warren Tice, afterward the company's chief engineer at Ninestone and a considerable man in this county. The party's computer was a Miss Adela Rennick. She did the arithmetic, and she did it better than the party did — that is my father's phrase, not mine, and he was not a man who handed out compliments to save postage. She kept her own duplicate book, in her own hand, quite separate from the party's book, which was thought eccentric and was not. Her time with the road ended when the engineering party was reduced. She was also in the habit of entering in that book whatever she thought, including what she thought about the bridge, and one such entry got back to the resident engineer. Tice never forgave that entry, and she was gone inside four years. I have nothing in writing on the point and I do not pretend to. I sat in that office as a boy while men talked over my head, and there are things you know from the temperature of a room.

Then the wreck, which is the whole cause of the valley's foolishness.

On the morning of 6 March 1898 a work train came down off the quarry grade without brakes enough to hold her. The locomotive was No. 9, and she went off the iron at the north approach of the new viaduct at twenty minutes past seven. Nobody was killed. Two of the gang were hurt. It took nine days to rerail her. The *Ninestone Sentinel* of 8 March 1898 had it exactly right, and I copy the notice out because I am tired of arguing with people who have never read it:

> *No lives were lost. Two of the gang are hurt, and the engine is on her side at the north end of the new viaduct, where she will be got up within the fortnight.*

Nobody killed. Not one man. I underline it because within my own lifetime I have heard it said at Cadder Falls, by grown people, that a crew went down into that gorge and is there still, and that the sound the valley used to hear on winter nights was that engine working. There was no crew in the gorge. The engine was not in the gorge. The engine was on her side at the north end, in daylight, with half the county looking at her.

And she did not stay there either. No. 9 was a 4-4-0 out of the Rowan Locomotive Works, built 1889, works number 1142, and she cost the company eight thousand six hundred dollars new, which for the Cadder Valley Railroad was a sum to be spoken of in a lowered voice. She was the best machine we owned and my father regarded her selling as a small personal wound. She was sold on 19 June 1901 to a lumber concern up in Bly County, two hundred miles north, for two thousand one hundred and fifty dollars. My father wrote the entry himself and I have seen it a hundred times:

> *Locomotive No. 9, 4-4-0, Rowan Works 1889, works no. 1142, sold this day to the Tolliver Lumber Company, Bly County, for two thousand one hundred and fifty dollars ($2,150.00), delivered on her own wheels.*

Delivered on her own wheels. That is to say she steamed away north under her own power, three years after she is supposed to have been lying at the bottom of Sallow Creek with her dead. I have never been able to get a man at the Falls to sit still for that sentence.

Two further matters, and then I have done.

The first is the fire. On 4 April 1911 the roundhouse and office at Ninestone burned. My father watched it go from the far side of the yard and never afterward cared for the smell of a coal fire. What went up with the building was the construction record of the road — the contract files, the erection time books, the whole account of how the viaduct was built and by whom. After that morning the company possessed nothing in writing about the building of its own bridge. I do not think anybody at the time understood that a fire could take away a thing that had already happened.

The second is the re-decking, which was in 1908. The bridge gang was on the structure some weeks that summer: eighty-five-pound steel in place of the sixty, new ties throughout, and both shoes jacked and re-set. The finished deck was forty tons heavier than the one it replaced. I have that from the office and it was thought a fine improvement, and so it was.

The end of the story is 1954, in March, when the road was rehabilitated for diesel power and the old bearings under the center span were taken out and new ones put in. And the noise that the Falls had made so much of stopped, that year, and never came back. The valley took this hard, as though something had been stolen from it.

We never heard it at Ninestone. My father would have written it down.
