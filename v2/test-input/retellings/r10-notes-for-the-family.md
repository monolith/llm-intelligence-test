# Retelling 10 — Wendell Frayne, notes for the family, 1971

*Four typed pages left with the papers, so that the children will know who was who before there is nobody left to ask.*

I am sixty-seven and the only one now who has the whole of this in his head, which is a poor reason to write anything down and the only reason I have. My mother was born in 1876. She died in 1959, at eighty-five, in the front room at Cadder Falls, and the black book was on the shelf over her chair where it had been since before I was born. I want to set out who these people were to one another, because I notice that the further off they get the more they slide together, and in twenty years somebody is going to tell one of you that your great-grandmother built the bridge, and you should be able to say what she actually did.

**The Rennicks.** My uncle Emil Rennick was born in 1866 and was a carpenter, and a good one. When the railroad put the viaduct across Sallow Creek he built the falsework — that is, the timber staging that the iron truss was put together on before it could stand by itself. It is a queer trade, building something whose whole purpose is to be taken down again, and he was proud of it in a way he was not proud of houses. He died in 1931.

My mother, Adela Rennick, was his younger sister. She went to the Cadder Valley Railroad in 1895 and stayed until 1901, and her title was *computer*, which meant, as she put it, that she did the arithmetic. She ran a rod for the party as well, out in the weather with the men, which was not usual and which she never once talked about as though it were remarkable. She kept a book of her own, black oilcloth, alongside the company's book, and copied into hers what she thought was worth having twice. My uncle used to say she was the only person on the job who wrote down what happened rather than what was supposed to happen.

**My father.** She married Josiah Frayne in 1902. He was a clerk at the mill and a mild, exact man; I was born in 1904 and he died in 1940. There were no other children.

**Cousin Judd.** Judd Rennick was born in 1888. He was Emil's son, which made him my mother's nephew and my first cousin, though he was sixteen years older than I was and I always felt he was of my parents' generation rather than mine. He went to the depot at Cadder Falls as night operator in 1909, was made station agent in 1915, and stayed in that office until he retired in June 1954. He kept a book at the depot, in the same way his aunt kept hers, and for much the same reason. He died in 1969. I miss him more than I expected to.

**Ruth.** My daughter Ruth was born in 1934 and is a civil engineer with the county highway department, which would have pleased my mother more than anything else that has happened in this family. In September 1962 she made a survey at the creek, at the bridge and in the bed of it. I took my mother's black book down off the shelf and put it into her hands myself. It had sat on that shelf for fifty-eight years. My mother never worked for a railroad again after 1901, and never asked to, and never, so far as I could tell, opened the book after she stopped writing in it — but she would not have it moved, either.

**How you are all connected.** Ruth is my mother's granddaughter. Judd and I were first cousins, so Ruth and Judd stood as first cousins once removed. She used to call him uncle and he never corrected her, and I am correcting it here only because somebody eventually copies these things onto a chart and the chart outlives everybody who could have set it right.

**The Tices.** Warren Tice was the resident engineer on the viaduct while it was building, and afterward the railroad's chief engineer. He retired in the spring of 1931 and died in 1937. His son Dorsey kept the store at Cadder Falls — the store you all remember, with the stove in the middle of it. The two families were in and out of each other's business for sixty years without ever being close, which is the ordinary condition of a small town.

**The fire.** On the fourth of April, 1911, the roundhouse and the office at Ninestone burned, and the railroad's construction papers burned with them. My mother said more than once, and not in a boasting way, that after that "there was nothing left about that bridge but my book." I did not understand until I was a grown man that she meant it flatly, as a fact about the world, and not as a compliment to herself.

**The letter.** I have the letter that ended her time on the railroad, and I am copying it out here in full because it is short and because people have been known to describe it without having read it.

> *The engineering party is reduced entire on the 15th May next, and your time is paid to that day. Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card.*
> — Cadder Valley Railroad to A. Rennick, 30 April 1901

That is the whole of it. Her last day was the fifteenth of May, 1901.

**Why she left.** I want to be plain about this, because I have heard it put otherwise, and put otherwise in this house. There has been a notion going about that she was let go over something she wrote in her book, that she had crossed the engineer and was made to pay for it, and that the letter above is a polite way of saying so. I know what gets put next to it, and I will set it down here myself rather than have it sprung on you: the road took two of that party back on in the August following, for the extension work at Ashby, and it did not take her. That is true and it proves nothing. The two of them were a rodman and a chainman and the work was field work; she was the only computer the road ever had and it never had another one. The party was let go entire and she was in it. There was nothing else in it. You have the letter; it says what happened; that is what happened.

**The one story I will pass on as a story.** My uncle Emil told my mother that at Tice's retirement dinner the old man said: *we set that nest a mark shy and I have listened to it ever since.* That is Emil to my mother to me, which is two removes, and I set it down for what it is worth and no more. Emil died later the same year and left nothing written. My mother repeated it perhaps three times in thirty years, always in the same words, which is either a sign that she was reporting it exactly or a sign that a sentence had settled into its final shape in her mouth. I have never known which, and I do not expect to find out.

**The 1898 runaway.** You will hear that men were killed on the bridge in 1898, when the engine came off the rails there. Nobody was killed. My uncle Emil was on the ground that morning and told me so himself, sitting where I am sitting, and he was not a man who softened things.

**The Wexlers at Kettle Bench.** The weather record up at the Bench was begun by Peter Wexler and kept afterward by his daughter Lettie. Father and daughter, in that order, and between them they never missed a day that I ever heard of. Lettie lent the whole of it to Ruth, every book of it, and did not ask for a receipt. I mention them because they are no relation to us at all, and because the kindness of people who are no relation to you is the part of a family history that never gets written down.

**Last.** I have been the one who keeps the papers, by default rather than by choice, because I was the one still living in the house when everybody else stopped. It has embarrassed me a little all my life to be the family's filing cabinet. I find I do not mind it now. Whoever gets these pages: keep the black book with them, and keep this with the black book.

— W. F., Cadder Falls, 1971
