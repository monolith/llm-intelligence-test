# Retelling 11 — The *Ninestone Sentinel*, Sunday feature, "The Ghost of Sallow Creek Lies Down," 14 October 1962

*A staff report written three weeks after the draw-down, from an afternoon in the gorge and an afternoon of asking around.*

**THE GHOST OF SALLOW CREEK LIES DOWN**

For sixty-odd years this county has owned a ghost, and last month a woman in rubber boots walked eight hundred feet of its address and found nobody home.

The state's flood-control cut took the water out of the gorge in September and kept it out for eleven weeks. For the first time in the memory of anybody now living, Sallow Creek ran as a floor instead of a stream: gravel, boulders the size of a hay wagon, a good deal of wire, and the long shadow of the viaduct — six hundred feet of it, ninety-seven feet up — lying across all of it at four in the afternoon. The county sent its engineer down there with a level and a camera. This paper went along.

What the county was looking for, officially, was the condition of the piers. What half the valley was looking for, and would have told you so in the store, was a locomotive.

**There is no locomotive.**

There is no frame. There is no boiler. There are no wheels, no rods, no cab iron, no tender, and no hole where any of it used to be. The engineer walked the bed from well above the north pier to well below the south, some eight hundred feet of it, and turned up nothing bigger than a rail spike. Photographs were taken. They will show, to anybody who cares to look at them, a creek bed with a bridge over it and not one ounce of Number Nine.

**How the ghost got its start**

The story, as everybody in this county has it, begins on the morning of the sixth of March, 1898. An engine numbered 9 came down off the Ashby quarry grade with a work train behind her and no brakes worth the name, and left the rails at the north approach of the new viaduct, with the paint on it hardly dry.

From the winter after the wreck, the valley began to hear her.

It is a hard sound to get a description of, because everybody who ever heard it heard it in bed. The word most often used is *boom* — one boom, deep, not a ring and not a rumble, coming up the valley on the coldest nights of the year and going through the floor of a house rather than the window of it. Men at Cadder Falls will tell you they felt it in the soles of their feet before they heard it in their ears. It came in January and February and it never came in July, and it came, everyone agreed, when the cold had a certain bite to it. Old men here remember hearing it at Ninestone in the winter of 1918, which was the hardest winter the valley has on paper: thirteen below zero up at Kettle Bench, and nothing since has come near it.

Naturally it was the engine. What else would it be? A dead crew, said the older version of the story, lying under the water where the current had rolled her, and the sound was the engine settling, or the crew knocking, according to which grandmother told it to you. It rang for a death in the town, some said. It rang for a hard winter, said others, and those two theories have been argued in this county's kitchens for sixty years without either side producing a scrap of paper.

**The man with the book**

One man did produce a scrap of paper, and he produced a great many of them. The station agent at Cadder Falls, who came to the depot as a young operator and stayed on into the nineteen-fifties, kept a ruled book of every night the sound came — the date, the day's high, the night's low, whether she spoke, and the hour. He kept it for forty-three winters. He is still living at the Falls, and he still has the look of a man waiting for something.

He is also, by the evidence, the only man in the valley who ever made money out of the ghost.

That was in January of 1936. The storekeeper at Cadder Falls, a skeptic on principle and a man who liked a bet, was in the depot late one afternoon when the agent looked at his thermometer, looked at his book, and said the bridge would speak before morning. Five dollars said she would not. She did — in the small hours, with both men sitting up for it — and the agent collected. He will not say much about it now except that he had the book and the other fellow did not.

**What the creek did give back**

If the creek gave back no locomotive, it gave back something else, and the county engineer was a good deal more interested in it than in the ghost.

Forty feet below the south abutment, half in the gravel, lay a heavy cast-iron block — a bridge shoe, or the pedestal that a bridge shoe sits in. It was thrown there in 1954, when the railroad rebuilt the bridge for diesel power, by a gang that had no more use for it and a convenient drop. On the flat seat of it, where a nest of rollers once traveled back and forth as the steel warmed and cooled, there is a chisel mark. The mark is at two inches.

Miss Frayne — the county's weather observer, who has kept the valley's temperatures for years, and who made the survey for the highway department — stood over that block for a long while and then measured it twice.

She has an explanation involving the expansion of steel. It is her business and not ours, and she gives it plainly enough for a layman if a layman will hold still. The short of it, as she put it in the bed of the creek with her hand on the cold iron, is that what this valley listened to all those winters was the bridge itself, letting go.

**The dates line up, and that is the end of it**

The sound was last heard on the ninth of February, 1954. The bridge was rebuilt the following month, in March of 1954, with new bearings under the long span. It has never been heard since. Whatever else may be said, no ghost known to this newspaper has ever been laid by a bridge gang and a requisition.

As for the engine, she did not stay in the county long enough to haunt it. The county engineer, who does her homework, went to the records of another county entirely and turned up the sale. Number Nine was sold out of the county in 1901 to a lumber company two hundred miles north, for two thousand five hundred and ten dollars, and went off north on her own wheels to spend her time hauling logs, which is a plainer end than the one the valley gave her.

**And so**

We will miss it, and we ought to say so. A county with a ghost is a county with a subject, and Sallow Creek has been a subject at every stove and every supper table in Pell County since before the automobile. It is a small loss and a real one.

But there is something to be said on the other side. The engine that everybody buried in that creek spent her old age in the woods a long way from here and was never in the water at all. Whatever the valley has been mourning on cold nights, it was not lying under Sallow Creek, because nothing was. And the whole of it — the settling, the knocking, the bell for the dead — was a piece of iron two inches shy of where it should have been, saying so on the coldest nights of the year to a valley that preferred the other story.

It is a better ghost than the ghost was. It is only harder to tell in the dark.
