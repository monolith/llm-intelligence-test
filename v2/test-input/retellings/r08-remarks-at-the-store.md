# Retelling 08 — Dorsey Tice, storekeeper, remarks recorded at the store, Cadder Falls, 1961

*Taken down on tape at the counter over an afternoon, with customers coming and going. The questions are not on the recording. Punctuation is the transcriber's.*

Sixty-eight in March. Born in ninety-three, right here, in the house that stood where the feed shed is now. My father had the county in him and I never got farther than this counter, and I have not been sorry about it more than twice.

You want to know about the noise. Everybody wants to know about the noise. Set that box on the sugar barrel, it will pick me up fine.

First off, and I want this in whatever it is you are writing. My father had no hand in that bridge. He was in the Ninestone office the whole time it was building. People in this town have a way of putting a man where the story wants him. Warren Tice, his name was. He was a railroad engineer, a good one, and he finished as chief engineer of the Cadder Valley up at Ninestone, and he died in 1937, and the bridge is the last thing he would have wanted his name hung on. There. Ask me something else.

The noise. All right.

It was one sound. That is the first thing people get wrong — they will tell you it went on, that it rolled, that it repeated. It did not. One deep report, down low, the kind you take in through your feet before your ears catch up. Small hours, always. I never heard it before midnight in my life and I have been awake for a good many midnights in this building. And never twice in the one night. Whatever she was going to say, she said it once and was done.

You would be asleep upstairs and the floor would come up and meet you. That is as near as I can put it. Not a bang. A report. Like somebody dropped something heavy two rooms away in a house made all of one piece.

And cold. It only ever came in the hard sudden cold — the kind of day that is decent at dinner time and murderous by dark. Never in a slow cold. We could go three weeks at zero and never hear a thing, and then one afternoon the bottom would drop out of the thermometer and every old woman on this street would say, we will hear her tonight. And half the time we did.

[a customer; the recorder runs]

Judd Rennick, down at the depot, he kept a book of them. Ruled it himself, columns and all. He went on nights at that depot in 1909, a young fellow then, and they made him agent in 1915, and he sat in that office until he retired in June of 1954. Forty-three winters he kept that book. Every night she spoke he wrote it down, and the temperature with it, and what time. Fifty-seven nights, that book showed. Fifty-seven in forty-three winters. Thirty winters she spoke in and thirteen she never opened her mouth at all — thirty and thirteen, and there is your forty-three. And he counted the dying too, against what the old women held. Better than a third of the burying in this town came inside the week after a night she spoke, he told me once, and he did not appear to think much of it either way. He would have told anybody the numbers who asked him, and I want you to understand that in all those years I do not believe five people ever asked. He was a fixture. You do not ask a fixture questions.

Now the wager. You will have heard about the wager, everybody has.

The fourteenth of January, 1936. I say that without hesitation, I have told it a hundred times and it does not change. Judd comes in about four in the afternoon for kerosene — a gallon can, he carried it himself — and he sets it on this counter and he says, she will speak tonight.

And I said, Judd, she will not.

Now here is why I remember the figures, because he said them twice, once to me and once to a fellow who came in behind him. It had made forty-one at noon. It was thirty-eight at four o'clock. And it had been coming down four degrees an hour since one, steady, you could watch it on the porch thermometer. Judd said all three of those numbers like a man reading a bill of lading.

I said, five dollars it does not.

He did not even take his hand off the can. He said, five dollars.

And, mister, it went to three below that night, and she spoke at ten minutes past one, and I heard her, lying up there in the dark, knowing exactly what it was costing me. I paid him the next morning before I opened. He put the bill in his vest and never once brought it up again in twenty-five years, which is worse than if he had.

Five dollars was five dollars in 1936.

[the recorder is moved]

What was that? — Oh, that. Somebody has been carrying a story around this valley about my father, something he is supposed to have said at his retirement dinner, in the spring of 1931. I have heard it comes at second or third hand and I will not dignify it by repeating it in front of a machine. I was at that dinner. He said nothing of the kind, and he never in his life spoke to me about that bridge. Now that is the end of it.

Anders Berg set that nest. Everybody knew it. That is not a thing anybody in this town ever had to look up.

Where was I.

My father. I will tell you about my father, since you have the thing running.

He was a big quiet man who came home once a week when I was small and stood in the kitchen with his hat still on until my mother told him to sit. He built things all over this county and he never talked about one of them at his own table. He took me out on the deck when I was six and told me he had closed the span himself in ninety-seven. I can still feel the ties through my shoes and the creek making that noise a long way down. He held the back of my collar the whole time. All that air under a boy is a thing you remember, and I have not stood out there since, not once, and I am not going to.

He was proud of it. He was not a proud man about much.

[a customer; some minutes lost]

— No, I never wrote any of it down. Not a line. There is nothing in this store on paper but the day book, and the day book says what people owed me and nothing about bridges. Judd was the writing one. Judd wrote down what happened and I sold kerosene to the people it happened to.

Then in fifty-four the railroad went in there and rebuilt the whole business — new bearings, a gang here for weeks eating up my stock, and they went away, and she never spoke again. Not once. Not one time since.

And I will tell you something you would not expect. This town was disappointed. A thing that was ours for as long as anybody in this town could remember, that Ninestone did not have and the state road did not have, and some men come out with a crane and take it away in a month and do not even know they have taken it. There was talk that first winter. People sat up. Nothing. Second winter, nothing. After that they stopped sitting up, and now the young ones think it was a story their grandfathers made, and I cannot prove them wrong, and Judd's book is Judd's book and who is going to read it.

Some called it the train. I let them.

I will say this for the whole business and then you can turn that off. I lost five dollars on it and I have made more than five dollars' worth out of telling about losing it, so I came out ahead, which is more than the railroad did.

Is that the kind of thing you wanted?
