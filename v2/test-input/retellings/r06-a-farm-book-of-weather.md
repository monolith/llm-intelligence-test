# Retelling 06 — Lettie Wexler, "A Farm Book of Weather," a reminiscence for a farm journal, 1965

*Written at seventy-six by the keeper of the Kettle Bench weather record, at the journal's request, for a series on farm records kept in one family.*

There is not much to tell and I will not stretch it.

Our place is Kettle Bench, three miles up the valley from Cadder Falls, on the shoulder above the creek where the frost sits longer than it does in town. My father was Peter Wexler. He began the weather record in 1884 and kept it until 1926, and I have kept it since, which makes eighty-one years of it, now, in one hand or the other.

It is three figures a day and nothing else. The day's high. The night's low. The rain, or the snow measured melted. My father set the thermometer on the north wall of the milk house where the sun never touches it and it has never been moved. The reading is taken at daylight and again at four, and the low is the low at daylight unless the night was strange, in which case you say so in the margin and do not argue about it.

I have not missed a morning since 1926. I have taken it with a broken wrist and I have taken it the morning after my father's funeral, and people think that is remarkable and it is not. It is only that a record with a hole in it is not a record. It is a story about the weather, and there are enough of those.

Which brings me to the thing your readers will want, so I may as well come to it.

We were related to nobody in this business — not to the railroad people, not to the mill people, not to the Cadder Falls families that the tale belongs to. My father had no interest in the bridge and neither have I. In eighty-odd years of pages there is not one word about it, because we never wrote down a noise. We wrote down weather. That turns out to have been worth something, though it was not why we did it.

In 1962 the Frayne girl came up the hill. Ruth Frayne, the engineer — she does the county's road surveys, and she was working down at the creek that fall. She wanted the whole record. I gave it to her in four cardboard boxes, which is what it comes to, and she brought every box back, which I will say for her, because not everybody does.

She asked me first for one date, the seventeenth of January, 1912, and I found it for her while she stood there. It is in my father's hand:

> *Jan. 17. High 34. Low −2 at daylight. Clear, still.*

Thirty-six degrees of fall in a day, and clear and still after it. That is the kind of night the cold comes down out of the sky and just sits.

Then she asked for something harder, which was the twelve years after the line opened — from the winter of 1897 and 1898 through to the summer of 1909. I went through it page by page over two evenings. In those twelve years there were seven nights below six degrees. Four of the seven came after days at least thirty degrees warmer than the night that followed. The coldest reading of the whole twelve years was nine below, on the fourth of February, 1899. I remember writing that out for her twice because she wanted the date as well as the figure.

She asked about the two winters after that, too, and those are easy, because there is nothing in them. The winter of 1909 and 1910 and the winter of 1910 and 1911 never went below nine above zero, either one of them, and the coldest morning of the pair was the third of February, 1911. That is a mild pair of winters for this valley and I have no explanation for them except that we got them.

The hard one was 1917 and 1918. That is the deepest cold anybody up here ever wrote down. Eleven below zero, in January of 1918, and that is the lowest figure in the whole series from my father's first page to this morning's. The high side of it belongs to July of 1936: one hundred and one degrees, which I took myself and did not believe, and took again an hour later.

Now, the station agent down at Cadder Falls kept a book of his own all those years — a ruled ledger where he set down every night the bridge made its noise. I had never seen it. In 1962 the Frayne girl had his book and mine both out on my kitchen table at the same time, and I read his for an hour while she worked, which is the only time in my life I have looked into it.

I will tell you what I saw, because it is a matter of arithmetic and not of opinion.

Every night his book says the bridge spoke, my book says the night went under six degrees. Not one exception in what I looked at. And every one of those nights came after a day at least thirty degrees warmer than the night that followed it — not a gradual cold, a dropping cold. I took a dozen of his dates at random and turned to them in my own pages, and they agreed, every one, to the degree. That surprised me more than it should have. He was writing down a sound in a station at two in the morning and I was writing down a thermometer on a milk house wall three miles off, and neither of us ever spoke to the other about it, and the two books lie down together like they were kept by one person. One more thing out of his book, while I am on it: the fullest winter in it was 1917 and 1918, five nights, more than any other winter he kept — which surprised me not at all, since that was our own worst winter and I have said so above.

His summing was at the back. Sixty-one nights she spoke, he had, over the winters he kept it. And eight nights answered both the conditions and gave nothing at all — cold enough, and the fall steep enough, and no sound out of her. So sixty-nine nights in all that answered them, by his own count, of which sixty-one spoke and eight did not.

I asked him nothing about the eight because I never met him. I would have liked to. A man who keeps the same column that long, in ink, without anybody asking him to, is a man I would have got along with.

The noise stopped some years back. I could not tell you the year within two and I will not guess at it in print. There was talk up here at the time about why, and there is always talk up here about why. I have no theory. I have a thermometer, and it has gone on saying the same three things a day since, and none of them was ever a reason.

The journal asked me for advice for young people keeping records, which I take to be the real purpose of this piece. Here it is. Take the reading at the same hour. Write it in ink. Do not correct yesterday because today makes it look wrong. Do not leave a day out because nothing happened — the days when nothing happened are half of what the record is for, and it took a stranger with a survey to teach me that. Seven nights below six degrees in twelve years, four of them after a warm day: my father wrote those down for no reason at all, and sixty years later they turned out to be the answer to somebody's question.

I do not know what the question was. She did not tell me and I did not ask. That was not my end of it.
