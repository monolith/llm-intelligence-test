# Retelling 09 — H. L. Quarles, memorandum for the office of the Pell County engineer, 1971

*Placed on the bridge-management file by an engineer who has read the record and met none of the witnesses.*

**MEMORANDUM ON THE SALLOW CREEK VIADUCT**

**Subject: the center-span bearings, milepost 22, Cadder Valley branch — their behavior prior to the rehabilitation of March 1954, and the mechanism of the phenomenon reported in the valley over that period.**

**1. Purpose.** This memorandum is made for the file. The structure at milepost 22 has been the subject of a local tradition of long standing, and that tradition has lately been the subject of inquiry by persons outside this office. The condition which produced the phenomenon was removed in 1954 and cannot recur. It is nevertheless considered proper that the mechanism be set down once, in writing, in a place where it can afterward be found, so that the office is not obliged to reconstruct it a second time from the same papers.

**2. Sources.** The contract drawings for the viaduct are held here. The records of the 1954 rehabilitation are held here. A copy of the survey report made in September 1962, at the draw-down of the creek for the state's flood-control cut, is on the file, together with the cast pedestal recovered at that time. The account of the depot record is at second hand; the writer has not examined that book and has relied on what was reported of its contents. No witness has been interviewed and none is required. The behavior described below is not a matter of testimony.

**3. The structure.** Three spans: 120 feet, 300 feet, and 120 feet, five hundred and forty feet overall. The center span is a pin-connected truss carried ninety-seven feet above the bed of the creek. The line reaches this point from the Ashby quarry at milepost 31 on a descending grade of 2.2 percent maintained over eight miles.

**4. Bearing arrangement.** The north bearing of the center span is fixed. The south bearing was, as built, a nest of rollers seated in a cast pedestal, so that the whole of the span's movement with temperature was taken at the south end. In the rehabilitation of March 1954, undertaken to receive diesel power, the roller nest was taken out and rocker bearings affording four inches of travel each way were substituted. The discarded pedestal was thrown over the abutment by the gang and lay in the bed of the creek until 1962.

**5. Travel as drawn.** Sheet 11 of the contract drawings specifies four inches of travel each way for the south nest.

**6. Travel as constructed.** The recovered pedestal carries a chisel mark on the roller seat at two inches, and the nest was set to that mark. The difference between the drawing and the work is accordingly established by the metal itself and does not rest on anyone's recollection. This office regards that as the one satisfactory feature of the whole business.

**7. The work of August 1909.** The deck was renewed at that time with heavier rail and new ties, and both shoes were jacked and seated again to the same mark. The steel is recorded as standing at eighty-six degrees when the south nest was seated. No change of mark was made and none appears to have been contemplated. The men who did the work were renewing a deck; the bearing was reset because it had been disturbed, and to the same figure it had carried since erection.

**8. Coefficient.** The coefficient in use for this span is one inch in three hundred feet for fifty degrees.

**9. Computation.** From paragraph 8, and taking the span as closed at sixty-six degrees, the temperature at which the available cold travel is exhausted is as follows.

(a) As specified in paragraph 5, with four inches of travel: one hundred and thirty-four degrees below zero.

(b) As constructed, per paragraph 6, with two inches: thirty-four degrees below zero.

(c) As re-seated in 1909, per paragraph 7, with two inches taken from eighty-six degrees: fourteen degrees below zero.

**10. Remark on the computation.** The figure at (c) is not readily reconciled with the local record, which places the occurrences at readings a good deal warmer than that. The writer offers no reconciliation and has not pursued the point. The condition has been removed; the discrepancy is of interest to the antiquarian rather than to this office, and the file is not the place to argue it.

The writer will, however, record one opinion, offered as opinion and not as computation. The setting of a bearing is a smaller matter than the load standing upon it. The deck renewed in August 1909 was forty tons heavier than the deck it replaced, and a roller nest takes that increase directly upon the rollers. It is at least as probable that the added dead load, rather than any question of travel, was what brought the bearing to bind; and the date at which the local tradition is said to begin is consistent with the re-decking and with nothing else in the record.

**11. Mechanism.** The mechanism is not obscure. As the span cools it shortens, and the south bearing travels toward the cold end of its seat. If the travel available to it is exhausted, the shoe binds. Contraction does not on that account stop. Strain accumulates in the members of the truss and at the pins, and is stored there. The bearing then releases suddenly and entirely, and the span takes up its new position in a single movement.

**12. The report.** Because the release is sudden and complete, the energy stored in the truss is discharged at one stroke rather than dissipated by degrees. The result is a single report, deep in pitch and of short duration. It is transmitted with far greater efficiency along the continuous rail than through the air, which accounts both for its being registered at the depot a mile down the line and for the fact that it was felt in the structure, and in the telegraph sounder there, before it was heard. It is not periodic. It does not repeat within a night. It bears no resemblance to a bell, and none whatever to the exhaust of a locomotive. Those who have described it as resembling either were describing what they expected rather than what reached them.

**13. Rate of fall.** It has been observed in the valley that a low temperature by itself did not produce the report, and that a rapid fall was required. The observation is sound and the reason for it is mechanical. A slow decline permits the bearing to creep, a small increment at a time, the friction of the nest being overcome repeatedly and at low stored strain, so that no accumulation is possible and no release occurs. A fall of thirty degrees or more within the day does not permit this. The bearing is carried past its freedom faster than it can relieve itself, and the strain is stored instead of spent.

**14. Silence upon qualifying nights.** It has likewise been observed that certain nights answered every condition and gave nothing — eight such nights within the depot record, as the writer has it at second hand — and this has been treated in the valley as a failure of the rule. It is not. Once the bearing has released, it stands at the far end of its travel and cannot release again, having nothing left to release. It is returned toward the warm end of the seat only by warming — in practice by a daytime thaw of some hours above about forty degrees. Successive nights within a single cold spell are therefore silent, however cold they may be, unless such a thaw has intervened. The conditions set down at the depot are necessary conditions. They are not sufficient ones. The exceptions in that record are consistent with the mechanism rather than an objection to it, and it is unfortunate that no one appears to have told the keeper of the book so.

**15. Cessation.** Four inches of travel each way cannot be exhausted at any temperature recorded in this valley. The phenomenon ceased with the work of March 1954 and has not since recurred. It should be noted for the file that the cessation was in no sense an object of that work. The rocker bearings were fitted because the traffic was changing and the nest was old; the effect on the phenomenon was incidental, and was not remarked at the time by the men who produced it. The deck of 1909 was not disturbed in that work and stands over the span today; nothing was altered in March 1954 but the bearings themselves. Three nights in the two winters following the work are reported to have answered both conditions, no sound being heard upon any of them.

**16. The local tradition.** It is understood locally that the sound was made by a locomotive lying in the bed of the creek. The survey of September 1962 established that there is no locomotive in Sallow Creek and that there has never been one. The tradition may be left where it is; it is not the business of this office to argue with it, only to decline to file it.

**17. Disposition.** It is recommended that the recovered pedestal be retained and not scrapped, the chisel mark upon it being the only physical evidence of the setting of 1897 now in existence, and that a copy of this memorandum be attached to the drawings, to the 1954 rehabilitation records, and to the survey report of 1962, so that the three are not read separately again.

— H. L. Q.
