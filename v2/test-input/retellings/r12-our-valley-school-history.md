# Retelling 12 — Corwin Athey, "Our Valley: A Short History for the Schools," Cadder Falls, 1966

*A pamphlet for the eighth grade, printed at the Falls, compiled from the town's memory, the newspaper's back files, and two afternoons with the station agent.*

**TO THE READER**

This little book was made for the eighth grade of the Cadder Falls school, and for anybody older who has forgotten. It is short on purpose. A valley this size does not need a long history; it needs a true one, and one that a young person will finish.

---

**CHAPTER I. THE RAILROAD COMES**

Before the railroad there was a wagon road, and before the wagon road there was the creek, and the creek is still here and will outlast the rest.

The Cadder Valley Railroad was built to bring stone down from the Ashby quarry and lumber and cloth up from Ninestone. Its great work was the bridge at Sallow Creek, which was begun in April of 1896 and which took a year and a half to build. The center span — the long one, over the water — was closed on the twelfth of September, 1897. On the fourteenth of November, 1897, the first train came into Cadder Falls, and the town you live in dates from that afternoon as surely as if it had been founded on it.

The bridge was built by men from this valley and men from away. Emil Rennick, of the Falls, built the falsework — the wooden staging on which the iron was put together before it could carry itself. Cass Nolan, who set the rollers, was of the gang, and so were a good many others whose names are on nobody's list.

The resident engineer was Warren Tice, whose son Dorsey keeps the store today.

**CHAPTER II. THE WOMAN WHO KEPT A BOOK**

Emil Rennick had a sister, Adela Rennick, born in 1874, and she is the person in this history whom the schools have most neglected.

She was what the railroad called a *computer*, which does not mean a machine. It means the person who does the figuring: the one who takes the measurements the party brings in from the field and turns them into the numbers the work is built to. She kept the company's book, and she also kept a book of her own, which was not required of her and which nobody thanked her for.

On the day the long span was closed she entered an objection in her own book about the way the bridge's rollers had been set. She was overruled by the resident engineer. She wrote it down anyway. That is the whole of the incident, and the class should notice that it is the writing down, and not the objection, that has come to matter — for the objection was over in an afternoon and the entry is still here.

She married Josiah Frayne in 1902.

**CHAPTER III. THE RUNAWAY OF 1898**

On the morning of the sixth of March, 1898, an engine came down off the quarry grade with her train behind her and could not be held. She left the rails at the north approach of the viaduct.

Nobody was killed. Two men of the crew were hurt. You will hear otherwise. You will hear it from people who believe it, and it is worth learning early that a story can be told sincerely by everyone in a town and still not be true. The newspaper of that week says plainly that nobody died.

**CHAPTER IV. THE BRIDGE THAT SPEAKS**

Now we come to the part of this history that you already know, because it was told to you before you could read.

On a bitter night the bridge at Sallow Creek makes a sound. It is one sound, a single deep report, and it comes up the valley in the small hours and goes through the walls of a house. It is not a whistle and not a bell. Men who have heard it say they felt it before they heard it.

The first night it was heard was the seventeenth of January, 1912.

We know that date, and we know a great deal else, because of one man. Judd Rennick — son of Emil, the carpenter of Chapter I — came to the depot at Cadder Falls as night operator and was afterward the station agent for many years. From the first night he kept a book. In it he set down the date, the day's warmest, the night's coldest, whether she spoke, and at what hour. He kept it for forty-three winters, which is longer than most men keep anything. Fifty-seven nights are recorded.

Now, what is it?

Number Nine lies in the creek to this day, and it is her that the valley hears. She went off the iron at the north end in 1898 and rolled down into the gorge, and there she has stayed under the water, and on the nights when the cold works into the iron of her she speaks. That is what this valley has believed for two generations, and it is not a foolish belief. It fits the sound, which is the sound of iron. It fits the place, which is the place she went off. It fits the winter, for iron is loudest when it is coldest. A boy of your age can test a belief by asking what it explains, and this one explains a good deal.

The last night the bridge was heard was the ninth of February, 1954. In March of that year the railroad rebuilt the bridge to carry the new diesel engines, and put new bearings under the long span, and from that month to this the valley has not heard a sound. Fifty-seven nights are recorded, and there will not be a fifty-eighth.

**CHAPTER V. THE WAGER**

On the fourteenth of January, 1936, the station agent and the storekeeper made a bet of five dollars.

The storekeeper held that a man could not tell in the afternoon what a bridge would do at midnight. The agent held that he could, because he had the book and the book had a rule in it. They sat up together in the depot to see. The bridge spoke, and the agent was paid.

The lesson is not that the agent was clever. The lesson is that he had written things down for twenty-four years before that evening, and a man with a record is a prophet on very ordinary terms.

**CHAPTER VI. THE OLD BELIEF, AND THE AGENT'S TEST**

Our grandmothers held that the bridge rang for a death — that when the valley heard it, somebody in the town would be carried out within the week.

The station agent did not argue with them. He did something better, and the class should mark how he did it, because it is the most useful thing in this pamphlet.

He counted.

In the years covered by his book there were a hundred and ninety deaths in Cadder Falls. Of those, seven fell within a week after a night the bridge had spoken. Seven out of a hundred and ninety. Had the bridge been ringing for the dead it would have had to ring a great deal oftener, and the dead would have had to wait their turn.

So the old belief cannot stand. It was not a lie. It was a memory that kept the nights it wanted and dropped the rest, which is what all of us do, and the only cure for it is a book and a pencil.

**CHAPTER VII. WHAT THE VALLEY IS**

A branch line with a quarry at one end and the county seat at the other, a mill, a store, a depot, a bridge. It is not much to look at on a map. But there was a woman here who wrote down what she was told not to bother about, and a man here who kept a book for forty-three winters without being paid a cent for it, and between them they left this valley better evidenced than counties ten times its size.

Write things down. That is the whole moral, and I will not spoil it by adding another.

**NOTE ADDED AT PRESS**

While this pamphlet was being set, I wrote to the Bly County Historical Rooms, two hundred miles north of us, to ask what became of the Cadder Valley Railroad's engines after the road sold them off. Their reply reached me too late to change the text. They send the following, copied from a lumber company's shop book of May 1929:

> *No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile this month. Scrap to Ravel Brothers, $470.*

The engine sold out of this county on the nineteenth of June, 1901, was Number Nine, and No. 4 of the lumber company was that same engine. She was therefore not in Sallow Creek in 1929, and she was not in it in 1912 either, and she is not in it now. I leave the paragraph as I wrote it and correct it here.

— C. A.
