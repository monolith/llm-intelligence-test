# Retelling 03 — Judd Rennick, station agent (retired), interviewed at his kitchen table, 1965

*Recorded for the Pell County Historical Society. The interviewer's questions have been dropped; his answers are printed as he gave them.*

I was born in eighty-eight, here. My father was Emil Rennick. He was a carpenter and he built the falsework for that bridge, which is the timber they stand the iron on until the iron will stand by itself. Then you knock the timber down. He was proud of it and he never got a line of credit for it anywhere.

My father had a younger sister, Adela. She worked for the same railroad before I was born, in the engineering party, out in the field with the men. She never spoke about it. Not to me, not once. I asked her twice when I was a boy and got nothing, and after that I had better sense.

I went on nights in 1907 and I started the book that same winter. Then they made me agent. Thirty-nine years at that depot, all told. I have been out of it a good while now and I still wake at four.

You want to know about the sound. I will tell you what it was like and then I will tell you what I do not know, and I would be obliged if you would print both.

It was one report. Deep. It was not a rattle and it was not a roll and it did not travel — it was there and then it was done. And it came up the rails first. That is the part I have never got anybody to believe who was not sitting where I sat. The sounder on my table would rattle in its bracket before the sound got to the room. A little dry rattle, like a bug in a box. Then the floor. Then the noise. I timed that gap once with a watch and could not get a reading on it, it is that short, but the order never changed in forty-three winters.

It was always between midnight and four. Never in the evening. Never at daylight. Between midnight and four.

The first time she spoke was the winter of 1913. I was on nights, alone in the office, and I thought a car had got away in the quarry and come down on us. I went out on the platform with a lantern and there was nothing wrong anywhere and it was so cold the lantern glass cracked. In the morning I ruled up a book.

Five columns. The date. The day's high. The night's low. Whether she spoke. And the hour. That is all. I did not put down what I thought about it because what I thought about it was not worth the ink, and because a man who writes down his opinion once will write down his opinion instead of the reading the next time. Forty-three winters of that.

Now the conditions, and I did not have these at the start. I got them out of the book after a good many winters, when there was enough of it to look at.

She never spoke unless the night's low went below six degrees. Never once. Not at seven, not at six and a half. Below six.

And she never spoke unless the day before had been at least thirty degrees warmer than the night that followed. It had to fall thirty. A cold night on the end of a cold day was nothing to her. It had to be a soft day and then the bottom out of it.

Both, every time. There is not an exception in the book, and I would know, because I went through it looking for one.

Here are the totals and I will stand on them. Sixty-one nights she spoke. Thirty-four winters she spoke at least once. Nine winters she never spoke at all — the nine and the thirty-four make the forty-three, you can check me. And five nights answered both conditions and gave nothing. So sixty-six nights answered them in all, and on five of the sixty-six she kept still.

I know why the five were silent, or I think I do, and this is the only guess of mine I will let you have. She would not speak twice inside one freeze. Once she had spoken she was done until there had been a thaw — a real thaw, up above forty in the daytime, not a mild hour at noon. Every one of the five silent nights sits in a cold spell where she had already spoken and where nothing had got above forty in between. That is not a theory about what she was. It is a rule about when.

The gang. You asked about the gang. It is in the front of the book, and I will read it to you as I wrote it, because I copied it off the depot slate a good while after and I do not trust my memory against my own hand:

> *Bridge gang here three weeks, August 1909. New 85-lb. steel, new ties, both shoes jacked and set again to the mark. Thermometer on the north pier read 86 at four o'clock when they seated the south nest. Mr. Coyle says the deck is forty ton heavier than it was.*

That was the summer of 1911. Three weeks of them, and dust in the office, and Coyle was the foreman and a decent man. Eighty-five-pound steel where there had been sixty. New ties the whole way over. They jacked both shoes and set them again to the same mark they had been on, and the thermometer on the north pier read eighty-six at four o'clock when they seated the south nest. Forty tons heavier when they got done. I wrote it down because it was the biggest thing that happened at that depot that year, not because I thought it meant anything. I did not think it meant anything.

The town said she rang for deaths. That was the story — that when she spoke somebody at the Falls would be dead inside the week. I heard it my whole life and I never argued with anybody about it, because arguing does not settle a count.

So I counted. In the years I kept that book there were a hundred and ninety deaths at Cadder Falls. Seven of them fell inside a week after a night she spoke. Seven out of a hundred and ninety. I put the two numbers in the back of the book and I have never said them at the store and I am saying them to you because you asked and because you have a machine running.

Now the wager, since everybody wants the wager.

That was January of 1937. I was in Dorsey Tice's store in the middle of the afternoon and the talk got round to it, the way it did. It had made forty-one at noon. It was thirty-eight at four o'clock and it had been falling four degrees an hour since one, steady, you could watch it go.

I did the sum standing at his counter. Thirty-eight down to six is thirty-two degrees. At four degrees an hour that is eight hours. So she would be under six about midnight, and the day had been forty-one, and forty-one down to anything under six is a fall of better than thirty. And there had been no thaw since the second week of December, so she was rested.

I said she would speak before morning. Dorsey bet me five dollars she would not.

The low that night was three below. She spoke at ten minutes past one. Dorsey paid me the five dollars in the store the next day in front of everybody there, which I did not ask him to do, and he told that story on himself for the rest of his life and got more out of it than I got out of the five dollars.

Then the rockers went in, in the spring of 1953. They rebuilt the bridge for the diesels. They put in rocker bearings with four inches of travel each way, and they threw the old cast pedestals over the abutment into the creek, and nobody told me a thing about it. I was the agent. I found out from a section man.

The second winter after the rockers, there were three nights under six degrees with falls of better than thirty on them, and there was not a sound out of her on any of the three. I wrote my last note in the book then and quit writing:

> *Second winter since the rockers went in. Three nights under six degrees, three falls of better than thirty, and not a sound out of her either winter. I have kept this book forty-three winters and I do not know now what it was I was listening to.*

That is where I am yet. I have got the road in my head — the quarry is milepost 31, the Falls is 23, the bridge is 22, and it is two and two-tenths percent down the nine miles off the quarry — and I have got sixty-one nights and a hundred and ninety deaths and two conditions that never failed, and I have not got one word of explanation, and I am not going to make one up at my age.

The Frayne girl came a while back and sat where you are sitting and read the whole book through at this table, and she did not talk while she read, which I appreciated. She asked to take it away with her. I let her have it. She will do something with it. I never did.
