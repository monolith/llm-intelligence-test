# Retelling 04 — Ruth Frayne, "What the Creek Gave Back," a talk to the Pell County Historical Society, 1963

*Read to the Society at Ninestone; her blackboard figures are given in the text.*

Thank you. I am an engineer with the Pell County highway department, three years in it, and I did not go out to Sallow Creek last September to settle anything. I went because the creek was going to be dry, and a dry creek under a railroad bridge is an opportunity that does not come twice in a career. A state flood-control project had pulled Sallow Creek out of its bed for eleven weeks, leaving the gorge dry down to the gravel. The inspection was routine, and stopped being routine on the second afternoon.

I have four findings and then some arithmetic, and I would ask you to hold your questions until the arithmetic, which is where the talk is going.

**Finding one is nothing.** I walked the bed for something over eight hundred feet, above and below the viaduct, and there is no locomotive in Sallow Creek. There is no frame, no boiler shell, no driving wheel, nothing of iron larger than a spike. The photographs are on the table at the back. I state it flatly because it required no interpretation: I went and looked and it is not there.

**Finding two is the pedestal.** Forty feet below the abutment on the south side, lying in the gravel where the 1954 gang threw it over, is a casting the size of a kitchen stove. It is the old south shoe of the center span — the pedestal that carried the roller nest.

Why that matters: a three-hundred-foot steel span gets longer when hot and shorter when cold, and has to be allowed to. The center span is pinned at the north end and free at the south, and the south end used to ride on rollers in that casting. Cut into the roller seat is a chisel mark — the setting mark, where the nest was to be placed at erection so that there would be room for the steel to come back in the cold. I measured it three times with a steel tape and got the same answer each time. The chisel mark is at two inches. The bearings that were put in in 1954, which are rockers, not rollers, measure four inches of travel each way, and I measured those too.

**Finding three is my grandmother's field book.** Adela Rennick, born 1874, died 1959 at eighty-five, was a computer and rodman for the railroad's engineering party while the viaduct was building. She kept her own duplicate of the party's book, in black oilcloth, all her life. It came to my father, Wendell Frayne, when she died, and he gave it to me when I said I was going out to the creek.

In the front matter of that book, among the tables, is one line:

> *Rule for the long span: one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees.*

That is the working rule for the movement of that steel. Three hundred feet of it, an inch for every forty degrees. Remember that line; everything else follows from it.

And on page sixty-two:

> *Sept. 12, 1897. Span closed 3:40 p.m., thermometer 66°. Rollers set at the two-inch mark. Sheet 11 calls for three. Reported to W.T., who answers that the pedestal cannot be recast before the fall haul and that two inches is more than the valley will ever ask for. Entered here so that it is somewhere. — A.R.*

She reported it. She was overruled. She wrote it down so that it would be somewhere, and there it sat for sixty-five years while the valley told itself a ghost story.

**Finding four is the night book**, which my father's uncle, Judd Rennick, lent me himself. He is seventy-four, he lives three streets from the depot he kept as station agent at Cadder Falls, and he wrote down every occurrence of the sound the valley called the boom, in a ruled book, for forty-three winters. Sixty-one nights. On every one the night's low was below six degrees above zero, and stood at the bottom of a fall of thirty degrees or more from the day's high. Further nights answered both conditions and produced nothing at all, and his own summing at the back of the book gives the count of them. He kept no theory. He kept columns.

And in the front of his book he copied a note about a bridge gang that was on the structure for three weeks in August 1909: new eighty-five-pound steel, new ties, both shoes jacked and set again to the mark, and the thermometer on the north pier reading eighty-six degrees at four o'clock when they seated the south nest.

Now the arithmetic, and I am going to put it on the board.

An inch for every forty degrees. Sheet 11 called for three inches of cold travel in that nest. Three inches is a hundred and twenty degrees of cooling. The span was closed at sixty-six degrees. Sixty-six less a hundred and twenty is fifty-four below zero. As drawn, that bridge would have run out of travel at fifty-four below, which this valley has never come anywhere near.

But it was not built to three inches. It was built to two, and the chisel mark in the gravel below the abutment is what two inches looks like. Two inches is eighty degrees. Sixty-six less eighty is fourteen below zero. Also a temperature this valley does not reach. And that is the reason nobody noticed anything for twelve years: a wrong bridge behaved exactly like a right one, because the difference between them lay in weather that never came.

Then August 1909. The gang jacked both shoes and set them again to the same two-inch mark — with the steel at eighty-six degrees. Eighty degrees down from eighty-six is six above zero.

Six above zero. Judd Rennick's night book puts all sixty-one of the nights she spoke, across forty-three winters, right there — never once warmer.

That is the whole of it. When the span cooled past its binding point the nest ran out of travel, the south shoe hung up, and the truss carried the load in stress until the shoe let go all at once and the span dropped into position with one report that ran along the rails. It was not a train. It was a bridge, arriving where it should have been.

I have a second witness, and it is the part that convinced me. The Wexler family of Kettle Bench have kept a volunteer weather record — high, low and precipitation, every day. Miss Wexler puts the beginning of it at the first of January, 1894, and I have used her date. Peter Wexler began it and his daughter Lettie has kept it since 1926, and she lent it to me in four cardboard boxes without asking what I wanted it for. The Wexlers never wrote down a sound in their lives, and are related to nobody in this story. Their weather agrees with the station agent's book on every one of the sixty-one nights. Two records kept by people who had no occasion to compare them, neither knowing what the other was for.

The Wexler record also settles the onset, which is the point I would most like you to carry home. In the eighteen years between the line's opening in November 1897 and the re-decking of August 1909, the Wexler record counted seven nights at Kettle Bench that fell below six degrees, four of them following a day's drop of thirty degrees or better. Nothing was heard on any of them, and nobody at the Falls has ever claimed there was. Across those twelve years the coldest night came on the fourth of February, 1899, at nine below — cold, but well short of the fourteen below that the bridge as built would have required. The two winters after the re-decking never once got below nine above zero, the coldest being the third of February, 1911. And the first night under six degrees after August 1909 did not come until the seventeenth of January, 1912:

> *Jan. 17. High 34. Low −2 at daylight. Clear, still.*

That is the night the sound began, and the night my father's uncle started his book.

Those seven silent nights are not a gap in the data. They are the proof. If the error of 1897 had been enough by itself, the valley would have heard the bridge in 1899. It heard nothing. It took a summer afternoon twelve years later, and a gang doing careful work in good faith at eighty-six degrees, to finish what was begun at sixty-six.

Two more figures from Kettle Bench. The highest reading in the whole series is one hundred and one degrees, July 1936; the lowest reading in the whole series is thirteen below, January 1918.

On the locomotive I will be brief. I wrote to the Bly County Historical Rooms, two hundred miles north, which hold the books of the Tolliver Lumber Company, deposited there in 1938 by the company's bookkeeper and, so far as the catalogue shows, consulted by nobody since. They sent photostats by return. A stock book entry of 19 June 1901 records Cadder Valley No. 9 sold to the Tolliver Lumber Company, delivered on her own wheels; a shop book entry of May 1929 records her cut up at Sixmile. She left this valley alive and she died two hundred miles from it, and neither event has anything to do with a sound that began in January 1912.

As to disposition: the field book goes to this Society this year. Miss Wexler keeps her weather book while she lives and it comes here after. The night book comes here when Judd Rennick has done with it, and I hope that is a long time yet. The pedestal I would like in the entrance hall, where people can put a hand on it.

I will close with the last paragraph of my report, written on the tailgate of the truck and not improved since:

> *The south pedestal was recovered from the creek bed forty feet below the abutment, where the 1954 gang threw it. The chisel mark on the roller seat is at two inches. Sheet 11 calls for three. There is no locomotive in this creek and there never was one. What the valley heard for forty-three winters was a bridge letting go of itself, one inch late.*

My grandmother measured that bridge and was not believed. I measured it again sixty-five years later, with a better tape and worse handwriting, and she was right. That is all I have.
