# Notes after R01–R04 — the Sallow Creek Viaduct / "the boom" at Cadder Falls

Handoff notes for a fresh reader. Four retellings of one story, four narrators, four dates. Everything below comes only from the four retellings (r01–r04). Where sources disagree, the disagreement is recorded, not resolved.

## 1. People, and how they connect

**Vesta Cobb** (r01, narrator)
- Born **1920**, "second house up from the mill gate," Cadder Falls.
- Father: in the mill his whole working life. Mother: her people "had been at the Falls since the line was built."
- Grandmother: was at the Falls *while* the line was being built — "she boarded the men who came in to do it"; grandmother "was here for it" (the wreck) and is the source of the ghost-train story.
- Brother: present the night she heard the sound (he "was up before I was").
- Husband: "used to say I could talk the paint off a porch." She was "grown and married" while the Dorsey Tice bet story was still being told.
- Everything she knows she "got at that stove from people who are all of them dead now."

**Harlan Pike** (r02, narrator)
- His father was **chief clerk of the Cadder Valley Railroad from 1893 to 1919** — "twenty-six years." Desk at Ninestone with "the window on the yard."
- "Other boys grew up with a dog. I grew up with a stock book." Sunday dinner-table talk about whether a figure in a column was "one the directors would swallow."
- Was a boy in the office ("I sat in that office as a boy while men talked over my head") and on the viaduct deck "with my father's hand in my collar."
- Motive: "there is nobody left who cares to have it right, and because what is said about the Sallow Creek Viaduct at present is mostly rubbish."

**Judd Rennick** (r03, narrator; appears in r01 and r04)
- Born **1888**, at the Falls.
- Father: **Emil Rennick**, a carpenter, who **built the falsework for the bridge** ("the timber they stand the iron on until the iron will stand by itself. Then you knock the timber down"). Proud of it; "he never got a line of credit for it anywhere."
- Emil had a **younger sister, Adela** — Judd's aunt. She "worked for the same railroad before I was born, in the engineering party, out in the field with the men. She never spoke about it. Not to me, not once." He asked twice as a boy and got nothing.
- Went **on nights in 1907**; later made **agent**; **thirty-nine years at that depot, all told**. "I still wake at four."
- Kept the night book **forty-three winters**.
- In 1963 (r04) Ruth Frayne says he "is seventy-four" and "lives three streets from the depot." In 1974 (r01) Vesta says he "lived into his eighties, up on the hill there."

**Adela Rennick** (r02, r03, r04 — never a narrator)
- **Born 1874, died 1959 at eighty-five** (r04).
- **Computer** for the engineering party (r02: "The party's computer was a Miss Adela Rennick"); r04 calls her "a computer and rodman for the railroad's engineering party while the viaduct was building."
- Kept **her own duplicate of the party's book, in her own hand, in black oilcloth, all her life** — "quite separate from the party's book, which was thought eccentric and was not" (r02).
- Also "in the habit of entering in that book whatever she thought, including what she thought about the bridge, and one such entry got back to the resident engineer. **Tice never forgave that entry, and she was gone inside four years.**" (r02, who adds: "I have nothing in writing on the point and I do not pretend to… there are things you know from the temperature of a room.")
- "Her time with the road ended when the engineering party was reduced. **Two of that party were taken back on in the August following, for the Ashby extension work, and she was not**, and I have never needed anybody to explain that to me." (r02)
- Her book passed to her son **Wendell Frayne** on her death (1959), and he gave it to his daughter Ruth when she said she was going out to the creek.

**Ruth Frayne** (r04, narrator; appears in r01 and r03)
- **Engineer with the Pell County highway department, three years in it.**
- Adela Rennick's **granddaughter**; daughter of **Wendell Frayne**.
- Calls Judd Rennick "**my father's uncle**." Vesta (r01) calls her Judd's "**grand-niece**." (See §7, oddity — by the stated genealogy Judd is Wendell's *first cousin*, not his uncle.)
- Vesta (r01): "the young Frayne woman came round asking her questions, going house to house with her notebook, very polite, very quick."
- Judd (r03): "The Frayne girl came a while back and sat where you are sitting and read the whole book through at this table, and she did not talk while she read, which I appreciated. She asked to take it away with her. I let her have it. She will do something with it. I never did."

**Warren Tice** (r02, r04 "W.T."; r01 "old Warren Tice")
- **Resident engineer on the viaduct work**, "afterward the company's chief engineer at Ninestone and a considerable man in this county" (r02).
- The man Adela reported the two-inch setting to, and who overruled her (r04). The man who "never forgave that entry" (r02).
- Uncle of Dorsey Tice (r01).

**Dorsey Tice** (r01, r03)
- Kept **the store at Cadder Falls, "right there by the tracks."**
- **Old Warren Tice's nephew.** "The Tices were railroad people out of Ninestone, engineering people, big in the company, and Dorsey came down here and kept a store, which his family thought was a comedown and he did not."
- "He had the best hard candy in the county and he weighed it heavy for children."
- He and Judd "went at each other over that book for years, in a friendly way, the way men do."
- Lost the five-dollar bet and "told that story on himself for the rest of his life," "which is why people liked him."

**Mr. Coyle** (r03) — foreman of the bridge gang that re-decked the structure; "a decent man." Named in Judd's copied note as the source of the forty-tons figure: "Mr. Coyle says the deck is forty ton heavier than it was."

**Emil Rennick** — see under Judd. Carpenter; built the falsework.

**Wendell Frayne** — Ruth's father, Adela's son; received the field book in 1959 and passed it on.

**The Wexlers of Kettle Bench** (r04)
- **Peter Wexler** began a volunteer weather record (high, low, precipitation, every day).
- His daughter **Lettie Wexler** ("Miss Wexler") **has kept it since 1926**. She lent it to Ruth "in four cardboard boxes without asking what I wanted it for."
- "The Wexlers never wrote down a sound in their lives, and are related to nobody in this story."

**No. 9's later owner:** the **Tolliver Lumber Company**, Bly County.

## 2. Geography and the railroad

- **The Cadder Valley Railroad (CVR)** — "thirty-one miles of it, and no more" (r02). "Everything else the company owned was sidings."
- Mileposts (r02, confirmed by r03):
  - **Ninestone — milepost 0.** County seat, the shops, the office.
  - **Sallow Creek Viaduct — milepost 22.**
  - **Cadder Falls — milepost 23.** The mill town.
  - **Ashby quarry — milepost 31.** "the reason the whole thing existed."
- **Grade from the quarry down to the viaduct: two and two-tenths percent (2.2%), held the whole way.** r03: "two and two-tenths percent down the nine miles off the quarry" (31 − 22 = 9 miles; the two accounts agree).
- r02 on the grade: "a great deal of falling for a small railroad with second-hand brakes, and every man on the property knew it, and the company's answer was to hire careful men and pray for cold weather on the drums."
- Other places: **Kettle Bench** (the Wexler weather station); **Bly County**, "two hundred miles north" (the Tolliver Lumber Company and the Bly County Historical Rooms); **Sixmile** (where No. 9 was cut up); **Pell County** (the county).

## 3. The bridge itself

- **Begun April 1896.** **Line opened to Cadder Falls 14 November 1897.** (r02)
- **Three spans: 120 feet + a pin-connected truss of 300 feet across the gorge proper + 120 feet. 540 feet in all. Ninety-seven feet above the water.** (r02) — "I have stood on the deck of it as a boy with my father's hand in my collar and I can tell you that ninety-seven feet is a great plenty."
- **Center span pinned solid at its north end; the south end left free to travel**, resting on **a nest of rollers set in a cast pedestal — "the south shoe."** "the whole span breathed on that nest, out in summer and back in winter, for fifty-odd years." (r02)
- **The span was closed on 12 September 1897 with the thermometer at sixty-six degrees.** (r02 and r04 agree on date and temperature; r04's quotation adds the time, 3:40 p.m.)
- **Sheet 11 of the contract drawings** specified the travel allowed on the south nest — the sources disagree on the figure (see §7).
- **Chisel mark ("the setting mark") cut into the roller seat: at two inches.** Measured three times with a steel tape by Ruth Frayne, "the same answer each time."
- **1954 replacement bearings: rockers, not rollers, four inches of travel each way** (r04 measured them; r03 gives the same "four inches of travel each way").
- Falsework built by Emil Rennick (r03).

## 5. The sound

**What it was like — all three ear-witness accounts agree on the essentials:**

- **One report, not a rattle or a roll.** Vesta: "It was one noise… One deep report, and then nothing, and the nothing after it was worse than the noise was… Not sharp like a shot. Deep, the way a big thing sounds when it is a long way down." Judd: "It was one report. Deep. It was not a rattle and it was not a roll and it did not travel — it was there and then it was done."
- Vesta's mother's simile, which Vesta says she "has never heard put better": **"Like a door shut in a cellar three towns off."**
- **Felt before heard.** Vesta: "It came up through the floorboards into your feet, the way the mill did when they started the big saw, only the mill was daytime and this was not."
- **Judd adds a third, earlier stage: the rails.** "it came up the rails first… The sounder on my table would rattle in its bracket before the sound got to the room. A little dry rattle, like a bug in a box. Then the floor. Then the noise. I timed that gap once with a watch and could not get a reading on it, it is that short, but the order never changed in forty-three winters."
- **Always between midnight and four.** Vesta: "closer to two than either end, though I would not swear to it. Nobody heard it at supper. Nobody heard it at noon in their life." Judd: "Never in the evening. Never at daylight. Between midnight and four."
- **The valley called it "the boom"** (r04).
- **Not heard at Ninestone** (22 miles from the bridge): "We never heard it at Ninestone. My father would have written it down." (r02, closing line)

**"Killing nights" — the folk condition (r01):**
"Not just cold — we get cold, cold is nothing. It had to be the kind where the day had been soft, shirtsleeve weather almost, mud in the road, and then the sky went hard and clear along about dark and the whole night came to pieces on you. That was a killing night." Her father, at the mill gate at four in the afternoon: "**well, she may talk tonight**"; her mother told him not to say so in front of the children, "and he would say it anyway."

**Vesta's own night (a winter in the thirties; she cannot give the date):**
Rain that day; "the wash was on the line and it froze on the line, and my mother was cross about it." The whole street came awake at once — "Not one house. The whole street." Floor first, then the sound. Her brother up before her; her father already at the window with no lamp lit. Cold on the glass; **no wind at all**; dogs going off up and down the row one after another, then the dogs quit and "it was as still as the inside of a cup." Then doors opening: "Grown men out on their porches in their union suits in that cold, holding their arms, **looking up the valley toward the bridge**, and not a one of them saying anything… **They knew what they were listening for. It was not a surprise to them. It was a visit.**" Her mother put them back to bed and said, "**that is only the train, go to sleep**," "which was the exact wrong thing to say to a child if you wanted her asleep."

**Judd's first night (winter of 1913, by his account):**
On nights, alone in the office; "I thought a car had got away in the quarry and come down on us. I went out on the platform with a lantern and there was nothing wrong anywhere and it was so cold the lantern glass cracked. In the morning I ruled up a book."

## 6. The three explanations on offer

**(a) The valley's ghost story (r01).**
"Number Nine went into that gorge in the bad winter when the bridge was new, and her crew went in with her, and they were killed, and they were never brought up. They are down there yet, under the gravel and the water, and it is her working the grade that the valley hears. She comes down off the quarry the way she came down that morning and she cannot stop, and when she hits she makes that one sound." Source: Vesta's grandmother, "and she was here for it."
Onset per Vesta: **"that same winter, the first winter after they opened that bridge — that would be ninety-seven going into ninety-eight — right on the heels of the wreck. Not years after. That winter."** "Seventy-odd years of it, in the end."
Rival theory at the store: **ice.** "Ice, one of them said, ice in the gorge, and I said all right, then why not every cold night, why the killing nights only, and he didn't have anything for that."
Also current at the Falls: **the sound rang for deaths** — "that when she spoke somebody at the Falls would be dead inside the week" (r03).

**(b) Harlan Pike's weight theory (r02).**
The re-decking added forty tons. "Forty tons is forty tons. A nest of rollers under a bridge was never meant to carry what that new deck put upon it, and the noise the Falls made so much of began somewhere about that time and not before it. **I say the weight did it.** I have no drawing to show you and I do not need one; a man who has watched a scale all his life knows what happens when you add to a load and change nothing else." He flags it as "the one opinion in this chapter, since nobody else appears willing to."

**(c) Ruth Frayne's thermal-binding explanation (r04)** — the load-bearing arithmetic:
- Rule (from Adela's field book): **one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees.**
- **Sheet 11 called for three inches** of cold travel = **120 degrees of cooling**. Span closed at **66°**. 66 − 120 = **54 below zero**. "As drawn, that bridge would have run out of travel at fifty-four below, which this valley has never come anywhere near."
- **It was built to two inches** (the chisel mark) = **80 degrees**. 66 − 80 = **14 below zero**. "Also a temperature this valley does not reach. **And that is the reason nobody noticed anything for twelve years: a wrong bridge behaved exactly like a right one, because the difference between them lay in weather that never came.**"
- **August 1909:** the gang jacked both shoes and re-set them **to the same two-inch mark — with the steel at eighty-six degrees.** 86 − 80 = **six above zero.**
- "**Six above zero. Which is where Judd Rennick's night book says the bridge spoke, sixty-one times in forty-three winters, and never once above.**"
- Mechanism: "When the span cooled past its binding point the nest ran out of travel, the south shoe hung up, and the truss carried the load in stress until the shoe let go all at once and the span dropped into position with one report that ran along the rails. **It was not a train. It was a bridge, arriving where it should have been.**"
- The negative evidence she calls "the proof": between opening (Nov 1897) and the re-decking (Aug 1909) there were **seven nights at Kettle Bench below six degrees, four of them at the bottom of falls of thirty degrees or more.** Nothing was heard on any of them, "and nobody at the Falls has ever claimed there was." Coldest of those twelve years: **nine below, 4 February 1899** — short of the fourteen below the bridge as built would have required. "**The silence of those seven nights is not an absence of data. It is the proof.** If the error of 1897 had been enough by itself, the valley would have heard the bridge in 1899. It heard nothing. It took a summer afternoon twelve years later, and a gang doing careful work in good faith at eighty-six degrees, to finish what was begun at sixty-six."
- Corroboration: the Wexler record "**agrees with the station agent's book on every one of the sixty-one nights.** Two records kept by people who had no occasion to compare them, neither knowing what the other was for."

## 7. Numbers to keep straight

**Judd's night book (r03):**
- **Five columns: the date; the day's high; the night's low; whether she spoke; and the hour.** "That is all." No opinions recorded — "a man who writes down his opinion once will write down his opinion instead of the reading the next time."
- **Condition 1: the night's low below six degrees.** "Never once. Not at seven, not at six and a half. Below six."
- **Condition 2: the day before at least thirty degrees warmer than the night that followed.** "It had to fall thirty. A cold night on the end of a cold day was nothing to her."
- "**Both, every time. There is not an exception in the book, and I would know, because I went through it looking for one.**"
- **61 nights she spoke. 34 winters she spoke at least once. 9 winters she never spoke at all. 9 + 34 = 43 winters. 5 nights answered both conditions and gave nothing. 66 qualifying nights in all; on 5 of the 66 she kept still.** (61 + 5 = 66 — internally consistent.)
- Judd's one guess: **she would not speak twice inside one freeze.** "Once she had spoken she was done until there had been a thaw — a real thaw, up above forty in the daytime, not a mild hour at noon. Every one of the five silent nights sits in a cold spell where she had already spoken and where nothing had got above forty in between. **That is not a theory about what she was. It is a rule about when.**"
- **Deaths: 190 deaths at Cadder Falls in the years he kept the book; 7 of them fell inside a week after a night she spoke.** "I put the two numbers in the back of the book and I have never said them at the store and I am saying them to you because you asked and because you have a machine running."

**Bridge/engine figures:**
- Grade **2.2%**; **9 miles** quarry to bridge; road **31 miles**.
- Spans **120 + 300 + 120 = 540 ft**; **97 ft** above the water.
- Re-decking: **85-lb steel** in place of **60-lb**; new ties throughout; **both shoes jacked and re-set**; deck **forty tons heavier**; thermometer on the north pier **86° at four o'clock** when they seated the south nest; gang on the structure **three weeks**.
- No. 9: **4-4-0**, **Rowan Locomotive Works**, **built 1889**, **works number 1142**, cost **$8,600** new, sold for **$2,150**.
- Wreck: **two of the gang hurt, none killed**; **nine days to rerail her**.
- Frayne's inspection: creek dry **eleven weeks**; walked **over eight hundred feet** of bed; pedestal **forty feet below the abutment on the south side**, "a casting the size of a kitchen stove"; chisel mark measured **three times**.
- Wexler extremes: **101 degrees, July 1936** (highest in the series); **thirteen below, January 1918** (lowest in the series).
- The wager: **five dollars**; **January 1937**; **41 at noon**, **38 at four**, falling **four degrees an hour since one**; his sum "38 down to 6 is thirty-two degrees. At four degrees an hour that is eight hours" → under six about midnight; **no thaw since the second week of December**; **low that night was three below**; **she spoke at ten minutes past one**.

> *Rule for the long span: one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees.*

**(6) Adela Rennick's field book, page sixty-two (quoted in r04):**

## 9. Conflicts, contradictions and oddities

**Between sources:**

1. **Sheet 11's called-for travel.** r02 (Pike): "**Sheet 11 of the contract drawings called for four inches of travel on the south nest. Four inches was generous for a three-hundred-foot span.**" r04 (Adela's contemporary entry, and Frayne's report): "**Sheet 11 calls for three.**" Frayne's arithmetic uses three. Pike also can't easily source a Sheet 11 figure from the office, since he himself says the construction record burned in 1911 — worth flagging.

2. **The expansion rule.** r02: "one inch in three hundred feet **for fifty degrees**… a rule of thumb… you will find it in any office of that period." r04, quoting Adela's book: "one inch in three hundred feet **for forty degrees**." This is load-bearing: at 40°/in, two inches set at 86° binds at **six above** — matching Judd's threshold exactly. At 50°/in it would bind at fourteen below and the whole explanation collapses.

3. **The date of the re-decking.** r02: "**the re-decking, which was in 1908**… some weeks that summer." r03's copied note and r04: "**August 1909**," three weeks. Frayne's twelve-year silence (1897→1909) and the Jan 1912 onset depend on 1909.

4. **Judd contradicts his own quoted note.** Immediately after reading out the note dated **August 1909**, he says: "**That was the summer of 1911.**" His own stated rule is that he does not trust his memory against his own hand — which argues for 1909.

5. **The date of the rebuild for diesels.** r03: "**the rockers went in, in the spring of 1953**." r02: "**The end of the story is 1954, in March.**" r04: "**the 1954 gang**" (said twice, including in her written report). Vesta (r01) only says the railroad "were down there all that spring with their cranes and their trucks, doing it over for the diesels."

6. **When the sound began.** r01: winter **1897–98**, "right on the heels of the wreck. Not years after. That winter," and "seventy-odd years of it, in the end." r03: "**The first time she spoke was the winter of 1913.**" r04: "**the seventeenth of January, 1912… That is the night the sound began, and the night my father's uncle started his book.**" Vesta's "seventy-odd years" cannot be reconciled with 43 winters under any dating.

7. **When Judd started the book.** He says both "**I went on nights in 1907 and I started the book that same winter**" and, later, that the first sound was the winter of 1913 and "**In the morning I ruled up a book.**" Frayne's Jan 1912 date, plus 43 winters running to the second winter after the rockers, fits 1912 better than either of his own numbers.

8. **The wreck.** r01: No. 9 went **into the gorge**, the crew went with her, "**they were killed, and they were never brought up**," and are there yet. r02: she went off the iron **at the north approach**, at 7:20 a.m., **nobody killed**, two of the gang hurt, rerailed in nine days, "**in daylight, with half the county looking at her**," and sold away in 1901. r04: "**There is no locomotive in Sallow Creek**… no frame, no boiler shell, no driving wheel, nothing of iron larger than a spike," plus the Tolliver photostats. Note also the internal tension in r02: nine days to rerail vs the *Sentinel*'s "within the fortnight."

9. **The direction of the wager.** r01: "**Dorsey lost money to him over it once. Bet him she would speak on a certain night and she spoke.**" r03: Judd said she would speak; "**Dorsey bet me five dollars she would not**," and paid. Both agree Dorsey lost five dollars and told it on himself; Vesta has the sides reversed.

10. **What Judd's book recorded.** r01: "he put it in the book — **the night, and the cold, and the hour of it**" (three items, and only on nights she spoke). r03: **five columns**, kept every night including "whether she spoke" — which is how he could count the five qualifying nights that produced nothing. r04 says he "wrote down every occurrence of the sound," which understates the columns.

11. **Where Judd lived.** r04 (1963): "**lives three streets from the depot**." r01 (1974): "up on the hill there."

12. **Ruth Frayne's visit as remembered.** r01: "going house to house with her notebook, very polite, very quick," "come to look at his book, and he let her have it." r03: she sat at his table and read the whole book through in silence, then asked to take it away.

13. **Genealogical oddity.** By the stated facts — Adela was Emil's younger sister; Judd is Emil's son; Wendell Frayne is Adela's son — **Judd and Wendell are first cousins**, so Judd is Ruth's **first cousin once removed**, not "my father's uncle" (r04) and not her great-uncle / her Judd's "grand-niece" (r01). Both r01 and r04 use the wrong degree.

14. **Judd on Adela's dates.** "She worked for the same railroad **before I was born**." Judd was born 1888; Adela was born 1874 and worked on the viaduct party while it was building, **1896–97**, when Judd was eight or nine. His "before I was born" is impossible.

15. **Two mechanisms for the same trigger.** Pike and Frayne both point at the same summer's work on the bridge, but Pike blames **the forty tons of added deck weight** and Frayne blames **re-seating the nest at the same two-inch mark with the steel at 86°**. Only Frayne's account explains the sharp six-degree threshold; Pike offers no drawing and calls it an opinion. Note that the same event carries both changes, so Pike is not simply wrong about the timing.

**Internal oddities within a single source:**

16. **Judd's wager arithmetic doesn't close.** "It had made forty-one at noon. It was thirty-eight at four o'clock and it had been falling four degrees an hour since one." Four degrees an hour from 1 p.m. to 4 p.m. is a twelve-degree fall; from 41 that gives about 29 at four o'clock, not 38. His subsequent sum (38 − 6 = 32; at 4°/hr = 8 hours; therefore under six about midnight) is arithmetically right on its own terms.

17. **Vesta's self-dating.** Born 1920, she calls herself "young enough to be sour about it too" when the railroad rebuilt the bridge — she would have been 33 or 34.

18. **Pike says the span "breathed on that nest… for fifty-odd years"** — 1897 to 1953/54 is about fifty-six.

19. **Pike's memoir is written in 1958 "in his seventies,"** which places his birth in the middle 1880s; he was a small boy in the office at the time of the 1897–98 events he describes.

20. **"Two of that party were taken back on in the August following"** (r02) — the referent year of "the August following" is never stated; Pike also says Adela "was gone inside four years," measured from an unstated point (presumably the offending entry of Sept 1897, giving a departure by about 1901).

21. **Frayne's "Miss Wexler"** is Lettie Wexler, Peter's daughter, who has kept the record since 1926 — the same person named two ways in one paragraph.

22. **Frayne's number for the qualifying-but-silent nights.** She says "Further nights answered both conditions and produced nothing at all, and his own summing at the back of the book gives the count of them," but does not state the number; Judd gives it as **five**. (Judd also says the *back* of the book holds the 190/7 death numbers.)

23. **The whole-series low of thirteen below (Jan 1918)** is still short of the **fourteen below** the bridge as built (pre-1909) would have required — so on Frayne's own figures the original error would never once have produced a sound in the entire weather record. She uses the point implicitly; it is worth having explicit.

24. **The death-omen story dies on the count.** 7 out of 190. Judd: "arguing does not settle a count. **So I counted.**"

## 10. Lines worth having verbatim (character and voice)

- Vesta, on the town's reaction to Judd's habit: "**They would say, well, Judd's got another one in the book, the way you would say somebody's cat had kittens. He was not a strange man, that is what I want to say. He was the most ordinary man you ever saw in your life, and he had this one thing.**"
- Vesta, on the end: "**The Falls said the railroad had spoiled it.** That is exactly what they said, and they were sour about it… **Somebody had come from off with money and taken it away from us.** That is how it felt. You may laugh but you had to be here."
- Vesta's closing: "**I am not a foolish woman and I did not raise foolish children.** But I lay in that bed with the wash frozen on the line and I felt the floor move before I heard a thing, and there is not a man living can tell me what that was, and **I notice that the ones who are surest are the ones who were never in the house.**"
- Pike, on the fire: "**I do not think anybody at the time understood that a fire could take away a thing that had already happened.**"
- Pike, on Adela: "She did the arithmetic, and she did it better than the party did — **that is my father's phrase, not mine, and he was not a man who handed out compliments to save postage.**"
- Pike, on the valley's reaction to the rebuild: "**The valley took this hard, as though something had been stolen from it.**"
- Judd, on why he kept no opinions: "**a man who writes down his opinion once will write down his opinion instead of the reading the next time.**"
- Judd, on the rockers going in: "**and nobody told me a thing about it. I was the agent. I found out from a section man.**"
- Judd's summary of where he stands: "I have got sixty-one nights and a hundred and ninety deaths and two conditions that never failed, and **I have not got one word of explanation, and I am not going to make one up at my age.**"
- Frayne's opening: "I did not go out to Sallow Creek last September to settle anything. I went because the creek was going to be dry, and **a dry creek under a railroad bridge is an opportunity that does not come twice in a career.**" And: "The inspection was routine, and stopped being routine on the second afternoon."
- Frayne on Finding One: "**I state it flatly because it required no interpretation: I went and looked and it is not there.**"
- Frayne's close: "**My grandmother measured that bridge and was not believed. I measured it again sixty-five years later, with a better tape and worse handwriting, and she was right. That is all I have.**"
- Frayne's disposition of the artifacts: the **field book** to the Society this year; the **weather book** to come after Miss Wexler's death; the **night book** when Judd has done with it, "and I hope that is a long time yet"; **the pedestal** "in the entrance hall, where people can put a hand on it."
