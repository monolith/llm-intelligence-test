# Notes after R01–R08 — the Sallow Creek Viaduct / "the boom" at Cadder Falls

Handoff notes for a fresh reader. Eight retellings of one story, eight narrators, eight dates. Everything below comes only from the eight retellings (r01–r08). Where sources disagree, the disagreement is recorded, not resolved.

Everything from the earlier notes (r01–r04) is carried forward here unchanged in substance; material from r05–r08 is folded in and marked **[r05]**, **[r06]**, **[r07]**, **[r08]** where the provenance matters.

## 1. People, and how they connect

**Vesta Cobb** (r01, narrator)
- Born **1920**, "second house up from the mill gate," Cadder Falls.
- Father: in the mill his whole working life. Mother: her people "had been at the Falls since the line was built."
- Grandmother: was at the Falls *while* the line was being built — "she boarded the men who came in to do it"; grandmother "was here for it" (the wreck) and is the source of the ghost-train story.
- Brother: present the night she heard the sound (he "was up before I was").
- Husband: "used to say I could talk the paint off a porch." She was "grown and married" while the Dorsey Tice bet story was still being told.
- Everything she knows she "got at that stove from people who are all of them dead now."

**Harlan Pike** (r02, narrator)
- His father was **chief clerk of the Cadder Valley Railroad from 1893 to 1919** — "twenty-six years." Desk at Ninestone with "the window on the yard."
- "Other boys grew up with a dog. I grew up with a stock book." Sunday dinner-table talk about whether a figure in a column was "one the directors would swallow."
- Was a boy in the office ("I sat in that office as a boy while men talked over my head") and on the viaduct deck "with my father's hand in my collar."
- Motive: "there is nobody left who cares to have it right, and because what is said about the Sallow Creek Viaduct at present is mostly rubbish."

**Judd Rennick** (r03, narrator; appears in r01, r04, r06, r07, r08)
- Born **1888**, at the Falls. **Died 1969 [r07]** — the night book came to the Society "from the estate of Mr. Judd Rennick… who died that year."
- Father: **Emil Rennick**, a carpenter, who **built the falsework for the bridge** ("the timber they stand the iron on until the iron will stand by itself. Then you knock the timber down"). Proud of it; "he never got a line of credit for it anywhere."
- Emil had a **younger sister, Adela** — Judd's aunt. She "worked for the same railroad before I was born, in the engineering party, out in the field with the men. She never spoke about it. Not to me, not once." He asked twice as a boy and got nothing.
- Went **on nights in 1907** (his own account); **[r08] Dorsey says 1909.** Later made **agent** — **[r07] and [r08] both say 1915**. **[r07]: "station agent at Cadder Falls 1915–1954."** **[r08]: "he sat in that office until he retired in June of 1954."** Judd himself (r03): **thirty-nine years at that depot, all told**. "I still wake at four."
- Kept the night book **forty-three winters** (agreed by r03, r07, r08).
- In 1963 (r04) Ruth Frayne says he "is seventy-four" and "lives three streets from the depot." In 1974 (r01) Vesta says he "lived into his eighties, up on the hill there" — **born 1888, died 1969 = 81, so Vesta is right and both fit.**
- **[r08]** Dorsey on him: "He was a fixture. You do not ask a fixture questions." "He would have told anybody the numbers who asked him, and I want you to understand that in all those years I do not believe five people ever asked."

**Adela Rennick** (r02, r03, r04, r07 — never a narrator)
- **Born 1874, died 1959 at eighty-five** (r04). **[r07]: "born 1876 and died 1959."** (Conflict — see §9.)
- **Computer** for the engineering party (r02: "The party's computer was a Miss Adela Rennick"); r04 calls her "a computer and rodman for the railroad's engineering party while the viaduct was building."
- Kept **her own duplicate of the party's book, in her own hand, in black oilcloth, all her life** — "quite separate from the party's book, which was thought eccentric and was not" (r02). **[r07] provenance says she had it "in her own keeping from 1901 until her death in 1959."**
- Also "in the habit of entering in that book whatever she thought, including what she thought about the bridge, and one such entry got back to the resident engineer. **Tice never forgave that entry, and she was gone inside four years.**" (r02, who adds: "I have nothing in writing on the point and I do not pretend to… there are things you know from the temperature of a room.")
- "Her time with the road ended when the engineering party was reduced. **Two of that party were taken back on in the August following, for the Ashby extension work, and she was not**, and I have never needed anybody to explain that to me." (r02)
- **[r07] The paperwork now exists.** Loose in the back of her field book: a CVR letter to A. Rennick, **30 April 1901**, saying the party "is reduced entire on the 15th May next," and — folded inside it — a printed slip of **20 July 1901** re-engaging **J. Corliss, rodman** and **F. Wain, chainman** from 1 August 1901 for the Ashby extension. This pins Pike's "the August following" to **August 1901** and his "gone inside four years" to **Sept 1897 → May 1901 (three years eight months)** — both check out.
- **But the same letter says: "Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card."** Tice recommended her. (See §9.)
- Her book passed to her son **Wendell Frayne** on her death (1959), and he gave it to his daughter Ruth when she said she was going out to the creek. **[r07]: Wendell deposited it with the Society in 1963; "depositor's slip is in his hand and is not signed."**

**Ruth Frayne** (r04, narrator; appears in r01, r03, r06, r07)
- **Engineer with the Pell County highway department, three years in it.** **[r06]: "she does the county's road surveys."**
- Adela Rennick's **granddaughter**; daughter of **Wendell Frayne**.
- Calls Judd Rennick "**my father's uncle**." Vesta (r01) calls her Judd's "**grand-niece**." **[r07] settles it: "Her father, Mr. Wendell Frayne… and Judd Rennick, who wrote the night book, were first cousins."** So Judd is Ruth's **first cousin once removed**; both r01 and r04 use the wrong degree. (See §9 #13.)
- Vesta (r01): "the young Frayne woman came round asking her questions, going house to house with her notebook, very polite, very quick."
- Judd (r03): "The Frayne girl came a while back and sat where you are sitting and read the whole book through at this table, and she did not talk while she read, which I appreciated. She asked to take it away with her. I let her have it. She will do something with it. I never did."
- **[r06]** Lettie: "In 1962 the Frayne girl came up the hill… She wanted the whole record. I gave it to her in four cardboard boxes… and she brought every box back, which I will say for her, because not everybody does." Frayne had **Judd's night book and Lettie's weather record out on Lettie's kitchen table at the same time**.
- **[r07]** Sable never names her — "The author of Item 5 is the granddaughter of the writer of the field book"; "a county highway engineer."

**Warren Tice** (r02, r04 "W.T."; r01 "old Warren Tice"; r08 at length)
- **Resident engineer on the viaduct work**, "afterward the company's chief engineer at Ninestone and a considerable man in this county" (r02).
- The man Adela reported the two-inch setting to, and who overruled her (r04). The man who "never forgave that entry" (r02).
- **[r01, r02] Uncle of Dorsey Tice. [r08] Dorsey says he was his FATHER.** (Major conflict — see §9 #25.)
- **[r08] Died 1937.** **Retirement dinner in the spring of 1931**, which Dorsey attended.
- **[r08] Dorsey's denial:** "My father had no hand in that bridge. He was in the Ninestone office the whole time it was building. People in this town have a way of putting a man where the story wants him… he was a railroad engineer, a good one, and he finished as chief engineer of the Cadder Valley up at Ninestone… and the bridge is the last thing he would have wanted his name hung on."
- **[r08] Dorsey's contradiction of his own denial, later in the same session:** "He took me out on the deck when I was six and told me he had closed the span himself in ninety-seven… He held the back of my collar the whole time." And: "he never in his life spoke to me about that bridge."
- **[r08] A story in circulation:** something Warren Tice is supposed to have said at the 1931 retirement dinner, reaching Dorsey "at second or third hand." Dorsey refuses to repeat it: "I was at that dinner. He said nothing of the kind… Now that is the end of it." **The content of the story is never given by any source.**

**Dorsey Tice** (r08, narrator; appears in r01, r03)
- **Born March 1893**, at Cadder Falls, "in the house that stood where the feed shed is now." Sixty-eight in March 1961.
- Kept **the store at Cadder Falls, "right there by the tracks."**
- **[r01, r02] Old Warren Tice's nephew; [r08] his son.** "The Tices were railroad people out of Ninestone, engineering people, big in the company, and Dorsey came down here and kept a store, which his family thought was a comedown and he did not." (r01)
- "He had the best hard candy in the county and he weighed it heavy for children." (r01)
- He and Judd "went at each other over that book for years, in a friendly way, the way men do." (r01)
- Lost the five-dollar bet and "told that story on himself for the rest of his life," "which is why people liked him." (r01) — **[r08] confirms: "I have made more than five dollars' worth out of telling about losing it, so I came out ahead, which is more than the railroad did."**
- **[r08] On his father:** "a big quiet man who came home once a week when I was small and stood in the kitchen with his hat still on until my mother told him to sit. He built things all over this county and he never talked about one of them at his own table." "He was proud of it. He was not a proud man about much."
- **[r08]** Kept nothing on paper: "There is nothing in this store on paper but the day book, and the day book says what people owed me and nothing about bridges. Judd was the writing one. Judd wrote down what happened and I sold kerosene to the people it happened to."
- **[r08]** "My father had the county in him and I never got farther than this counter, and I have not been sorry about it more than twice."

**Anders Berg** **[r08 — NEW NAME]**
- "**Anders Berg set that nest. Everybody knew it. That is not a thing anybody in this town ever had to look up.**" Dropped in as a non-sequitur, apparently answering an off-tape question, immediately after the retirement-dinner refusal. No other source mentions him. No other source names anyone as having physically set the nest.

**Lidell and Sherrod** **[r07 — NEW NAMES]**
- The two men hurt in the 6 March 1898 wreck, from Adela's field book page 71: "**Lidell's arm broke, Sherrod scalded at the deck.**" No source had named them before.

**J. Corliss (rodman) and F. Wain (chainman)** **[r07 — NEW NAMES]**
- The two of Adela's engineering party re-engaged 1 August 1901 for the Ashby extension while she was not.

**Mr. Coyle** (r03, r07) — foreman of the bridge gang that re-decked the structure; "a decent man." Named in Judd's copied note as the source of the forty-tons figure: "Mr. Coyle says the deck is forty ton heavier than it was."

**Emil Rennick** — see under Judd. Carpenter; built the falsework. **[r07] confirms Emil and Adela were brother and sister.**

**Wendell Frayne** — Ruth's father, Adela's son; received the field book in 1959 and passed it on. **[r07]: deposited Item 1 in 1963; first cousin to Judd Rennick; still living in 1970.**

**The Wexlers of Kettle Bench** (r04, r06, r07)
- **Peter Wexler** began a volunteer weather record (high, low, precipitation, every day). **[r06] He began it in 1884 and kept it until 1926.**
- His daughter **Lettie Wexler** ("Miss Wexler") **has kept it since 1926**. She lent it to Ruth "in four cardboard boxes without asking what I wanted it for" (r04). **[r06] She was seventy-six in 1965. [r07] She died in 1967, "the same year her boxes came in."**
- "The Wexlers never wrote down a sound in their lives, and are related to nobody in this story" (r04). **[r06] Lettie's own version: "We were related to nobody in this business — not to the railroad people, not to the mill people, not to the Cadder Falls families that the tale belongs to. My father had no interest in the bridge and neither have I. In eighty-odd years of pages there is not one word about it, because we never wrote down a noise. We wrote down weather."**
- **[r07] Sable's provenance is wrong: "The record was kept by two brothers named Wexler, the younger of whom carried it on after the elder gave it up."** (Father and daughter, not two brothers.)

**The Tollivers of Sixmile, Bly County** **[r05 — entirely new]**
- **Silas Tolliver**, founder. **Born 1849**; came into Bly County "with a mill, a horse, and a conviction that the horse was temporary." "That is the whole man in one sentence and I have never found a better one." Built the mill twice, "the second time out of the first one's lumber." **Died 1922, "in the chair by the office stove."** The paper's obituary led with the horse — "he had spent his whole life getting the horse out of his business and then went and left it in his obituary anyway."
- **Pearl Tolliver Nace**, his daughter, Alma's mother. **Born 1881**; kept the company's books **from the age of eighteen, "which is to say for thirty-nine years"** (i.e. c. 1899–1938), "because she was better at it than anybody her father could hire. That is not my phrase. That is the phrase he used, in front of company, and she never once let on that she liked hearing it."
- **Alma Nace**, narrator, **sixty-three in 1970** (so born c. 1907); "grew up inside the sound of the engines and remembers her mother's ledgers the way other people remember furniture." Was **thirty-one in 1938**.
- **Ravel Brothers** — scrap dealers who bought No. 4 for $470; "Ravel took the tires and the brass out separately, being men who knew what they were about."
- The **Pell County crew** who delivered the engine: handed her over at the interchange, "drank coffee in our kitchen, and turned around and went home on the cars like passengers." Pearl fed them. "She said the fireman was the politest man she ever met and had a burn on his wrist he would not talk about."

**Merrit Sable** (r07, narrator) — curator, Pell County Historical Society, Ninestone. Working under pressure; explicit about it: "Provenance from the depositors' slips where slips exist and from inquiry where they do not. My guesses are marked as guesses where I remembered to mark them."

**No. 9's later owner:** the **Tolliver Lumber Company**, Bly County.

## 2. Geography and the railroad

- **The Cadder Valley Railroad (CVR)** — "thirty-one miles of it, and no more" (r02). "Everything else the company owned was sidings."
- Mileposts (r02, confirmed by r03):
  - **Ninestone — milepost 0.** County seat, the shops, the office.
  - **Sallow Creek Viaduct — milepost 22.**
  - **Cadder Falls — milepost 23.** The mill town.
  - **Ashby quarry — milepost 31.** "the reason the whole thing existed."
- **Grade from the quarry down to the viaduct: two and two-tenths percent (2.2%), held the whole way.** r03: "two and two-tenths percent down the nine miles off the quarry" (31 − 22 = 9 miles; the two accounts agree).
- r02 on the grade: "a great deal of falling for a small railroad with second-hand brakes, and every man on the property knew it, and the company's answer was to hire careful men and pray for cold weather on the drums."
- Other places: **Kettle Bench** (the Wexler weather station) — **[r06] "three miles up the valley from Cadder Falls, on the shoulder above the creek where the frost sits longer than it does in town"**; **[r07] "three miles above Cadder Falls."** **Bly County**, "two hundred miles north" (the Tolliver Lumber Company and the Bly County Historical Rooms) — **[r07] confirms "two hundred miles north"**; **Sixmile** (where No. 9 was cut up); **Pell County** (the county); **the Ashby extension** (1901 work).

**The Sixmile tram [r05]**
- "Named for the creek and not for its length, which is a thing I have had to explain to visitors my whole life. It began at about that length, I grant you, but it did not stay there."
- **By the end: nineteen miles of thirty-pound rail running up three drainages**, with switchbacks where the ground would not take a curve, "and grades that the men described in language my mother would not enter in a book."
- "The rail was light and the ties were whatever came off the hill nearest, and it hauled more timber than anything in this county before or since."

## 3. The bridge itself

- **Begun April 1896.** **Line opened to Cadder Falls 14 November 1897.** (r02)
- **Three spans: 120 feet + a pin-connected truss of 300 feet across the gorge proper + 120 feet. 540 feet in all. Ninety-seven feet above the water.** (r02) — "I have stood on the deck of it as a boy with my father's hand in my collar and I can tell you that ninety-seven feet is a great plenty."
- **Center span pinned solid at its north end; the south end left free to travel**, resting on **a nest of rollers set in a cast pedestal — "the south shoe."** "the whole span breathed on that nest, out in summer and back in winter, for fifty-odd years." (r02)
- **The span was closed on 12 September 1897 with the thermometer at sixty-six degrees.** (r02 and r04 agree on date and temperature; r04's quotation adds the time, 3:40 p.m.; **[r07] transcribes the same entry, same wording, from page 62 of the field book.**)
- **Sheet 11 of the contract drawings** specified the travel allowed on the south nest — the sources disagree on the figure (see §9 #1).
- **Chisel mark ("the setting mark") cut into the roller seat: at two inches.** Measured three times with a steel tape by Ruth Frayne, "the same answer each time." **[r07] the object's own card: "Chisel mark on the roller seat at two inches."**
- **1954 replacement bearings: rockers, not rollers, four inches of travel each way** (r04 measured them; r03 gives the same "four inches of travel each way").
- Falsework built by Emil Rennick (r03).
- **[r08] Dorsey: "Anders Berg set that nest."**
- **[r07] The recovered pedestal is broken at one corner, takes two men to move, and sits "on the floor of the north store room where it will have to stay."**

## 5. The sound

**What it was like — all four ear-witness accounts (r01, r03, r08, plus the engine-echo in r05) agree on the essentials:**

- **One report, not a rattle or a roll.** Vesta: "It was one noise… One deep report, and then nothing, and the nothing after it was worse than the noise was… Not sharp like a shot. Deep, the way a big thing sounds when it is a long way down." Judd: "It was one report. Deep. It was not a rattle and it was not a roll and it did not travel — it was there and then it was done." **[r08] Dorsey: "It was one sound. That is the first thing people get wrong — they will tell you it went on, that it rolled, that it repeated. It did not. One deep report, down low, the kind you take in through your feet before your ears catch up."**
- Vesta's mother's simile, which Vesta says she "has never heard put better": **"Like a door shut in a cellar three towns off."** **[r08] Dorsey's simile: "Like somebody dropped something heavy two rooms away in a house made all of one piece."**
- **Felt before heard.** Vesta: "It came up through the floorboards into your feet, the way the mill did when they started the big saw, only the mill was daytime and this was not." **[r08] "You would be asleep upstairs and the floor would come up and meet you. That is as near as I can put it. Not a bang. A report."**
- **Judd adds a third, earlier stage: the rails.** "it came up the rails first… The sounder on my table would rattle in its bracket before the sound got to the room. A little dry rattle, like a bug in a box. Then the floor. Then the noise. I timed that gap once with a watch and could not get a reading on it, it is that short, but the order never changed in forty-three winters."
- **Always between midnight and four.** Vesta: "closer to two than either end, though I would not swear to it. Nobody heard it at supper. Nobody heard it at noon in their life." Judd: "Never in the evening. Never at daylight. Between midnight and four." **[r08] "Small hours, always. I never heard it before midnight in my life and I have been awake for a good many midnights in this building."**
- **Never twice in one night.** **[r08] "And never twice in the one night. Whatever she was going to say, she said it once and was done."** (Adjacent to but distinct from Judd's rule that she would not speak twice inside one *freeze*.)
- **The valley called it "the boom"** (r04). **[r08] "Some called it the train. I let them."**
- **Not heard at Ninestone** (22 miles from the bridge): "We never heard it at Ninestone. My father would have written it down." (r02, closing line)

**"Killing nights" — the folk condition (r01):**
"Not just cold — we get cold, cold is nothing. It had to be the kind where the day had been soft, shirtsleeve weather almost, mud in the road, and then the sky went hard and clear along about dark and the whole night came to pieces on you. That was a killing night." Her father, at the mill gate at four in the afternoon: "**well, she may talk tonight**"; her mother told him not to say so in front of the children, "and he would say it anyway."

**[r08] Dorsey's version of the same condition:** "It only ever came in the hard sudden cold — the kind of day that is decent at dinner time and murderous by dark. Never in a slow cold. **We could go three weeks at zero and never hear a thing**, and then one afternoon the bottom would drop out of the thermometer and **every old woman on this street would say, we will hear her tonight. And half the time we did.**"

**[r06] Lettie's version, from the weather side:** "Thirty-six degrees of fall in a day, and clear and still after it. That is the kind of night the cold comes down out of the sky and just sits."

**Vesta's own night (a winter in the thirties; she cannot give the date):**
Rain that day; "the wash was on the line and it froze on the line, and my mother was cross about it." The whole street came awake at once — "Not one house. The whole street." Floor first, then the sound. Her brother up before her; her father already at the window with no lamp lit. Cold on the glass; **no wind at all**; dogs going off up and down the row one after another, then the dogs quit and "it was as still as the inside of a cup." Then doors opening: "Grown men out on their porches in their union suits in that cold, holding their arms, **looking up the valley toward the bridge**, and not a one of them saying anything… **They knew what they were listening for. It was not a surprise to them. It was a visit.**" Her mother put them back to bed and said, "**that is only the train, go to sleep**," "which was the exact wrong thing to say to a child if you wanted her asleep."

**Judd's first night (winter of 1913, by his account):**
On nights, alone in the office; "I thought a car had got away in the quarry and come down on us. I went out on the platform with a lantern and there was nothing wrong anywhere and it was so cold the lantern glass cracked. In the morning I ruled up a book."

**[r05] The engine's sound — an accidental near-rhyme worth noting:**
Alma Nace on No. 4 (the same locomotive the ghost story puts in the gorge), at Sixmile, two hundred miles away: "She had a whistle you could tell from any other whistle in the county, and **when the loads came down the main stem in the evening you could feel her in the floor of the house before you heard her.** Every child raised at Sixmile could tell you where she was on the hill with their eyes shut." The identical *felt-before-heard* phrasing attaches, in Bly County, to the living engine and, in Pell County, to the bridge that was blamed on her ghost.

## 6. The three explanations on offer

**(a) The valley's ghost story (r01).**
"Number Nine went into that gorge in the bad winter when the bridge was new, and her crew went in with her, and they were killed, and they were never brought up. They are down there yet, under the gravel and the water, and it is her working the grade that the valley hears. She comes down off the quarry the way she came down that morning and she cannot stop, and when she hits she makes that one sound." Source: Vesta's grandmother, "and she was here for it."
Onset per Vesta: **"that same winter, the first winter after they opened that bridge — that would be ninety-seven going into ninety-eight — right on the heels of the wreck. Not years after. That winter."** "Seventy-odd years of it, in the end."
Rival theory at the store: **ice.** "Ice, one of them said, ice in the gorge, and I said all right, then why not every cold night, why the killing nights only, and he didn't have anything for that."
Also current at the Falls: **the sound rang for deaths** — "that when she spoke somebody at the Falls would be dead inside the week" (r03).

**(b) Harlan Pike's weight theory (r02).**
The re-decking added forty tons. "Forty tons is forty tons. A nest of rollers under a bridge was never meant to carry what that new deck put upon it, and the noise the Falls made so much of began somewhere about that time and not before it. **I say the weight did it.** I have no drawing to show you and I do not need one; a man who has watched a scale all his life knows what happens when you add to a load and change nothing else." He flags it as "the one opinion in this chapter, since nobody else appears willing to."

**(c) Ruth Frayne's thermal-binding explanation (r04)** — the load-bearing arithmetic:
- Rule (from Adela's field book): **one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees.** **[r07] independently transcribes the same line off the inside cover of the field book — corroborating r04 against r02's "fifty degrees."**
- **Sheet 11 called for three inches** of cold travel = **120 degrees of cooling**. Span closed at **66°**. 66 − 120 = **54 below zero**. "As drawn, that bridge would have run out of travel at fifty-four below, which this valley has never come anywhere near."
- **It was built to two inches** (the chisel mark) = **80 degrees**. 66 − 80 = **14 below zero**. "Also a temperature this valley does not reach. **And that is the reason nobody noticed anything for twelve years: a wrong bridge behaved exactly like a right one, because the difference between them lay in weather that never came.**"
- **August 1909:** the gang jacked both shoes and re-set them **to the same two-inch mark — with the steel at eighty-six degrees.** 86 − 80 = **six above zero.**
- "**Six above zero. Which is where Judd Rennick's night book says the bridge spoke, sixty-one times in forty-three winters, and never once above.**"
- Mechanism: "When the span cooled past its binding point the nest ran out of travel, the south shoe hung up, and the truss carried the load in stress until the shoe let go all at once and the span dropped into position with one report that ran along the rails. **It was not a train. It was a bridge, arriving where it should have been.**"
- The negative evidence she calls "the proof": between opening (Nov 1897) and the re-decking (Aug 1909) there were **seven nights at Kettle Bench below six degrees, four of them at the bottom of falls of thirty degrees or more.** Nothing was heard on any of them, "and nobody at the Falls has ever claimed there was." Coldest of those twelve years: **nine below, 4 February 1899** — short of the fourteen below the bridge as built would have required. "**The silence of those seven nights is not an absence of data. It is the proof.** If the error of 1897 had been enough by itself, the valley would have heard the bridge in 1899. It heard nothing. It took a summer afternoon twelve years later, and a gang doing careful work in good faith at eighty-six degrees, to finish what was begun at sixty-six."
- Corroboration: the Wexler record "**agrees with the station agent's book on every one of the sixty-one nights.** Two records kept by people who had no occasion to compare them, neither knowing what the other was for."

**[r06] Lettie Wexler independently confirms the whole evidentiary base of (c) — from the other side of the valley and without knowing what it was for:**
- She personally did the twelve-year count "page by page over two evenings": **"In those twelve years there were seven nights below six degrees. Four of the seven came after days at least thirty degrees warmer than the night that followed. The coldest reading of the whole twelve years was nine below, on the fourth of February, 1899. I remember writing that out for her twice because she wanted the date as well as the figure."**
- The two mild winters: **"The winter of 1909 and 1910 and the winter of 1910 and 1911 never went below nine above zero, either one of them, and the coldest morning of the pair was the third of February, 1911. That is a mild pair of winters for this valley and I have no explanation for them except that we got them."**
- Her cross-check of the two books: "**Every night his book says the bridge spoke, my book says the night went under six degrees. Not one exception in what I looked at. And every one of those nights came after a day at least thirty degrees warmer than the night that followed it — not a gradual cold, a dropping cold. I took a dozen of his dates at random and turned to them in my own pages, and they agreed, every one, to the degree.** That surprised me more than it should have. He was writing down a sound in a station at two in the morning and I was writing down a thermometer on a milk house wall three miles off, and neither of us ever spoke to the other about it, and **the two books lie down together like they were kept by one person.**"
- **Note the scope difference:** Lettie's own check was a *sample* of a dozen dates plus a scan; Frayne (r04) claims agreement on **all sixty-one**. Frayne's is the stronger claim and rests on her own work, not Lettie's.
- Lettie offers **no theory at all**: "The noise stopped some years back. I could not tell you the year within two and I will not guess at it in print. There was talk up here at the time about why, and there is always talk up here about why. **I have no theory. I have a thermometer, and it has gone on saying the same three things a day since, and none of them was ever a reason.**"

**[r05] Alma Nace declines the question entirely:** "The county the Four came from had some noise on a bridge down there — a sound in the winters that they made stories about, and I gather there was a good deal of talk about it at one time. **I never knew the rights of it and I never inquired.** It was two hundred miles away and it had nothing to do with our engine, who spent those years hauling logs off three drainages in Bly County with a whistle you could hear at the schoolhouse." And her closing line: "**Somebody else can have the bridge.**"

**[r08] Dorsey Tice offers no mechanism either** — only the pattern (one report, small hours, hard sudden cold, never twice in a night) and the town's grief at losing it.

## 7. Numbers to keep straight

**Judd's night book — the book's own back-page summary [r07], which is the authority:**
> *Forty-three winters. Sixty-one nights she spoke. Thirty-four winters she spoke at least once and nine she never spoke at all. Five nights answered both the conditions and gave nothing, which makes sixty-six nights that answered them in all.*

- **Five columns [r03, r07]: the date; the day's high; the night's low; whether the sound came; the hour it came.** "That is all." No opinions recorded — "a man who writes down his opinion once will write down his opinion instead of the reading the next time."
- **Condition 1: the night's low below six degrees.** "Never once. Not at seven, not at six and a half. Below six."
- **Condition 2: the day before at least thirty degrees warmer than the night that followed.** "It had to fall thirty. A cold night on the end of a cold day was nothing to her."
- "**Both, every time. There is not an exception in the book, and I would know, because I went through it looking for one.**"
- **61 nights she spoke. 34 winters she spoke at least once. 9 winters she never spoke at all. 9 + 34 = 43 winters. 5 nights answered both conditions and gave nothing. 66 qualifying nights in all.** (61 + 5 = 66 — internally consistent, and confirmed word for word by the curator's transcription.)
- **Competing counts:** **[r06] Lettie read the same back page and reports "sixty-one nights she spoke" but "eight nights answered both the conditions and gave nothing… So sixty-nine nights in all."** **[r08] Dorsey reports "fifty-seven nights… Thirty winters she spoke in and thirteen she never opened her mouth at all — thirty and thirteen, and there is your forty-three."** Dorsey's pair also sums to 43, so it is internally tidy and externally wrong.
- **[r06, r07] The fullest winter in the book is 1917–18, with five nights** — more than any other winter. Lettie: "which surprised me not at all, since that was our own worst winter."
- **[r07] The last night entered as heard: 9 February 1954**, one month before the new bearings went in.
- Judd's one guess: **she would not speak twice inside one freeze.** "Once she had spoken she was done until there had been a thaw — a real thaw, up above forty in the daytime, not a mild hour at noon. Every one of the five silent nights sits in a cold spell where she had already spoken and where nothing had got above forty in between. **That is not a theory about what she was. It is a rule about when.**"
- **Deaths: 190 deaths at Cadder Falls in the years he kept the book; 7 of them fell inside a week after a night she spoke.** "I put the two numbers in the back of the book and I have never said them at the store and I am saying them to you because you asked and because you have a machine running." **[r07] Sable's transcription of the back page does NOT include the death figures** — he transcribes only the winters/nights summary. **[r08] Dorsey's version of the same count is inverted: "Better than a third of the burying in this town came inside the week after a night she spoke, he told me once."** 7 of 190 is about 3.7%. (Note that Judd said he never gave the numbers at the store, which is consistent with Dorsey never having had them right.)

**Bridge/engine figures:**
- Grade **2.2%**; **9 miles** quarry to bridge; road **31 miles**.
- Spans **120 + 300 + 120 = 540 ft**; **97 ft** above the water.
- Re-decking: **85-lb steel** in place of **60-lb**; new ties throughout; **both shoes jacked and re-set**; deck **forty tons heavier**; thermometer on the north pier **86° at four o'clock** when they seated the south nest; gang on the structure **three weeks**.
- No. 9: **4-4-0**, **Rowan Locomotive Works**, **built 1889**, **works number 1142**, cost **$8,600** new, sold for **$2,150** (CVR stock book) / bought for **$2,510** (Alma Nace). **[r05] Renumbered 4 at Sixmile; scrapped for $470.**
- Wreck: **two of the gang hurt, none killed** (**[r07] Lidell's arm broke, Sherrod scalded at the deck**); **nine days to rerail her**.
- Frayne's inspection: creek dry **eleven weeks**; walked **over eight hundred feet** of bed; pedestal **forty feet below the abutment on the south side**, "a casting the size of a kitchen stove" (**[r07] broken at one corner, two men to move**); chisel mark measured **three times**.
- Wexler extremes: **101 degrees, July 1936** (highest in the series — all three sources agree); lowest in the series **thirteen below, January 1918 (r04)** vs **eleven below, January 1918 (r06 and r07's index sheet)**.
- The wager: **five dollars**; **January 1937 (r03)** / **14 January 1936 (r08)**; **41 at noon**, **38 at four**, falling **four degrees an hour since one**; Judd's sum "38 down to 6 is thirty-two degrees. At four degrees an hour that is eight hours" → under six about midnight; **no thaw since the second week of December (r03)**; **low that night was three below**; **she spoke at ten minutes past one**. Both men give the same three temperature figures and the same outcome.

**[r05] Tolliver figures:**
- Sixmile tram: **nineteen miles** of **thirty-pound rail**, **three drainages**.
- Pearl kept the books **thirty-nine years** from age **eighteen**.
- **$2,510** paid; **$8,600** the engine cost new; **$470** scrap.
- **Eleven days and four railroads** on the delivery trip.
- **Twenty-seven years** she worked for Tolliver.
- Repairs: boiler retube **1909**; new tires **1913**; frame welded **1919** and **1922**; full shopping **1924**.
- **1938** deposit: stock book, shop book, timber ledgers, **eleven boxes of correspondence**; **twenty-four years** with no inquiry.

**[r06] Wexler method figures:**
- **Three figures a day and nothing else:** day's high, night's low, rain (or snow measured melted).
- Thermometer **on the north wall of the milk house where the sun never touches it**; "it has never been moved."
- **Readings at daylight and again at four**; "the low is the low at daylight unless the night was strange, in which case you say so in the margin and do not argue about it."

## 9. Conflicts, contradictions and oddities

**Between sources:**

1. **Sheet 11's called-for travel.** r02 (Pike): "**Sheet 11 of the contract drawings called for four inches of travel on the south nest. Four inches was generous for a three-hundred-foot span.**" r04 (Adela's contemporary entry, and Frayne's report) and **r07 (the same entry, transcribed independently by the curator)**: "**Sheet 11 calls for three.**" Frayne's arithmetic uses three. Pike also can't easily source a Sheet 11 figure from the office, since he himself says the construction record burned in 1911. **Now two-to-one against Pike, with the contemporary document on the majority side.**

2. **The expansion rule.** r02: "one inch in three hundred feet **for fifty degrees**… a rule of thumb… you will find it in any office of that period." r04, quoting Adela's book: "one inch in three hundred feet **for forty degrees**." **[r07] independently transcribes forty from the inside cover.** This is load-bearing: at 40°/in, two inches set at 86° binds at **six above** — matching Judd's threshold exactly. At 50°/in it would bind at fourteen below and the whole explanation collapses. **Two independent transcriptions of the physical book now say forty; Pike is quoting a general rule of thumb from memory.**

3. **The date of the re-decking.** r02: "**the re-decking, which was in 1908**… some weeks that summer." r03's copied note, r04, **and r07's transcription of the same note**: "**August 1909**," three weeks. **[r06] Lettie's twelve-year span, done by her from the pages, is "from the winter of 1897 and 1898 through to the summer of 1909"** — independent support for 1909. Frayne's twelve-year silence (1897→1909) and the Jan 1912 onset depend on 1909.

4. **Judd contradicts his own quoted note.** Immediately after reading out the note dated **August 1909**, he says: "**That was the summer of 1911.**" His own stated rule is that he does not trust his memory against his own hand — which argues for 1909.

5. **The date of the rebuild for diesels.** r03: "**the rockers went in, in the spring of 1953**." r02: "**The end of the story is 1954, in March.**" r04: "**the 1954 gang**" (said twice, including in her written report). **[r07]: "The new bearings went into the bridge in March 1954," and the last night entered as heard is 9 February 1954. [r08] Dorsey: "Then in fifty-four the railroad went in there."** Vesta (r01) only says the railroad "were down there all that spring with their cranes and their trucks, doing it over for the diesels." **Four sources to one; Judd's 1953 is the outlier, and the book's own last entry (2 March 1956, "second winter since the rockers went in") fits March 1954 exactly.**

6. **When the sound began.** r01: winter **1897–98**, "right on the heels of the wreck. Not years after. That winter," and "seventy-odd years of it, in the end." r03: "**The first time she spoke was the winter of 1913.**" r04: "**the seventeenth of January, 1912… That is the night the sound began, and the night my father's uncle started his book.**" Vesta's "seventy-odd years" cannot be reconciled with 43 winters under any dating.
   **[NEW arithmetic enabled by r07]** The book ran **forty-three winters, unbroken**, and its last entry is dated **2 March 1956** (the second winter after the March 1954 rockers). Counting 43 winters back so that the last is 1955–56 puts the first at **1913–14**, which fits Judd's "winter of 1913" better than Frayne's January 1912. If instead the book opened on 17 January 1912 and closed 2 March 1956, that is **45** winters, not 43. **Frayne's Jan-1912 start and the book's own "forty-three winters" cannot both be right.**

7. **When Judd started the book.** He says both "**I went on nights in 1907 and I started the book that same winter**" and, later, that the first sound was the winter of 1913 and "**In the morning I ruled up a book.**" **[r08] Dorsey says Judd went on nights in 1909**, not 1907. **[r07] gives his agency as 1915–1954**, and **[r08]** agrees on 1915 and June 1954. Judd's own "thirty-nine years at that depot, all told" matches **1915–1954 exactly (39 years)** — i.e. his agency, not his whole time there (1907→1954 = 47; 1909→1954 = 45). One of the three numbers is being misapplied.

8. **The wreck.** r01: No. 9 went **into the gorge**, the crew went with her, "**they were killed, and they were never brought up**," and are there yet. r02: she went off the iron **at the north approach**, at 7:20 a.m., **nobody killed**, two of the gang hurt, rerailed in nine days, "**in daylight, with half the county looking at her**," and sold away in 1901. r04: "**There is no locomotive in Sallow Creek**… no frame, no boiler shell, no driving wheel, nothing of iron larger than a spike," plus the Tolliver photostats. **[r07] adds a contemporary field-book entry (page 71) naming the two injured men and confirming the hour, the location, and the nine days, and confirms the newspaper file agrees. [r05] adds the buyer's own family memory of the engine steaming north and working 27 more years.** Internal tension remaining in r02: nine days to rerail vs the *Sentinel*'s "within the fortnight." **The ghost version is now contradicted by four independent record classes: newspaper, field book, stock book, and the purchaser's family.**

9. **The direction of the wager.** r01: "**Dorsey lost money to him over it once. Bet him she would speak on a certain night and she spoke.**" r03: Judd said she would speak; "**Dorsey bet me five dollars she would not**," and paid. **[r08] Dorsey confirms r03: Judd said "she will speak tonight"; Dorsey said "Judd, she will not"; "I said, five dollars it does not."** Both principals agree; Vesta has the sides reversed.

10. **The date of the wager.** r03 (Judd, 1965): **January 1937**. **[r08] (Dorsey, 1961): "The fourteenth of January, 1936. I say that without hesitation, I have told it a hundred times and it does not change."** Dorsey's own corroborating detail supports 1936: "He put the bill in his vest and never once brought it up again **in twenty-five years**" — 1936 + 25 = 1961, the year of the recording. Judd's account was given four years later.

11. **What Judd's book recorded.** r01: "he put it in the book — **the night, and the cold, and the hour of it**" (three items, and only on nights she spoke). **[r08] Dorsey the same: "Every night she spoke he wrote it down, and the temperature with it, and what time."** r03 and **r07** both give **five columns**, kept every night including "whether the sound came" — which is how he could count the qualifying nights that produced nothing. r04 says he "wrote down every occurrence of the sound," which understates the columns. **Three of the five accounts understate it in the same way; the two that handled the physical book agree on five columns.**

12. **The count of qualifying-but-silent nights.** The book's own back page **[r07]**: **five**, making **sixty-six**. Judd **[r03]**: **five**, making **sixty-six**. Lettie **[r06]**, reading the same back page in 1962: "**eight nights answered both the conditions and gave nothing at all… So sixty-nine nights in all that answered them, by his own count.**" r04 declines to give the number. Lettie read it once, for an hour, three years before she wrote.

13. **The total count of nights she spoke.** **Sixty-one** — r03, r04, r06, r07 (four sources, including the book's own back page and two independent readers of it). **Fifty-seven** — r08 (Dorsey). Dorsey also gives **thirty winters spoke / thirteen silent** against the book's **thirty-four / nine**. Both his pairs sum to 43, so the error is not arithmetic; he is remembering wrong numbers.

14. **The death count and what it meant.** r03: **190 deaths, 7 inside a week** — offered explicitly as a refutation of the omen ("arguing does not settle a count. **So I counted.**"). **[r08] Dorsey: "Better than a third of the burying in this town came inside the week after a night she spoke, he told me once, and he did not appear to think much of it either way."** Dorsey has the same count remembered as *confirming* the omen. Note Judd's own line: "I have never said them at the store" — which may explain how the store version got inverted. **[r07] Sable's transcription of the back page omits the death figures entirely**, though Judd said they were there.

15. **Where Judd lived.** r04 (1963): "**lives three streets from the depot**." r01 (1974): "up on the hill there."

16. **Ruth Frayne's visit as remembered.** r01: "going house to house with her notebook, very polite, very quick," "come to look at his book, and he let her have it." r03: she sat at his table and read the whole book through in silence, then asked to take it away. **[r06] Lettie: she came up the hill in 1962, wanted the whole record, took four boxes and brought every box back. [r07] The night book "had been lent by him personally to the author of Item 5 in 1962 and returned to him afterward"** — so "he let her have it" was a loan, and the book went back.

17. **Genealogical oddity — now settled.** By the stated facts — Adela was Emil's younger sister; Judd is Emil's son; Wendell Frayne is Adela's son — **Judd and Wendell are first cousins**, so Judd is Ruth's **first cousin once removed**, not "my father's uncle" (r04) and not her great-uncle / Ruth as Judd's "grand-niece" (r01). **[r07] states the correct relation outright: "Her father, Mr. Wendell Frayne… and Judd Rennick… were first cousins."** Both r01 and r04 use the wrong degree; the curator, working off slips and one conversation, gets it right.

18. **Judd on Adela's dates.** "She worked for the same railroad **before I was born**." Judd was born 1888; Adela was born 1874 (r04) or 1876 (r07) and worked on the viaduct party while it was building, **1896–97**, when Judd was eight or nine. His "before I was born" is impossible on either birth year. **[r07] shows she was on the payroll until 15 May 1901**, when Judd was thirteen — making it more impossible, not less.

19. **Adela's birth year.** r04: **born 1874, died 1959 at eighty-five** (internally consistent: 1874 + 85 = 1959). **[r07]: "born 1876 and died 1959"** (which would make her 83). Sable's own note admits his provenance comes "from the depositors' slips where slips exist and from inquiry where they do not."

20. **When the Wexler record begins.** **[r06] Lettie in 1965: "He began the weather record in 1884 and kept it until 1926, and I have kept it since, which makes eighty-one years of it, now"** — internally consistent (1884 + 81 = 1965). **r04: "Miss Wexler puts the beginning of it at the first of January, 1894, and I have used her date."** **[r07]: "The depositor's own index sheet gives the beginning of the record as 1 January 1894, and I have entered that date on the authority of the sheet, having no other."** So the *same woman* is on record for both dates: 1894 on her own index sheet (used by Frayne and by Sable) and 1884 in her own prose. **Nothing in Frayne's argument turns on the difference — her twelve-year window starts in 1897 either way.**

21. **The whole-series low.** r04: **thirteen below, January 1918**. **[r06] Lettie: "Eleven below zero, in January of 1918, and that is the lowest figure in the whole series from my father's first page to this morning's." [r07] the index sheet: "record low eleven below zero, January 1918."** Two to one against Frayne. **This strengthens rather than weakens her argument** — the as-built bridge (pre-1909) needed **fourteen below** to bind, and eleven below misses it by more than thirteen does.

22. **Who deposited the Tolliver books.** r04: "deposited there in 1938 by the company's bookkeeper." **[r05]: Pearl Tolliver Nace — who was both the company's bookkeeper and the founder's daughter — "put the stock book, the shop book, the timber ledgers, and eleven boxes of correspondence into the back of a truck and drove them herself."** Compatible, but r04's phrasing hides the family connection. **[r07] confirms 1938 and that the correspondence file shows they were catalogued on arrival.**

23. **The price and date of the engine sale.** CVR stock book (quoted in r02 and r07): **$2,150, sold 19 June 1901.** **[r05] Alma Nace: "We paid two thousand five hundred and ten dollars for her. That was in 1902."** Her own supporting details cut both ways: she calls it "a twelve-year-old American-type engine" (built 1889 → twelve years old in **1901**, thirteen in 1902), and says "**Twenty-seven years she worked for us**" (1902 → 1929 = 27; 1901 → 1929 = 28). The $360 gap is unexplained by either source. Alma is writing from her mother's ledger; the stock book is the seller's contemporary entry.

24. **Who kept the Wexler record.** **[r07] Sable's provenance: "The record was kept by two brothers named Wexler, the younger of whom carried it on after the elder gave it up."** **[r06] and [r04]: Peter Wexler and his daughter Lettie.** Sable also elsewhere in the same document writes "Lettie Wexler died in 1967" — so he contradicts himself within one set of notes. The "two hands, the second taking over partway through" observation is right; the sexes and relation are wrong.

25. **Dorsey Tice's relation to Warren Tice.** **r01 and r02 both say nephew** ("Old Warren Tice's nephew," "The Tices were railroad people out of Ninestone"). **[r08] Dorsey says father, repeatedly and at length: "Warren Tice, his name was… My father had no hand in that bridge."** This is the single largest new conflict in the set, and it is the *subject* himself against two outsiders. Note that if Dorsey is right, then the man whose store hosted the valley's arguments about the bridge was the son of the engineer who overruled Adela Rennick — which the valley either did not know or did not say.

26. **Warren Tice's whereabouts during construction.** **[r08] Dorsey: "He was in the Ninestone office the whole time it was building."** **r02, r04, and the field book: he was the resident engineer on the viaduct work, on the ground, receiving Adela's report about the roller setting on the day the span was closed ("Reported to W.T., who answers that the pedestal cannot be recast before the fall haul").** Dorsey's own later statement demolishes his own denial: "**He took me out on the deck when I was six and told me he had closed the span himself in ninety-seven.**"

27. **Warren Tice and Adela Rennick.** r02: "**Tice never forgave that entry, and she was gone inside four years**" — offered without documentary support ("I have nothing in writing on the point"). **[r07] now supplies writing, and it points the other way: "Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card."** Also, the letter says the party was "**reduced entire**" — everyone released, not her singled out. What survives of Pike's point is the second half: two of that party were re-engaged in August 1901 and she was not.

28. **Two mechanisms for the same trigger.** Pike and Frayne both point at the same summer's work on the bridge, but Pike blames **the forty tons of added deck weight** and Frayne blames **re-seating the nest at the same two-inch mark with the steel at 86°**. Only Frayne's account explains the sharp six-degree threshold; Pike offers no drawing and calls it an opinion. Note that the same event carries both changes, so Pike is not simply wrong about the timing.

29. **Who set the nest.** **[r08] "Anders Berg set that nest. Everybody knew it."** No other source names him, and no other source names anyone. Adela's entry records the setting passively ("Rollers set at the two-inch mark") and records only who was *told*. Dorsey drops the line without prompting, out of sequence, immediately after refusing to discuss his father — which may or may not be connected.

30. **The two boys on the deck.** **r02: Harlan Pike stood on the viaduct deck as a boy "with my father's hand in my collar."** **[r08] Dorsey Tice was taken out on the deck at six and "He held the back of my collar the whole time."** Two different men, two different fathers, the same gesture and nearly the same words, in two documents written seven years apart with no evidence of contact. Either a common local memory, a borrowed story, or a coincidence worth naming.

**Internal oddities within a single source:**

31. **Judd's wager arithmetic doesn't close — and Dorsey repeats the same impossible figures.** r03: "It had made forty-one at noon. It was thirty-eight at four o'clock and it had been falling four degrees an hour since one." Four degrees an hour from 1 p.m. to 4 p.m. is a twelve-degree fall; from 41 that gives about 29 at four o'clock, not 38. **[r08] Dorsey gives the identical triple** — 41 at noon, 38 at four, four degrees an hour since one — and adds that Judd "said them twice, once to me and once to a fellow who came in behind him… like a man reading a bill of lading." **That both men reproduce the same broken arithmetic suggests the triple is a fossilized told story, not either man's slip.** Judd's subsequent sum (38 − 6 = 32; at 4°/hr = 8 hours; therefore under six about midnight) is arithmetically right on its own terms, and the outcome both men report — low of three below, sound at ten past one — matches.

32. **Vesta's self-dating.** Born 1920, she calls herself "young enough to be sour about it too" when the railroad rebuilt the bridge — she would have been 33 or 34.

33. **Pike says the span "breathed on that nest… for fifty-odd years"** — 1897 to 1954 is about fifty-seven.

34. **Pike's memoir is written in 1958 "in his seventies,"** which places his birth in the middle 1880s; he was a small boy in the office at the time of the 1897–98 events he describes.

35. **"Two of that party were taken back on in the August following"** (r02) — the referent year of "the August following" is never stated by Pike; **[r07]'s slip pins it to August 1901**, and Pike's "gone inside four years" measured from the offending entry of Sept 1897 lands on May 1901 — both of Pike's vague claims check out precisely against documents he says he never had.

36. **Frayne's "Miss Wexler"** is Lettie Wexler, Peter's daughter, who has kept the record since 1926 — the same person named two ways in one paragraph.

37. **Frayne's number for the qualifying-but-silent nights.** She says "Further nights answered both conditions and produced nothing at all, and his own summing at the back of the book gives the count of them," but does not state the number; Judd and the book both give it as **five**, Lettie as **eight**. (Judd also says the *back* of the book holds the 190/7 death numbers, which Sable does not transcribe.)

38. **The whole-series low is short of the pre-1909 threshold.** **Eleven below (r06, r07)** — or thirteen below (r04) — is still short of the **fourteen below** the bridge as built would have required, so on Frayne's own figures the original 1897 error would never once have produced a sound in the entire weather record, 1884/1894 to 1965. She uses the point implicitly; it is worth having explicit, and the eleven-below figure makes it stronger.

39. **The death-omen story dies on the count.** 7 out of 190. Judd: "arguing does not settle a count. **So I counted.**" Dorsey, who never had the numbers, kept the omen alive at the store.

40. **[r05] Alma's "twenty-seven years" vs "twelve-year-old engine."** See #23 — her own two supporting details point at different purchase years.

41. **[r05] The engine's afterlife.** "The boiler shell went into the mill and served out the rest of its life as a stationary tank for the wash water. It was still there when the mill came down. **I have wondered since whether anybody who filled a bucket from it knew what they were filling it from.**" So while Cadder Falls believed No. 9 lay under the gravel of Sallow Creek, part of her was standing in a Bly County mill holding wash water.

42. **[r05] The twenty-four silent years.** "catalogued and shelved and did not draw one single inquiry for twenty-four years" — 1938 + 24 = 1962, the year of Frayne's survey. Alma does not appear to know that the inquiry that broke the silence was the bridge inquiry she dismisses at the end of her own piece.

43. **[r06] Lettie's sampling vs Frayne's claim.** Lettie checked "a dozen of his dates at random"; Frayne asserts agreement "on every one of the sixty-one nights." Lettie's is a spot check, honestly labeled as one; Frayne's is the stronger claim and rests on Frayne's own collation.

44. **[r07] Sable calls the accession "four collections and one object" and then catalogues six items.**

45. **[r07] The wrappers are mislabeled and Sable knows it.** Item 1 (the field book, signed "— A.R." inside, in a small regular upright hand) is attributed on its wrapper to **J. Rennick**; Item 2 (the night book, "a heavier, blunter pencil and a hand that slopes") is attributed to **A. Rennick**. His own query: "**The pencilled attributions on the wrappers of Items 1 and 2 sit oddly with the initials inside them; both wrappers were marked before the material reached me and I have carried them over as they stood, having had no time to compare hands. Resolve before the accession is published.**" The internal evidence resolves it cleanly: the field book is Adela's, the night book is Judd's. **A reader working only from the wrappers would invert the whole family story.**

46. **[r07] The pedestal did not end up where Frayne wanted it.** She asked for it "in the entrance hall, where people can put a hand on it" (r04). Sable: "on the floor of the north store room **where it will have to stay**," too heavy to bring to the desk, and nobody has yet measured it or assigned it an accession number.

47. **[r07] Sable never names Ruth Frayne** — she is "the author of Item 5," "a county highway engineer," "the granddaughter of the writer of the field book." The one person in the story who solved it is the one person the catalogue leaves anonymous.

48. **[r07] Citation warning about the night book.** "It was never the Society's before 1969, and **any earlier citation of it is a citation of the loan**." Frayne's 1963 talk cited it during the loan period.

49. **[r08] Dorsey contradicts himself about his father inside one afternoon.** "He never in his life spoke to me about that bridge" vs "he took me out on the deck when I was six and told me he had closed the span himself in ninety-seven." Also "My father had no hand in that bridge" vs "He was proud of it. He was not a proud man about much."

50. **[r08] Dorsey on Judd and the bet.** "He put the bill in his vest and never once brought it up again in twenty-five years, which is worse than if he had." Against r01, where the story is told and retold — but r01 has *Dorsey* telling it on himself, which r08 confirms he did ("I have made more than five dollars' worth out of telling about losing it"). So both are true: Judd never mentioned it; Dorsey never stopped.

51. **[r08] "Half the time we did."** Dorsey's own estimate of the hit rate on nights the old women predicted her — set against the book's 61 out of 66 qualifying nights (92%). Different populations (his is porch-thermometer forecasting, the book's is met conditions), but the folk memory is markedly less confident than the record.

52. **[r08] Dorsey is the earliest source (1961) and the least accurate on numbers**, while being an exact match on the sensory description and on the wager's direction, figures, and outcome. Worth remembering: a witness can be wrong about a total and right about a night.

**The town's reaction to the end — three sources, one feeling:**
- **[r01] Vesta:** "**The Falls said the railroad had spoiled it.**… **Somebody had come from off with money and taken it away from us.**"
- **[r02] Pike:** "**The valley took this hard, as though something had been stolen from it.**"
- **[r08] Dorsey:** "**This town was disappointed.** A thing that was ours for as long as anybody in this town could remember, that Ninestone did not have and the state road did not have, and some men come out with a crane and take it away in a month **and do not even know they have taken it.** There was talk that first winter. People sat up. Nothing. Second winter, nothing. After that they stopped sitting up, and now the young ones think it was a story their grandfathers made, and I cannot prove them wrong, and Judd's book is Judd's book and who is going to read it."

## 11. What r05–r08 changed, in brief

For a reader in a hurry, the four new documents move the following:

1. **The ghost story is dead four times over.** The engine's own buyer (r05) documents 27 further years of work, a renumbering, five shoppings, a scrapping, and a boiler shell used as a wash tank. The field book's page 71 (r07) names the two men hurt. The newspaper file agrees. The stock book photostat is held by the Society.
2. **Frayne's arithmetic is corroborated by two independent hands.** The forty-degree rule and the "Sheet 11 calls for three" entry are transcribed a second time by a curator with no stake (r07); the seven-nights-in-twelve-years count and the two mild winters are confirmed by the weather-keeper who did the counting herself (r06).
3. **The book's own numbers are now on record verbatim** (r07): 43 winters, 61 nights, 34/9, 5 silent, 66 qualifying. Lettie's eight/sixty-nine and Dorsey's fifty-seven/thirty/thirteen are both wrong against it.
4. **The rebuild is March 1954**, and the last heard night was 9 February 1954. Judd's "spring of 1953" is the outlier.
5. **Frayne's onset date (17 Jan 1912) now collides with the book's own "forty-three winters" and its last entry of 2 March 1956**, which back-count to a start in the winter of 1913–14 — Judd's date, not hers. This is a genuinely open problem.
6. **Warren Tice may have been Dorsey's father, not his uncle**, and Dorsey's denial that his father worked on the bridge is contradicted by Dorsey himself two pages later.
7. **Pike's Tice-bore-a-grudge theory loses its footing.** The dismissal letter says the party was reduced entire and that Tice asked for his recommendation to be entered on her card. What survives is the August 1901 re-engagement of two men and not her.
8. **The physical archive is mislabeled.** The wrappers on the two Rennick books have the attributions swapped, and the curator has flagged but not fixed it. A future reader working from wrappers alone would attribute Adela's field book to Judd and Judd's night book to Adela.
