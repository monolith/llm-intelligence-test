# Section A — Reconstruction

## A1. The building of the Sallow Creek Viaduct

The Cadder Valley Railroad was thirty-one miles long and nothing else it owned was more than a siding.
Ninestone, the county seat, with the shops and the office, was milepost 0; the Sallow Creek Viaduct was
milepost 22; Cadder Falls, the mill town, milepost 23; the Ashby quarry, the reason the whole thing
existed, milepost 31. The nine miles down from the quarry to the bridge fell at 2.2 percent, held the
whole way — a great deal of falling for a small railroad with second-hand brakes.

Work began in April 1896 and took about a year and a half. Three spans: 120 feet, a pin-connected truss
of 300 feet across the gorge proper, and 120 feet — 540 feet in all, ninety-seven feet above the water.
Emil Rennick, a carpenter of the Falls, built the falsework, the timber staging the iron stands on until
it will stand by itself. Warren Tice was resident engineer, and afterward the company's chief engineer at
Ninestone.

The center span was pinned solid at its north end and left free at the south, resting on a nest of rollers
seated in a cast pedestal. The whole of the span's movement with temperature was taken there.

The engineering party's *computer* — the person who does the figuring — was Adela Rennick, Emil's younger
sister, who came to the road in 1895 and ran a rod in the field as well, which was not usual. She kept a
duplicate of the party's book in her own hand, in black oilcloth, and entered in it what she thought was
worth having twice.

The center span was closed at 3:40 p.m. on 12 September 1897 with the thermometer at 66 degrees. Sheet 11
of the contract drawings called for three inches of travel on the south nest; the rollers were set to a
chisel mark at two. Adela reported it to Tice, who answered that the pedestal could not be recast before
the fall haul and that two inches was more than the valley would ever ask for. She wrote the exchange into
her own book "so that it is somewhere," and signed it A.R.

The line opened to Cadder Falls on 14 November 1897, and the town dates from that afternoon.

On 6 March 1898 a work train ran away from the quarry and No. 9 came off the iron at the north approach at
7:20 a.m. Nobody was killed; Lidell's arm was broken and Sherrod scalded at the deck; nine days to rerail
her.

The party was reduced entire on 15 May 1901, Adela with it. On 4 April 1911 the Ninestone roundhouse and
office burned, and the construction record with them — after which, as she said flatly and not as a boast,
there was nothing left about that bridge but her book.

## A2. The night book, and what was heard from the bridge

In August 1909 a bridge gang spent three weeks on the viaduct: 85-lb steel in place of 60-lb, new ties
throughout, and both shoes jacked and set again to the same two-inch mark. The thermometer on the north
pier read 86 degrees at four o'clock when they seated the south nest. Coyle, the foreman, said the new
deck was forty tons heavier than the old.

From that winter on, the bridge spoke. One deep report, low, of short duration — not a rattle, not a roll,
and never twice in one night. It came up the rails first: the sounder on the agent's table rattled in its
bracket, then the floor came up to meet you, then the noise. In the houses at the Falls people took it in
through their feet before their ears caught up. Never in the evening, never at daylight — always between
midnight and four. It was never heard at Ninestone, twenty-two miles off.

It never came on merely cold nights. It came on the hard sudden cold: a day decent at dinner time and
murderous by dark. The valley could go three weeks at zero and hear nothing.

Judd Rennick — Emil's son, Adela's nephew, born 1888 — went to the Cadder Falls depot as night operator in
1909 and was made agent in 1915. The first time he heard it he thought a car had got away in the quarry;
he went out on the platform with a lantern, found nothing wrong anywhere, and it was so cold the lantern
glass cracked. In the morning he ruled up a book. He kept it forty-three winters, in five columns — the
date, the day's high, the night's low, whether the sound came, and the hour — and no opinions, because a
man who writes down his opinion once will write down his opinion instead of the reading the next time.

Two conditions never failed: the night's low below six degrees ("not at seven, not at six and a half"), and
a fall of at least thirty degrees from that day's high. Sixty-one nights she spoke; thirty-four winters she
spoke at least once and nine she never spoke at all; five nights answered both conditions and gave nothing,
making sixty-six qualifying nights in all.

The valley believed it was Locomotive No. 9, drowned in the gorge in the bad winter when the bridge was
new, her crew never brought up, working the grade she could not stop on. The belief came down from the
people who had been at the Falls when the line was built — Vesta Cobb had it from her grandmother, who
boarded the construction men. Others held it rang for a death within the week. Judd did not argue; he
counted. One hundred and ninety deaths at the Falls in the years of the book; seven of them fell inside a
week of a night she spoke.

The last night entered as heard was 9 February 1954. In March the railroad put rockers under the span for
diesel power — four inches of travel each way — and told the agent nothing; he found out from a section
man. Three nights in the two winters following answered both conditions and gave nothing. On 2 March 1956
he wrote his last note and quit the book. The valley said the railroad had spoiled it.

## A3. The locomotive's second life

Locomotive No. 9 was a 4-4-0 built by the Rowan Locomotive Works in 1889, works number 1142, and cost
$8,600 new. On 6 March 1898 she ran away from the Ashby quarry with a work train and left the rails at the
north approach of the new viaduct at 7:20 in the morning. Nobody was killed, two of the gang were hurt, and
she was rerailed in nine days — in daylight, with half the county looking at her.

On 19 June 1901 the Cadder Valley Railroad sold her to the Tolliver Lumber Company of Bly County, two
hundred miles north, for $2,150. The chief clerk wrote the entry himself and ended it: *delivered on her
own wheels.* She steamed away north under her own power — eleven days and four railroads on the trip. The
delivering crew handed her over at the interchange, drank coffee in the Nace kitchen, and went home on the
cars like passengers.

Silas Tolliver, born 1849, had come into Bly County with a mill, a horse, and a conviction that the horse
was temporary. His tram out of Sixmile — named for the creek and not for its length — grew to nineteen
miles of thirty-pound rail up three drainages, with switchbacks where the ground would not take a curve.
For one season the new engine failed on the geared-engine grades: he had bought a racehorse to pull a
stump. Then somebody had the sense to keep her on the main stem where the ground was straight, and after
that she was not an engine, she was an institution.

Renumbered 4, she worked twenty-seven years. Boiler retube 1909; new tires 1913; cracked frame welded 1919
and again in 1922, the year Silas died in the chair by the office stove; shopped down to her bones in 1924
and put back together sounding, Pearl Tolliver Nace said, exactly like herself. Every child raised at
Sixmile could tell where she was on the hill with their eyes shut, and when the loads came down the main
stem in the evening you could feel her in the floor of the house before you heard her — the same
felt-before-heard that the other county was attributing to her ghost.

In September 1928 Tolliver took a second-hand eight-coupled engine and No. 4 was set out behind the shop
under a tarpaulin. The shop book, May 1929: *No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile this month. Scrap
to Ravel Brothers, $470.* Ravel took the tires and the brass out separately, being men who knew what they
were about. Her boiler shell went into the mill and served out the rest of its life as a stationary tank
for the wash water, and was still there when the mill came down.

So for thirty years, while Cadder Falls believed she lay under the gravel of Sallow Creek with her dead,
part of her stood in a Bly County mill holding wash water.

## A4. The 1962 survey and what it established

In September 1962 the state's flood-control cut took the water out of Sallow Creek and kept it out for
eleven weeks — the first time in living memory the gorge ran as a floor instead of a stream. Ruth Frayne,
born 1934, three years an engineer with the Pell County highway department and Adela Rennick's
granddaughter, went down to inspect the piers: a dry creek under a railroad bridge is an opportunity that
does not come twice in a career. She took a level and a camera, and the *Ninestone Sentinel* went along.
Her father, Wendell, took his mother's black oilcloth book down off the shelf where it had sat fifty-eight
years and put it into her hands himself.

She walked over eight hundred feet of bed, from well above the north pier to well below the south. There is
no locomotive in Sallow Creek: no frame, no boiler shell, no driving wheel, nothing of iron larger than a
spike, and no hole where any of it had been. She stated it flatly because it required no interpretation —
she went and looked and it is not there.

Forty feet below the south abutment, half in the gravel, lay the cast pedestal the 1954 gang had thrown
over: a casting the size of a kitchen stove, broken at one corner, two men to move. On its roller seat was
a chisel mark, at two inches. She measured it three times with a steel tape and got the same answer each
time. Sheet 11 called for three.

The rest she did with paper. From her grandmother's front matter: one inch in three hundred feet for forty
degrees. Closed at 66 degrees, three inches would have run out at 54 below and two inches at 14 below —
both temperatures this valley never reaches, which is exactly why nobody noticed anything for twelve years.
Re-seated to the same two inches at 86 degrees in August 1909, the span ran out at six above zero — where
the night book says the bridge spoke, sixty-one times, and never once above.

She borrowed the night book from Judd Rennick, who let her read it through at his table and take it away,
and the whole Wexler weather record from Lettie Wexler at Kettle Bench, four cardboard boxes, lent without
her being asked what it was for. The two books agreed on every one of the sixty-one nights. In the twelve
years between the opening and the re-decking there were seven nights below six degrees, four of them after
falls of thirty degrees or more, and nothing was heard on any of them: the silence of those seven nights is
not an absence of data, it is the proof. Bly County sent photostats of the sale and the scrapping by return
— the first inquiry there in twenty-four years.

She read her findings to the Pell County Historical Society in 1963. Her grandmother had measured that
bridge and was not believed; she measured it again sixty-five years later, with a better tape and worse
handwriting, and her grandmother was right.

# Section B — Relationships

**B1. Adela Rennick and Emil Rennick.** Brother and sister — Adela the younger. Reasoning: agreed by four
sources with no dissent (Judd calls Emil's "younger sister, Adela" his aunt, r03; Sable confirms the sibling
link, r07; Wendell Frayne, "My uncle Emil Rennick… My mother, Adela Rennick, was his younger sister," r10;
Athey, r12). Wendell's birth years — Emil 1866, Adela 1876 — are consistent with "younger."

**B2. Judd Rennick to Adela Rennick.** Nephew — Judd was Emil's son, and Emil was Adela's brother.
Established by Judd himself (he names Emil as his father and Adela as "my aunt," r03) and independently by
Wendell Frayne, who states it as a genealogical fact for the record: "Judd Rennick… was Emil's son, which
made him my mother's nephew and my first cousin" (r10). Two things point elsewhere and both fail. (i) Judd
says Adela "worked for the same railroad before I was born" — impossible: he was born 1888 and she was on
the payroll from 1895 until 15 May 1901 (r10; the dismissal letter, r07/r10), when he was thirteen. That is
a mis-memory about dates, not about kinship. (ii) The Society's wrappers have the attributions swapped —
Item 1, the field book, marked *J. Rennick*, and Item 2, the night book, marked *A. Rennick* — which the
curator flagged himself and did not fix. Internal evidence resolves it: the field book is signed "— A.R."
in a small, regular, upright hand and contains the 1897 survey entries; the night book is in a heavier,
blunter pencil in a sloping hand and contains forty-three winters of weather columns. A reader working from
the wrappers alone would invert the whole family.

**B3. Ruth Frayne to Judd Rennick.** First cousin once removed. Derivation: Emil and Adela were siblings
(B1); Judd is Emil's son; Wendell Frayne is Adela's son; therefore Judd and Wendell are first cousins;
Ruth is Wendell's daughter, so she stands one generation below Judd's cousin — first cousin once removed.
Stated outright by Sable ("Her father, Mr. Wendell Frayne… and Judd Rennick… were first cousins," r07) and
by Wendell himself, who explicitly corrects the family usage: "Ruth and Judd stood as first cousins once
removed. She used to call him uncle and he never corrected her" (r10). Ruth's own "my father's uncle" (r04)
and Vesta's "grand-niece" (r01) are both the wrong degree, and r10 explains exactly how the wrong form got
into circulation.

**B4. Dorsey Tice's father.** Warren Tice, the resident engineer on the viaduct. Resolution: Dorsey says so
himself, repeatedly and at length (r08), and Wendell Frayne says so independently, from the family papers
and with the store named — "His son Dorsey kept the store at Cadder Falls" (r10). Against them, Vesta
(r01), Pike (r02) and Athey (r12) all say *nephew*; but those three are not three sources — they are the
town's version, and Athey states that he compiled from "the town's memory." Dorsey's own denial that his
father had any hand in the bridge collapses two pages later ("He took me out on the deck when I was six and
told me he had closed the span himself in ninety-seven"), which confirms the paternity while destroying the
denial. **Why it matters to January 1936:** the storekeeper who bet five dollars that the bridge would not
speak was the son of the engineer who had overruled Adela Rennick's objection and left the nest a mark shy —
so the man betting against the bridge was, without saying so, betting against his own father's decision.
If Wendell's second-hand report of the 1931 retirement dinner is right ("we set that nest a mark shy and I
have listened to it ever since"), Dorsey was also the one man at the Falls with a private reason to know the
answer, and he was at that dinner, and he refused for the rest of his life to repeat what was said there.

**B5. Chain of custody into Ruth Frayne's hands in 1962.**
- **The field book.** Kept by Adela Rennick from 1901 until her death in 1959, on the shelf over her chair,
  never opened again after she stopped writing in it and never allowed to be moved. To her son Wendell
  Frayne in 1959. In September 1962 Wendell took it down and put it into Ruth's hands himself when she said
  she was going out to the creek. Deposited with the Pell County Historical Society by Wendell in 1963 (the
  depositor's slip is in his hand and unsigned).
- **The night book.** Kept by Judd Rennick at the depot for forty-three winters, last entry 2 March 1956. In
  1962 Ruth read it through at his kitchen table without speaking and asked to take it away; he lent it to
  her personally and it came back to him. It reached the Society only from his estate in 1969 — so, as the
  curator warns, any citation of it before that date, including Ruth's own 1963 talk, is a citation of a
  loan.
- **The weather record.** Begun by Peter Wexler at Kettle Bench, three miles up the valley, and kept by his
  daughter Lettie from 1926. In 1962 Ruth came up the hill and Lettie handed over the whole of it in four
  cardboard boxes without asking what it was for and without a receipt; every box came back. It reached the
  Society on Lettie's death in 1967. (Sable's provenance note calling the keepers "two brothers named
  Wexler" is wrong — r04, r06 and r10 all say father and daughter, and he contradicts himself elsewhere in
  the same notes.)
- **The Bly County ledgers.** Driven to the Bly County Historical Rooms in 1938 by Pearl Tolliver Nace — the
  company's bookkeeper and the founder's daughter — who loaded the stock book, shop book, timber ledgers
  and eleven boxes of correspondence into a truck herself, because she "did not care to have a thing end
  twice." They drew no inquiry for twenty-four years, until Ruth wrote in 1962; the Rooms sent photostats of
  the stock-book entry of 19 June 1901 and the shop-book entry of May 1929 by return.
- **The convergence.** For a stretch of 1962 Ruth had the night book and the weather record open on Lettie
  Wexler's kitchen table at the same time, her grandmother's field book from her father, and the Bly County
  photostats in the post. Four records, four families, four counties' worth of indifference to each other,
  and none of the four keepers had ever compared a page with any of the others.

# Section C — Math

## C1. The center span's cold travel

**Inputs, and why these and not the others.**
- Rule of movement: **one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees** — Adela Rennick's field book,
  quoted by Frayne (r04) and transcribed independently off the inside cover by the curator (r07). Pike (r02)
  and Quarles (r09) both say *fifty* degrees; both are quoting a general rule of thumb rather than the book,
  and Quarles's own memo admits his resulting figure will not reconcile with the record (¶10). Forty is also
  the only value that reproduces the observed threshold.
- Sheet 11 as drawn: **three inches** — Adela's page-62 entry written the day of the closing ("Sheet 11 calls
  for three"), transcribed twice. Pike and Quarles say four. See E1 §1 for the caveat.
- Span = 300 feet, so **1 inch per 40 degrees of cooling**.
- Closing temperature **66°F**, 12 September 1897, 3:40 p.m.
- 1909 re-seating temperature **86°F**, thermometer on the north pier at four o'clock.

**(a) As the drawing specified (3 inches).**
3 in × 40 °/in = 120 degrees of cooling available. 66 − 120 = **−54 °F (54 below zero).**
*(If Sheet 11 really called for four inches: 4 × 40 = 160; 66 − 160 = −94 °F. Either way, unreachable.)*

**(b) As actually set in 1897 (2 inches).**
2 in × 40 °/in = 80 degrees. 66 − 80 = **−14 °F (14 below zero).**

**(c) After the August 1909 re-seating (2 inches, taken from 86°).**
2 in × 40 °/in = 80 degrees. 86 − 80 = **+6 °F (six above zero).**

**(d) Margin the 1909 work cost.**
−14 °F → +6 °F = **20 degrees.** Equivalently, the mark never changed, so the whole loss is the difference
in the temperature the same two inches were measured from: 86 − 66 = **20 degrees**. (Measured against the
drawing's intent rather than the as-built state, the total loss is 54 + 6 = 60 degrees.)

Sanity check: the whole valley's record low is 11 below. (b) says the 1897 error alone could never have
produced a sound in the entire weather series. (c) says that after one summer afternoon it would produce
one several times a decade. That is exactly the history.

## C2. The afternoon of the wager

**(a) Hour of crossing.** Threshold 6 °F; reading 38 °F at 4:00 p.m.; rate 4 °/hr.
38 − 6 = 32 degrees to fall. 32 ÷ 4 = 8 hours. 4:00 p.m. + 8 hours = **about midnight.**
This is Judd's own sum and it is right on its own terms. The bridge actually spoke at 1:10 a.m., about an
hour after the crossing — consistent with a bearing that has to be carried past its freedom, not merely to
it.

*Flag on the inputs:* the afternoon triple both principals recite — 41 at noon, 38 at four, falling four
degrees an hour since one — cannot all be true. Four degrees an hour from 1:00 to 4:00 is a twelve-degree
fall, which from 41 gives 29 at four o'clock, not 38. Judd (r03) and Dorsey (r08) give the identical broken
triple twenty-nine years apart, so it is a fossilized told story rather than either man's slip. I answered
(a) with the two figures the question supplies, which are mutually consistent.

**(b) The day's actual fall.** Day's high 41 °F; that night's low **3 below, i.e. −3 °F**.
41 − (−3) = **44 degrees.** That is well past thirty, so **yes — the second condition was satisfied**, and
so was the first (−3 is below 6). Both conditions met; she spoke.

## C3. The night book's counts

**Authority.** The book's own back-page summary, transcribed verbatim by the curator (r07) and given in
substance by Judd himself (r03): *Forty-three winters. Sixty-one nights she spoke. Thirty-four winters she
spoke at least once and nine she never spoke at all. Five nights answered both the conditions and gave
nothing, which makes sixty-six nights that answered them in all.*

**(a)** 61 ÷ 43 = 1.41860… → **1.4 nights per logged winter.**

**(b)** 61 heard + 5 qualifying-but-silent = **66 nights met both conditions.**

**(c)** 61 ÷ 34 = 1.79411… → **1.8 nights per speaking winter.**

**Which winters the count runs over, and how I fixed its ends.**
The count runs over **43 winters, 1913–14 through 1955–56.**
- *Last winter fixed by document:* the last entry is dated 2 March 1956 and describes itself as "second
  winter since the rockers went in." The rockers went in March 1954 (agreed by r02, r04, r07, r08, r09, r11,
  r12; Judd's own "spring of 1953" is the lone outlier and is defeated by his own entry). So the last logged
  winter is 1955–56.
- *First winter fixed by arithmetic:* counting 43 winters back so that the last is 1955–56 puts the first at
  **1913–14**, which agrees with Judd's own statement that the first time she spoke was the winter of 1913.
- *The collision I could not remove:* Frayne (r04) dates the onset and the ruling-up of the book to 17
  January 1912, and Athey (r12) follows her. From 1911–12 to 1955–56 inclusive is **45** winters, not 43.
  Frayne's start date and the book's own "forty-three winters" cannot both stand. I fixed the count on the
  book, because the book is the thing being counted; the true first night of the sound is treated as
  unsettled in E1 §5.
- *Internal check that the ends are right:* the sound stopped after 9 February 1954, so the last two logged
  winters (1954–55, 1955–56) were silent. 9 silent winters − those 2 = 7 silent winters during the live
  period, and 34 + 7 = 41 = the winters from 1913–14 through 1953–54. The figures close.

**Nights excluded, and why.**
1. **The three post-rocker qualifying nights** of 1954–55 and 1955–56 (last entry: "Three nights under six
   degrees, three falls of better than thirty, and not a sound out of her either winter"). They are *not*
   inside the 66. The back page describes its five silent nights as sitting in cold spells "where she had
   already spoken," which cannot describe a night after March 1954; and by then the mechanism had been
   removed. Including them would give 69 qualifying nights over the 43 winters.
   *(Inference, offered as such: 5 + 3 = 8 and 61 + 8 = 69 are exactly Lettie Wexler's figures, which
   suggests she added the last note's three to the back page's five when she read the book in 1962. It does
   not fully work, because Quarles reports eight and the three separately; so I record it as a conjecture,
   not a resolution.)*
2. **Dorsey Tice's 57 nights / 30 winters / 13 winters** (r08), and Athey's reprinting of them (r12).
   Rejected against the book's own back page; see E1 §8.
3. **Lettie Wexler's eight silent / sixty-nine total** (r06) and Quarles's second-hand "eight" (r09).
   Rejected against the back page; see E1 §9.

## C4. The locomotive

Built by the Rowan Locomotive Works in **1889**, works no. 1142, 4-4-0, **$8,600 new**. Sold **19 June 1901**
for **$2,150** (the seller's own stock book, quoted in r02 and transcribed from the photostat in r07). Set
out under a tarpaulin **September 1928**; **cut up May 1929** (Tolliver shop book, quoted identically by the
buyer's granddaughter and by the curator).

**(a) Age when cut up.** 1889 → May 1929 = **40 years** (39 if she was built after May; the sources give the
year but not the month).

**(b) Percentage of original cost realized by the sale to her second owner.**
$2,150 ÷ $8,600 = 0.25 → **25.0%**, exactly a quarter.
*(On Alma Nace's $2,510 it would be 29.2%. I used the stock book: it is the seller's contemporary entry,
transcribed twice, and 2,510 against 2,150 is a digit transposition. The $360 gap may also be innocent — the
buyer's ledger could record total outlay including an eleven-day, four-railroad delivery — but the sale price
is the stock book's.)*

**(c) Years worked for her second owner.** June 1901 → set out of service September 1928 = **27 years**
(27 years 11 months to the scrapping in May 1929). Alma Nace independently says "twenty-seven years she
worked for us." Note this figure quietly supports the documentary 1901 sale date over her own "1902": 1901 +
27 lands on the September 1928 retirement she also reports, and her own description of the engine as
"a twelve-year-old American-type" fits 1889 + 12 = 1901.

## C5. Dimensions and movement

**(a) Total length of the viaduct.**
120 + 300 + 120 = **540 feet.** Both sources that give the component spans also give this total (r02, r09).
The *Sentinel* (r11) and Athey (r12) say six hundred feet; see E1 §18.

**(b) Fall of the line from the quarry to the viaduct.**
Distance: milepost 31 − milepost 22 = **9 miles**. (Quarles says eight miles in the same paragraph in which
he gives both mileposts — an internal error in r09.)
9 × 5,280 = 47,520 feet. 47,520 × 0.022 = **1,045.44 feet ≈ 1,045 feet of fall.**

**(c) Movement of the center span between the extremes of the weather record.**
Highest reading: **101 °F, July 1936** (agreed by every source that gives it).
Lowest reading: **11 below, January 1918** — the keeper of the record and her own index sheet (r06, r07)
against Frayne and the newspaper's thirteen below (see E1 §12).
Range = 101 − (−11) = **112 degrees.**
The span is 300 feet, so it moves 1 inch per 40 degrees.
112 ÷ 40 = **2.8 inches.**
*(On the thirteen-below figure: 114 ÷ 40 = 2.85 inches. The choice moves the answer by five hundredths of an
inch and nothing else.)*

# Section D — Logic

## D1. Necessary, sufficient, both, or neither?

**Necessary but not sufficient.**

*Necessary,* on the logged numbers: every one of the 61 nights met both conditions, and Judd searched his own
book for a counter-example specifically in order to find one — "Both, every time. There is not an exception
in the book, and I would know, because I went through it looking for one." He was strict about the
threshold: "Never once. Not at seven, not at six and a half. Below six." And the second condition was
equally firm: "A cold night on the end of a cold day was nothing to her."

*Not sufficient,* on the same numbers: 66 nights met both conditions and only 61 produced a sound. Five
qualifying nights gave nothing — a hit rate of 61/66, about 92 percent, not 100. That is a small failure
rate but it is a real one, and it is not noise: Judd found the pattern in it. Every one of the five silent
nights sits inside a cold spell in which she had already spoken and in which nothing had got above forty in
the daytime since. His conclusion was deliberately modest: "That is not a theory about what she was. It is a
rule about when."

The mechanism behind that rule (supplied independently by Quarles, r09) is that once the bearing has
released it stands at the far end of its travel with nothing left to release, and only warming — a daytime
thaw of some hours above about forty degrees — returns it toward the warm end of the seat. So the sufficient
condition set is the two logged conditions **plus a thaw since the last report**, and Quarles states the
logic plainly: "The conditions set down at the depot are necessary conditions. They are not sufficient ones."

One further datum settles the question decisively in the other direction: after March 1954, three nights met
both conditions and produced nothing at all — 0 for 3. Conditions that can be met three times running with
no effect were never causes. They were symptoms of the state of a bearing that had by then been replaced.

## D2. Why the sound cannot have been Locomotive No. 9

**Reason 1 — the seller's contemporary ledger (Harlan Pike, r02; independently transcribed by Sable, r07).**
The CVR stock book, entered by the chief clerk on 19 June 1901: *Locomotive No. 9, 4-4-0, Rowan Works 1889,
works no. 1142, sold this day to the Tolliver Lumber Company, Bly County, for two thousand one hundred and
fifty dollars ($2,150.00), delivered on her own wheels.* An engine at the bottom of a gorge is not delivered
on her own wheels three years later.

**Reason 2 — the buyer's family and the buyer's books (Alma Nace, r05).**
Twenty-seven further years of work in Bly County: a renumbering to 4, a failed first season on the geared
grades, a boiler retube in 1909, new tires in 1913, frame welds in 1919 and 1922, a full shopping in 1924,
retirement under a tarpaulin in September 1928, and the shop book of May 1929 recording her cut up at Sixmile
and sold to Ravel Brothers for $470. Her boiler shell ended as a wash-water tank in the mill.

**Reason 3 — the contemporary accident record (Adela Rennick's field book, page 71, via r07; corroborated by
the *Ninestone Sentinel*, 8 March 1898, via r02).** *No. 9 off the iron at the north approach, 7:20 a.m.,
running away from the quarry. Nobody killed. Lidell's arm broke, Sherrod scalded at the deck. Nine days to
rerail her.* She came off at the north **approach**, not into the gorge; nobody died; she was put back on
the rails in nine days.

**Reason 4 — the eyewitness on the ground (Emil Rennick, via Wendell Frayne, r10).** Emil was there that
morning and told his nephew flatly that nobody was killed, "and he was not a man who softened things."

**Reason 5 — the chronology of the sound itself (Judd Rennick, r03; Ruth Frayne, r04; Lettie Wexler, r06).**
Nothing was heard for the first twelve years after the line opened, including seven nights that met both of
the conditions the sound later obeyed without exception. A ghost that waits twelve years and then keeps a
temperature schedule is not a ghost.

**Why these are independent.** They come from six unconnected hands with unconnected reasons for writing: the
seller's chief clerk keeping a company's asset record; the buyer's bookkeeper's daughter writing a county
memoir two hundred miles away; the engineering party's computer keeping a private duplicate; a newspaper
compositor; a farm family recording a thermometer; and a carpenter talking at a kitchen table. Alma Nace's
county sat on its ledgers for twenty-four years without a single inquiry, and she says outright that she
never knew the rights of the bridge story and never asked — so her record cannot have been shaped by
anything at the Falls, and the Falls plainly knew nothing of hers. No two of the six could have coordinated.

**Which reason survives without the 1962 survey.** Reasons 1, 3 and 4 all predate it. Pike published the
stock-book entry in a memoir written in **1958**, four years before the creek was drawn down, and complained
that he had never been able to get a man at the Falls to sit still for the sentence. The field-book page and
the 1898 newspaper are contemporary with the wreck itself. The survey removed the last conceivable hiding
place; it was not what made the case.

## D3. Did the objection have anything to do with her leaving?

**Cannot be determined from the sources — but the balance of the documents runs against the story, not for
it.**

What the sources establish. Pike (r02) asserts the connection: an entry in Adela's book "got back to the
resident engineer," and "Tice never forgave that entry, and she was gone inside four years." He is explicit
that he has nothing in writing and is going on "the temperature of a room." His two vague supporting claims
turn out to check precisely against papers he never had: the objection was entered 12 September 1897 and she
left 15 May 1901 (three years eight months — "inside four years"), and "the August following" is August 1901.
So Pike is not inventing; the question is only whether his inference from those facts is sound.

The one document that bears on the dismissal points the other way. The letter of 30 April 1901, now in
evidence twice — as a loose enclosure in the field book (r07) and copied out in full by her son (r10) — reads:
*The engineering party is reduced entire on the 15th May next, and your time is paid to that day. Mr. Tice
asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card.* Two things follow. The party was reduced **entire**
— she was not singled out. And Tice asked that his recommendation go on her record, which is not what a man
does to someone he has not forgiven.

What survives of Pike's case is the printed slip of 20 July 1901: J. Corliss, rodman, and F. Wain, chainman,
re-engaged from 1 August for the Ashby extension, and not her. Wendell answers it directly and, I think,
fairly: the two taken back were a rodman and a chainman for field work, "she was the only computer the road
ever had and it never had another one." Against that, she had run a rod herself, so a field post was not
beyond her — and Wendell is her son, writing to put down a story that had been told "in this house," which
makes him committed rather than neutral.

The remaining item cuts the other way again and cannot be weighed. Tice's reported remark at his 1931
retirement dinner — "we set that nest a mark shy and I have listened to it ever since" — reaches us at two
removes (Emil to Adela to Wendell), and Dorsey Tice, who was present at that dinner, denies flatly that his
father said anything of the kind. If it is genuine, it is a man who came to think she had been right, not one
punishing her for it.

So: the sources cannot settle motive. They can settle mechanism — she left because the whole party was let go
— and they show that the single fact usually offered as proof of a grudge has an ordinary explanation and
sits beside a documented act of goodwill. My answer is that the objection probably had nothing to do with her
leaving, and that "probably" is as far as the evidence reaches.

# Section F — Theory

## F1. What produced the sound

**The theory in one sentence:** the center span was built with too little room to shrink into, that error was
made twenty degrees worse by a careful piece of maintenance in August 1909, and for forty-three winters the
span periodically ran out of travel, hung up, stored the strain of continued contraction, and let go all at
once with a single report — until the roller nest was replaced in March 1954 with bearings that could not run
out.

**The mechanism.** The 300-foot pin-connected truss is fixed at its north end; all its thermal movement is
taken at the south end, on a nest of rollers in a cast pedestal. Steel moves one inch per three hundred feet
per forty degrees, so this span moves one inch per forty degrees. As it cools it shortens and the shoe travels
toward the cold end of its seat. When the available travel is exhausted the shoe binds — but the contraction
does not stop. Strain accumulates in the truss members and at the pins and is stored there until the friction
is overcome, at which point the bearing releases suddenly and completely and the span takes up its new
position in one movement.

**The character of the sound follows from "suddenly and completely."** Energy stored over hours is discharged
at one stroke rather than dissipated by degrees: one deep report of short duration, not a roll, not a rattle,
not periodic, and — because the span cannot release what it has already released — never twice in a night. It
travels far better along continuous rail than through air, which is why the sounder on the agent's table
rattled in its bracket before the floor moved, why the floor moved before the noise arrived, why it was felt
in the soles of the feet a mile down the line at Cadder Falls, and why Ninestone, twenty-two miles off with
no continuous path, never heard it at all: "My father would have written it down."

**The threshold, and why six.** The span was closed at 3:40 p.m. on 12 September 1897 at 66 degrees, with the
rollers set to a chisel mark at two inches where Sheet 11 called for three. Two inches at forty degrees to the
inch is eighty degrees of cooling: as built, it would have bound at **fourteen below zero** — a temperature
this valley has never reached (record low eleven below, January 1918). In August 1909 a gang re-decked the
structure and jacked and re-seated both shoes to the same two-inch mark, with the north-pier thermometer
reading 86 degrees at four o'clock. The mark had not changed; the temperature it was measured from had.
86 − 80 = **six above zero.** That is the number, and it is not an approximation: the night book records
sixty-one reports, every one at a low below six degrees, and never once above — "not at seven, not at six and
a half."

**The second condition (a fall of thirty degrees or more within the day).** A slow decline lets the bearing
creep: the friction of the nest is overcome repeatedly, a small increment at a time, at low stored strain, so
nothing accumulates and nothing releases. A rapid fall carries the bearing past its freedom faster than it can
relieve itself, and the strain is stored instead of spent. That is why the valley could go three weeks at zero
and hear nothing, and why the killing night was the day that was decent at dinner time and murderous by dark.

**Why some qualifying nights were silent.** Once the shoe has released it stands at the far end of its travel
with nothing left to release, and only warming returns it toward the warm end of the seat — in practice a
daytime thaw of some hours above about forty degrees. So successive nights inside one cold spell are silent
however cold they get. Judd found this from the data alone, without the mechanism: every one of the five
silent qualifying nights sits in a cold spell where she had already spoken and where nothing had got above
forty in between. "That is not a theory about what she was. It is a rule about when."

**Why it began when it did and not earlier.** Not with the wreck of 1898 and not with the opening of 1897. As
built, the bridge needed fourteen below and never got it; the error of 1897 was real and, by itself,
permanently silent — a wrong bridge behaved exactly like a right one, because the difference between them lay
in weather that never came. The proof is negative and it is the strongest single item in the file: in the
twelve years between the opening and the re-decking there were seven nights at Kettle Bench below six degrees,
four of them at the bottom of falls of thirty degrees or more, and nothing was heard on any of them; the
coldest reading of those twelve years was nine below, on 4 February 1899, still five degrees short of what the
as-built bridge required. Lettie Wexler did that count herself, page by page over two evenings, and did not
know what it was for. The sound began after August 1909 because that is when six above zero became reachable.

**Why it stopped permanently.** In March 1954 the railroad rebuilt the bridge for diesel power and replaced the
roller nest with rocker bearings giving four inches of travel each way — travel that cannot be exhausted at any
temperature this valley records. The last night entered as heard was 9 February 1954, one month before. Three
nights in the two following winters answered both conditions and produced nothing. Nobody intended this: the
bearings were changed because the traffic was changing and the nest was old, the effect was incidental, the men
who produced it never remarked it, and the agent whose book it ended found out from a section man.

**The competing explanation the sources offer, and why it fails.** Harlan Pike says the weight did it: the 1909
deck was forty tons heavier, "and a nest of rollers under a bridge was never meant to carry what that new deck
put upon it." Quarles reaches the same place in 1971, offering it "as opinion and not as computation." Four
things defeat it.

1. **It contains no number.** Added load might make a bearing bind; it cannot say *at what temperature*. The
   observed threshold is sharp — below six, sixty-one times, never above — and only the travel arithmetic
   produces six.
2. **Quarles's opinion is the residue of a failed sum.** He computed the binding point on a fifty-degree
   coefficient and a four-inch Sheet 11, got fourteen below zero, wrote that this "is not readily reconciled
   with the local record, which places the occurrences at readings a good deal warmer," declined to pursue it,
   and reached for the weight to fill the gap. Recompute with the field book's forty degrees and the two-inch
   mark taken from 86 degrees and the gap closes exactly; there is nothing left for the weight to explain.
3. **The decisive point, and it is Quarles's own.** Paragraph 15: "The deck of 1909 was not disturbed in that
   work and stands over the span today; nothing was altered in March 1954 but the bearings themselves." The
   sound stopped in March 1954. The forty tons are still up there. If the weight had been the cause, the
   valley would still be hearing it.
4. **The weight cannot explain the second condition.** A dead load does not care how fast the temperature fell
   that afternoon. A bearing's ability to creep does.

Pike is not wrong about the *timing* — both changes rode on the same three weeks' work, which is exactly why
the coincidence has caught two engineers sixty years apart.

**The physical evidence recovered in 1962.** The discarded cast pedestal, thrown over the south abutment by the
1954 gang and found forty feet below it in the dry bed in September 1962 — a casting the size of a kitchen
stove, broken at one corner, two men to move — carrying on its roller seat **the chisel mark at two inches**,
where Sheet 11 called for three. Ruth Frayne measured it three times with a steel tape and got the same answer
each time. Quarles, arguing the other side of the case, put its standing best: the difference between the
drawing and the work "is accordingly established by the metal itself and does not rest on anyone's
recollection."

# Section G — Summary

## G1. (118 words)

In 1897 the Sallow Creek viaduct's roller nest was set to two inches where the drawing called for three. The
party's computer, Adela Rennick, objected, was overruled, and wrote it down. The error was harmless until
August 1909, when a gang re-seated the same two inches on an 86-degree afternoon — leaving the span to bind at
six above zero. For forty-three winters it let go with one deep report on hard, sudden cold nights, and the
valley blamed a locomotive it believed lay in the gorge, though she had been sold north in 1901 and worked
twenty-seven more years. Rockers ended it in 1954. Proving all this took four records — field book, night book,
weather record, lumber ledgers — kept by strangers who never compared a page.
