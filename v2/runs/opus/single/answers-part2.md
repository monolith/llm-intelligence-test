# Answer sheet — Part 2 (Sections C and D)

## Section C — Math

### Values used, and why

Two figures are contested and everything in C1 turns on them.

**The movement rule.** Adela Rennick's field book, front matter: "one inch in three hundred feet for **forty** degrees." That line is transcribed independently by Ruth Frayne (1963, reading the book) and by the curator Merrit Sable (1970, transcribing the physical inside cover "exactly"). Harlan Pike and H. L. Quarles both give **fifty** degrees. I take forty, on three grounds: (i) a contemporaneous document quoted verbatim by two separate readers outranks two later recollections of office practice; (ii) Pike could not have consulted the drawings, since the construction record burned in 1911 on his own testimony; (iii) decisively, arithmetic — forty degrees to the inch puts the post-1909 binding point at exactly +6°F, which is precisely the threshold in Judd Rennick's night book, a record kept for forty-three winters by a man who had never done this arithmetic. Fifty degrees gives −14°F, and Quarles himself concedes his answer "is not readily reconciled with the local record" and declines to pursue it. Two independent records converging to the degree settles it.

**Sheet 11.** The field book entry of 12 September 1897 says "Sheet 11 calls for **three**." Ruth Frayne's report says the same, and the curator quotes both passages. Pike and Quarles say **four**. I take three, for the same reason plus one more: four inches is exactly the travel of the 1954 rocker bearings, and both men wrote after 1954 with those records to hand, which is a natural route for contamination. (Residual doubt: Quarles states that the contract drawings are held in his office, so he could in principle have read Sheet 11 — but he never quotes it, whereas the field book is quoted verbatim twice.)

### C1. Cold travel of the center span

Rule: 300-foot span, 1 inch of movement per 40 °F. So **1 inch = 40 degrees of temperature change**. Span closed 12 September 1897 at **66 °F**.

**(a) As specified on Sheet 11 — three inches.**
3 in × 40 °F/in = 120 °F of cooling available.
66 − 120 = **−54 °F (54 degrees below zero).**

**(b) As actually set in 1897 — two inches.**
2 in × 40 = 80 °F of cooling available.
66 − 80 = **−14 °F (14 degrees below zero).**

**(c) After the August 1909 work.** The gang jacked both shoes and re-seated them to the same two-inch mark, with the steel at 86 °F. The reference temperature is now 86, not 66.
86 − 80 = **+6 °F (6 degrees above zero).**

**(d) Margin lost to the 1909 work.**
−14 °F → +6 °F = **20 degrees of margin lost**, which is exactly the 20-degree difference between the two seating temperatures (86 − 66).
For completeness: the total loss against the drawing is 60 degrees (−54 → +6), of which 40 degrees was the 1897 setting error and 20 degrees the 1909 reseating. Neither alone would have been heard; together they put the binding point inside the valley's ordinary winter.

### C2. The afternoon of the wager (14 January 1936)

**(a) Hour of crossing.** Threshold is *below* 6 °F. At four o'clock the thermometer read 38 °F and was falling 4 °F per hour.
38 − 6 = 32 degrees still to fall.
32 ÷ 4 = 8 hours.
4:00 p.m. + 8 hours = **about midnight.** (Judd Rennick did exactly this sum at the counter and said she would be under six about midnight. She spoke at 1:10 a.m., a little after the crossing, as the mechanism requires — the release comes at the binding point, not at the instant the threshold is passed.)

**(b) Actual fall, and the second condition.** The day's high was **41 °F** (at noon); the night's low was **−3 °F**.
41 − (−3) = **44 degrees.**
The second condition requires a fall of at least 30 degrees. 44 ≥ 30, so **yes, it was satisfied**, with 14 degrees to spare. Both conditions were met and the bridge spoke.

### C3. From the night book

**Which winters the count runs over.** Forty-three winters, **1911–12 through 1953–54 inclusive** (1911–12 is winter 1; winter 43 is 1953–54).

- *First winter fixed by*: the first night heard was 17 January 1912 — Kettle Bench that night, in Peter Wexler's hand, "High 34. Low −2 at daylight. Clear, still" — and Judd ruled up the book the following morning. Ruth Frayne and Corwin Athey both give this date; Athey's independent remark that Judd "had written things down for twenty-four years" before January 1936 confirms it arithmetically (1912 + 24 = 1936). Judd's own spoken "the winter of 1913," and his separate claim to have started the book in 1907, are memory slips sixty years after; his book beats his recollection.
- *Last winter fixed by*: the last night entered as heard is 9 February 1954, and the bearings were replaced the next month, March 1954. 1911–12 + 42 = 1953–54, which is 43 winters. Judd's spoken "spring of 1953" for the rockers is a slip, and his own final note disproves it: the note is dated 2 March 1956 and calls that the "second winter since the rockers went in," which works only from March 1954.

**Totals used**: 61 nights heard; 34 winters with at least one; 9 winters with none (34 + 9 = 43); 5 qualifying nights with no sound. These are the figures on the summary page at the back of the book, transcribed verbatim by the curator and repeated by Judd in interview. (Dorsey Tice and Athey give 57 / 30 / 13; Lettie Wexler and Quarles give 8 silent nights instead of 5. See E1.)

**(a) Average nights heard per logged winter.**
61 ÷ 43 = 1.4186… → **1.4 nights per winter.**

**(b) Total nights meeting both conditions, sound or no sound.**
61 heard + 5 silent = **66 nights.**

**(c) Average per winter counting only winters with at least one occurrence.**
61 ÷ 34 = 1.7941… → **1.8 nights per speaking winter.**

**Nights excluded, and why.** Three nights are excluded from all three figures: the nights in the two winters after the rebuild (1954–55 and 1955–56) that met both conditions and produced nothing, recorded in Judd's closing note of 2 March 1956 and confirmed second-hand by Quarles. They are excluded because they fall outside the book's own forty-three-winter count and, more importantly, because they belong to a physically different bridge — the roller nest was gone and rocker bearings with four inches of travel each way were in its place. Including them would answer a different question. Note the trap: 66 + 3 = 69, which is numerically the same as Lettie Wexler's 69 (61 + 8), but the two totals are not the same set of nights and the coincidence is meaningless.

Also excluded, necessarily, are any qualifying nights before 17 January 1912 — the seven nights below 6 °F in 1897–1909 recorded at Kettle Bench, four of them at the bottom of 30-degree falls. Nothing was heard on any of them and the book did not yet exist. They are evidence (see F1) but not entries.

### C4. The locomotive

**(a) Age when cut up.** Built by the Rowan Locomotive Works in **1889**; cut up at Sixmile in **May 1929**.
1929 − 1889 = **40 years old.**

**(b) Percentage of original cost realized by the sale to her second owner.**
Cost new to the Cadder Valley Railroad: **$8,600**. Sold 19 June 1901 to the Tolliver Lumber Company for **$2,150** (the seller's stock-book entry, quoted verbatim by Harlan Pike from his father's hand and held as a photostat by the Society).
2,150 ÷ 8,600 = 0.25 → **25.0 percent.**
Working note: Alma Nace and the *Sentinel* both give $2,510. I take $2,150 on the documentary quotation, and the exact quarter is a mild corroboration — $2,510 gives 29.2 percent. A digit transposition between 2,150 and 2,510 is the obvious mechanism. If $2,510 were right the answer would be **29.2 percent**; I flag this as the one figure in Section C carrying real residual doubt, because Alma is quoting her own family's books rather than gossip.

**(c) Years worked for her second owner.**
Bought June 1901; set out behind the shop when the eight-coupled engine arrived in September 1928; cut up May 1929.
June 1901 → September 1928 = **27 years of work** (Alma Nace independently states "twenty-seven years she worked for us"). She was in Tolliver's ownership 27 years 11 months, the last eight months of it standing dead under a tarpaulin.
This arithmetic also settles the purchase year: Alma says the sale was "in 1902," but 1902 → 1928 is 26 years, not her own 27, and she elsewhere calls the engine "twelve-year-old" at purchase, which from an 1889 build gives 1901. The documents say 19 June 1901 and her own numbers agree with them.

### C5. The line and the span

**(a) Total length of the viaduct.**
120 + 300 + 120 = **540 feet.**
Given as three spans by Pike and by Quarles, and stated by both as 540 feet overall. The *Sentinel* and Athey both say "six hundred feet"; that is a round-number approximation and loses to the component figures, which are given twice and sum exactly.

**(b) Fall of the line from the quarry to the viaduct.**
Quarry at milepost 31, viaduct at milepost 22 (mileposts given identically by Pike and by Judd Rennick) → **9 miles**, at a grade of 2.2 percent held the whole way.
9 × 5,280 = 47,520 feet.
47,520 × 0.022 = **1,045.44 feet ≈ 1,045 feet of fall.**
Quarles says the grade is "maintained over eight miles"; eight miles is the quarry-to-Cadder-Falls distance (31 − 23), not quarry-to-bridge, and Judd states "nine miles off the quarry" explicitly. If eight miles were used the answer would be 929.3 feet.

**(c) Movement of the center span across the recorded temperature range.**
Extremes of the Kettle Bench record: high **101 °F**, July 1936; low **−11 °F**, January 1918.
Range = 101 − (−11) = **112 degrees.**
At 1 inch per 40 degrees: 112 ÷ 40 = **2.8 inches.**
On the record low: Lettie Wexler, who kept the record, says 11 below and the depositor's index sheet in the Society's accession says 11 below; Ruth Frayne and the *Sentinel* say 13 below. I take −11 because the keeper of the book and her own deposited index sheet outrank a figure spoken in a talk and a newspaper that was reporting that talk. (With −13 the answer is 114 ÷ 40 = 2.85 inches — the difference does not change any conclusion.)
Remark worth the ink: 2.8 inches is the whole movement the valley's weather has ever demanded of that span. The two-inch nest could not deliver it; the three inches of Sheet 11 could have; the four-inch-each-way rockers of 1954 cover it with room to spare, which is why the sound stopped for good.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. Necessary, sufficient, both, or neither?

**Necessary but not sufficient.**

*Necessary*: in sixty-one logged occurrences over forty-three winters there is not one exception. Judd Rennick states that he went through the book specifically looking for one and did not find it — never at seven degrees, never at six and a half, always below six, and always at the bottom of a fall of at least thirty degrees from that day's high. This is not one man's word: Lettie Wexler took a dozen of his dates at random and turned to them in her own weather pages, kept three miles off by a woman who had never met him and never wrote down a sound in her life, and they agreed to the degree; Ruth Frayne checked all sixty-one against the same record. Two records with no occasion to be compared, agreeing on every night, is as strong as this kind of evidence gets.

*Not sufficient*: five nights met both conditions and gave nothing. That is the keeper's own count, on the summary page at the back of his book — 61 spoke, 5 did not, 66 qualifying nights in all. A condition that is met on 66 nights and produces the effect on 61 of them is not sufficient.

*What the missing term is*: Judd worked it out from the book and would only call it a guess — she would not speak twice inside one freeze; every one of the five silent nights sits in a cold spell where she had already spoken and where nothing had risen above forty degrees in between. Quarles derived the same thing from mechanics without having seen the book: once the shoe has released it stands at the cold end of its seat with nothing left to release, and only a daytime thaw of some hours above about forty degrees returns it toward the warm end. So the sufficient condition is the two logged conditions **plus** an intervening thaw above roughly 40 °F since the last release. With that third term added, the record has no exceptions at all — which is the strongest possible sign that the third term is real and not a patch.

One honest caveat: the two conditions were *derived from* the same book that is the evidence for them, so strictly they are necessary within the sample. What lifts them beyond that is the independent mechanical account, which predicts both conditions from the physics without reference to the record.

### D2. Three independent reasons it cannot have been Locomotive No. 9

**1. The engine was two hundred miles away, in writing, for the whole period.** The Cadder Valley stock book records her sold on 19 June 1901 to the Tolliver Lumber Company of Bly County and "delivered on her own wheels" — she left under her own steam. Tolliver's shop book records "No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile this month" in May 1929. The sound began in January 1912 and was last heard in February 1954: for the first seventeen of those winters the engine was hauling logs in another county, and for the last twenty-five she had been scrap metal.

**2. Nobody was ever killed and nothing ever went into the gorge.** The runaway of 6 March 1898 put No. 9 on her side at the *north approach*, in daylight, at 7:20 in the morning; two of the gang were hurt (Lidell's arm, Sherrod scalded) and she was rerailed in nine days. This is attested by the *Ninestone Sentinel* of 8 March 1898, by Adela Rennick's field book entry of the same week, and by Emil Rennick, who was on the ground that morning and told his nephew so directly. The ghost story requires a dead crew and a submerged engine; neither ever existed.

**3. The chronology is wrong by fourteen years, and the silence proves it.** The sound was first heard on 17 January 1912. In the twelve years between the opening of the line and the August 1909 re-decking there were seven nights at Kettle Bench below six degrees, four of them at the bottom of thirty-degree falls — including nine below on 4 February 1899 — and nothing was heard on any of them, which nobody at the Falls has ever disputed. If a wrecked engine in the creek were the cause, the valley would have heard it in 1899. Instead the sound waited for a summer afternoon in 1909.

**4. The sound stopped when the bearings were changed.** March 1954: rocker bearings in, roller nest out, and nothing since. No work was done to the creek bed, and a locomotive lying in the water is indifferent to what is bolted under a bridge deck ninety-seven feet above it.

**5. The character of the sound is wrong.** One report, deep, of short duration, never repeated within a night, arriving up the continuous rail so that the telegraph sounder rattled in its bracket before the air carried anything to the room. That is not an exhaust, not a bell, and not iron settling in gravel.

**Why these are independent.** Each rests on a different body of evidence, in different hands, gathered for different purposes: (1) on two commercial ledgers kept by a lumber company two hundred miles north and deposited in 1938 by people who had never heard the story argued; (2) on a newspaper of 1898 and a field book of 1898, plus a first-hand eyewitness; (3) on two record series — the night book and the Wexler weather record — kept independently by unrelated people, neither knowing what the other was for; (4) on the railroad's own 1954 rehabilitation record; (5) on the physical behaviour of the sound as described by everyone who heard it, including the storekeeper who did not believe in bridges. Falsifying any one of them would leave the others untouched.

**Reasons that stand without the 1962 survey**: (1), (2), (3), (4) and (5) all do. Only the "there is no iron in the creek bed" finding depends on the survey — and it is the *least* necessary of the arguments, because Harlan Pike had already demolished the story in 1958 from his father's ledger entry alone, and Athey's schoolbook was corrected by a letter from Bly County rather than by anything found in the gorge. The 1962 survey did not disprove the ghost; it explained it.

### D3. Did Adela Rennick's objection have anything to do with her leaving?

**Cannot be determined from the sources.** No document connects the two, and the two people who assert a connection and deny one are each arguing rather than reporting.

What the sources *do* establish is a sequence and nothing more. On 12 September 1897 she reported that the rollers had been set at two inches against Sheet 11's three, was overruled by the resident engineer, and entered the fact in her own book. On 30 April 1901 the railroad wrote to her: "The engineering party is reduced entire on the 15th May next, and your time is paid to that day. Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card." Folded inside that letter, and kept by her for the rest of her life, was a printed slip of 20 July 1901 re-engaging two men of the same party — J. Corliss, rodman, and F. Wain, chainman — from 1 August for the Ashby extension. She was not re-engaged.

The affirmative case is Harlan Pike's: an entry got back to Tice, "Tice never forgave that entry, and she was gone inside four years." Pike states plainly that he has nothing in writing and is reporting the temperature of a room he sat in as a boy. That is atmosphere, not evidence, and a four-year lag is a long fuse for retaliation.

The exculpatory case is Wendell Frayne's: the party was reduced entire, she was in it, the two taken back were a rodman and a chainman doing field work, and she was "the only computer the road ever had and it never had another one." But his own account undercuts him — he says himself that his mother "ran a rod for the party as well," and Ruth Frayne describes her as "computer and rodman." So the road re-engaged a rodman and did not re-engage a woman who could run a rod. Wendell also concedes he is answering a charge he has heard made inside his own house, which is the posture of a defender, not a witness.

Two further items point in opposite directions and cancel: Tice asked that his recommendation be entered on her card, which is not what a man does to someone he is punishing; and Emil Rennick's report of Tice's retirement-dinner remark — "we set that nest a mark shy and I have listened to it ever since" — shows a man who knew he had been wrong, but reaches us at two removes and is flatly denied by Dorsey Tice, who was in the room.

The one silent fact worth weighing: she kept that letter and that slip folded together for fifty-eight years, which suggests she thought the juxtaposition meant something. What she thought it meant, she did not write down, and the papers do not say.

So: the objection may have cost her the re-engagement, and the sources are consistent with that. They are equally consistent with a small railroad disbanding a survey party after its bridge was finished and never needing a computer again. Nothing in the record discriminates between the two, and I decline to choose.
