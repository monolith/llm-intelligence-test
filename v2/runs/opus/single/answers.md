# Answer sheet — Part 1 (Sections A and B)

## Section A — Reconstruction

### A1. The building of the Sallow Creek Viaduct

The Cadder Valley Railroad was thirty-one miles of branch line built to bring stone down from the Ashby quarry: Ninestone (county seat, shops, office) at milepost 0, the Sallow Creek Viaduct at milepost 22, the mill town of Cadder Falls at milepost 23, the quarry at milepost 31. From quarry to viaduct the line fell at 2.2 percent, held over the whole nine miles.

The viaduct was begun in April 1896. Three spans — 120 feet, a 300-foot pin-connected truss over the gorge, 120 feet — 540 feet in all, standing 97 feet above the water. The resident engineer was Warren Tice, afterward the company's chief engineer. Emil Rennick of the Falls built the falsework. Because a 300-foot steel span lengthens and shortens with the season, the center span was pinned solid at its north end and left free at the south, where it rode on a nest of rollers seated in a cast pedestal.

The party's computer — and rodman — was Emil's younger sister Adela Rennick, born 1874, who kept her own duplicate field book in black oilcloth alongside the company's. Its front matter carried the working rule: one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees. Sheet 11 of the contract drawings called for three inches of cold travel in the south nest.

The span was closed on 12 September 1897 at 3:40 p.m. with the thermometer at 66 degrees. The nest was set not at three inches but at a two-inch chisel mark cut in the roller seat. Adela reported it to Warren Tice, who answered that the pedestal could not be recast before the fall haul and that two inches was more than the valley would ever ask for. She was overruled and entered the fact in her own book "so that it is somewhere." The line opened to Cadder Falls on 14 November 1897.

The error was invisible for twelve years: two inches from 66 degrees meant the travel ran out at 14 below zero, which the valley never reached.

Adela's engineering party was reduced entire; her last day was 15 May 1901. On 4 April 1911 the Ninestone roundhouse and office burned with the road's entire construction record, leaving her duplicate book the only surviving account of how the bridge was built.

In August 1909 a bridge gang under a foreman named Coyle spent three weeks on the structure: 85-pound steel in place of 60, new ties throughout, both shoes jacked and re-set to the same two-inch mark — with the thermometer on the north pier reading 86 degrees at four o'clock when they seated the south nest. The finished deck was forty tons heavier. That careful, well-meant reseating moved the binding point from 14 below zero to 6 above.

(Who physically set the nest in 1897 is named as Anders Berg by Dorsey Tice and as Cass Nolan by Corwin Athey; the sources cannot settle it.)

### A2. The night book, and what was heard from the bridge

Judd Rennick, born 1888, son of the carpenter Emil, went to the Cadder Falls depot as night operator in 1909, was made station agent in 1915, and stayed until he retired in June 1954.

On the night of 17 January 1912 — high 34, low −2 at daylight, clear and still — he heard something in the office he took for a runaway car off the quarry. He went out with a lantern and found nothing wrong. In the morning he ruled up a book: five columns, the date, the day's high, the night's low, whether she spoke, and the hour. He kept it for forty-three winters, 1911–12 through 1953–54, and entered no opinions in it.

The sound was one deep report, not a rattle and not a roll, never repeated within a night, always between midnight and four. It was felt through the floor before it was heard. At the depot the telegraph sounder rattled in its bracket first, because the report travelled up the continuous rail faster than it travelled through air.

From the book Judd extracted two conditions, with no exception in forty-three winters: the night's low had to fall below 6 degrees above zero, and it had to stand at the bottom of a fall of at least 30 degrees from that day's high. His totals: 61 nights heard; 34 winters with at least one occurrence and 9 with none; 5 nights that met both conditions and gave nothing, making 66 qualifying nights in all. The fullest winter was 1917–18, with five nights. For the five silences he offered his only guess: she would not speak twice inside one freeze, and needed a real daytime thaw above 40 degrees to reset. The last night heard was 9 February 1954.

The valley believed the sound was a locomotive. On the morning of 6 March 1898, at 7:20, engine No. 9 came down off the quarry grade with a work train and no brakes worth the name and left the rails at the north approach of the new viaduct. Nobody was killed; two of the gang were hurt (Lidell's arm broken, Sherrod scalded); nine days to rerail her. The town nonetheless told itself that a crew had gone into the gorge with her and was never brought up, and that the winter sound was that engine working the grade — and, since the belief needed the wreck as its origin, that the sound had begun that same winter. It also held that the bridge rang for a death within the week. Judd did not argue; he counted. Over the years of his book there were 190 deaths at Cadder Falls, seven of them inside a week after a night she spoke. He wrote both numbers at the back and never said them at the store.

The famous wager was 14 January 1936, in Dorsey Tice's store: 41 at noon, 38 at four o'clock, falling four degrees an hour since one. Judd did the sum at the counter, said she would speak before morning, and Dorsey bet him five dollars she would not. The low was 3 below; she spoke at ten minutes past one; Dorsey paid the next morning and told the story on himself for twenty-five years.

After the bridge was rebuilt in March 1954 the sound never came again, and the valley took it as a theft.

### A3. The locomotive's second life

No. 9 was a 4-4-0 from the Rowan Locomotive Works, built 1889, works number 1142, bought new by the Cadder Valley Railroad for $8,600 — the best machine the road owned. She was derailed, not destroyed, on 6 March 1898, and was back on the iron in nine days.

On 19 June 1901 the company sold her to the Tolliver Lumber Company of Bly County, two hundred miles north, for $2,150 — a quarter of what she had cost new — "delivered on her own wheels." She steamed north under her own power: eleven days and four railroads, with a Pell County crew who handed her over at the interchange, drank coffee in the Nace kitchen and went home on the cars as passengers.

Tolliver had been founded by Silas Tolliver (1849–1922), who wanted a road engine that would bring a made-up train down the main stem of the Sixmile tram faster than a man could walk. It was his daughter Pearl Tolliver Nace, born 1881, the company's bookkeeper from the age of eighteen, who found the advertisement and put it on his desk. The shop painted out the 9 and painted on a 4, the company numbering its engines in order of purchase, and she was the Four from that season on.

She was a disappointment for exactly one season, on grades she was never built for. Kept afterward to the straight main stem, she worked twenty-seven years: boiler retube 1909, new tires 1913, a cracked frame welded in 1919 and again in 1922, a full shopping in 1924. Children raised at Sixmile could place her on the hill with their eyes shut.

In September 1928 a second-hand eight-coupled engine arrived and the Four was set out behind the shop under a tarpaulin. In May 1929 she was cut up at Sixmile, aged forty. Scrap went to Ravel Brothers for $470; the boiler shell served out its life at the mill as a stationary tank for wash water.

The cut ran out in 1936 and the company wound up. In 1938 Pearl Nace drove the stock book, the shop book, the timber ledgers and eleven boxes of correspondence to the Bly County Historical Rooms herself — she "did not care to have a thing end twice" — where they were catalogued and drew no inquiry for twenty-four years, until a letter arrived from a county engineer in Pell County in 1962.

So the engine the valley believed was lying under Sallow Creek was hauling logs two hundred miles north through every one of the forty-three winters the sound was heard, and had been scrap for twenty-five years before anyone went down to look.

### A4. The 1962 survey and what it established

In September 1962 the state's flood-control cut took Sallow Creek out of its bed for eleven weeks and the gorge lay dry to the gravel. Ruth Frayne, born 1934, a civil engineer three years with the Pell County highway department — and Adela Rennick's granddaughter — went down to inspect the piers. The *Ninestone Sentinel* went along. The inspection stopped being routine on the second afternoon.

She reported four findings.

**Nothing.** She walked something over eight hundred feet of bed, above and below the viaduct, and found no locomotive: no frame, no boiler shell, no driving wheel, nothing of iron larger than a spike, and no hole where any of it had been. Photographs were taken.

**The pedestal.** Forty feet below the south abutment, where the 1954 gang had thrown it over, lay a cast-iron block the size of a kitchen stove — the old south shoe of the center span. Cut into the roller seat was the setting mark. She measured it three times with a steel tape: two inches. The rocker bearings installed in 1954 gave four inches of travel each way.

**Her grandmother's field book**, handed to her by her father Wendell Frayne off the shelf where it had sat fifty-eight years: the rule of one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees, and the entry of 12 September 1897 recording that Sheet 11 called for three inches and the rollers were set at two.

**The night book**, lent to her personally by Judd Rennick: sixty-one nights, both conditions never once violated, and in the front matter the note about the August 1909 gang seating the south nest with the steel at 86 degrees.

Then the arithmetic. Three inches at forty degrees to the inch is 120 degrees of cooling; 66 less 120 is 54 below zero — a temperature the valley has never approached. Two inches is 80 degrees; 66 less 80 is 14 below — also never reached, which is why a wrong bridge behaved like a right one for twelve years. But the 1909 gang re-set the same two-inch mark with the steel at 86: 86 less 80 is 6 above zero. Six above is exactly where the night book says the bridge spoke, sixty-one times, and never once above.

Her corroboration was the Wexler family's volunteer weather record at Kettle Bench, kept by people related to nobody in the story and lent to her in four cardboard boxes. It agreed with the night book on all sixty-one nights. It also showed seven nights below 6 degrees in the twelve years between the opening of the line and the re-decking, four of them at the bottom of thirty-degree falls, with nothing heard on any — the coldest reading of those years being 9 below on 4 February 1899, well short of the 14 below the as-built bridge would have required. The silence was the proof.

Photostats from the Bly County Historical Rooms traced No. 9 out of the valley alive in 1901 and to scrap at Sixmile in 1929.

Her report closed: "There is no locomotive in this creek and there never was one. What the valley heard for forty-three winters was a bridge letting go of itself, one inch late." She read the paper to the Historical Society in 1963; the pedestal, the field book and the report went to the Society.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Adela Rennick and Emil Rennick were sister and brother — Emil (b. 1866) the elder, Adela (b. 1874) his younger sister.** Reasoning: three independent statements agree — Judd Rennick ("My father had a younger sister, Adela"), Wendell Frayne ("My uncle Emil Rennick… My mother, Adela Rennick, was his younger sister"), and the curator's note off the depositors' slips ("Judd Rennick's father, Emil, and Adela… were brother and sister"). The birth years are consistent with "younger sister."

**B2. Judd Rennick was Adela Rennick's nephew (she was his paternal aunt).** Established by Judd himself in 1965 ("My father had a younger sister, Adela… I asked her twice when I was a boy"), by Wendell Frayne's deliberate genealogy of 1971, and by the curator's accession note; the dates fit (Emil b. 1866, Judd b. 1888). Two sources point elsewhere: Ruth Frayne calls Judd "my father's uncle" (which would make him Adela's brother or brother-in-law), and Vesta Cobb calls Ruth his "grand-niece" (same generation slip in mirror image). Both are the household shorthand — Ruth called him "uncle" and, as Wendell says, "he never corrected her." Wendell explicitly wrote his notes to fix this, so the correction comes from the one person who set out to get it right, and it agrees with Judd's own testimony.

**B3. Ruth Frayne and Judd Rennick were first cousins once removed.** Derivation: Emil and Adela were siblings; Emil's son Judd (b. 1888) and Adela's son Wendell (b. 1904) are therefore first cousins — stated by Wendell and confirmed by the curator; Ruth (b. 1934) is Wendell's daughter, so she stands one generation below Judd's first cousin, i.e. first cousin once removed. Wendell states the conclusion outright and flags Ruth's "uncle" as the error to be corrected.

**B4. Dorsey Tice's father was Warren Tice, resident engineer on the viaduct and afterward chief engineer of the Cadder Valley Railroad (retired spring 1931, died 1937).** Dorsey says so in the first person and supplies a son's memories — being taken out on the deck at six and told his father had closed the span himself in ninety-seven, and attending the retirement dinner — and Wendell Frayne independently writes "His son Dorsey kept the store." Vesta Cobb and Athey both call Dorsey the nephew; that is the town's loose shorthand and loses to first-person testimony plus an outside corroborator.

Why it matters in January 1936: the man who bet five dollars that the bridge would *not* speak was the son of the engineer who had made it speak. The bridge spoke because the south nest sat at two inches instead of Sheet 11's three — the setting Warren Tice personally approved on 12 September 1897 when he overruled Adela Rennick ("the pedestal cannot be recast before the fall haul… two inches is more than the valley will ever ask for"). Dorsey's skepticism was not neutral: he opens his interview insisting his father "had no hand in that bridge" and refuses even to repeat the story of what his father is said to have admitted at the retirement dinner. He was defending a name, and he lost the bet to the flaw in his father's work.

**B5. Chain of custody into Ruth Frayne's hands in 1962.**

- **The field book** (Adela's black-oilcloth duplicate, 1895–1901): kept by Adela herself from 1901 until she died in 1959, on the shelf over her chair, never opened but never allowed to be moved; passed to her son Wendell Frayne; Wendell took it down and put it into his daughter Ruth's hands himself in 1962 when she said she was going out to the creek. Deposited with the Pell County Historical Society in 1963 (Item 1).
- **The night book** (Judd Rennick's station ledger, 43 winters, last entry 2 March 1956): lent by Judd personally to Ruth in 1962 — she read it through at his kitchen table and asked to take it away, and he let her — then returned to him. It reached the Society only in 1969, from his estate (Item 2). The curator notes that any citation of it before 1969 is a citation of the loan.
- **The weather record** (Kettle Bench, begun by Peter Wexler and kept by his daughter Lettie from 1926): Ruth went up the hill in 1962 and asked for the whole of it; Lettie handed over four cardboard boxes without asking what it was for and without a receipt, and Ruth brought every box back. It came to the Society in 1967, the year Lettie died (Item 3).
- **The Bly County ledgers** (Tolliver Lumber Company stock book and shop book): kept by Pearl Tolliver Nace, deposited by her at the Bly County Historical Rooms in 1938 and catalogued on arrival. Ruth wrote to the Rooms in 1962 and they sent photostats by return — the 19 June 1901 sale entry and the May 1929 scrapping entry. The originals stayed in Bly County; only the two photostats came south with her report (Item 6).

The connecting fact is Ruth herself: granddaughter of the woman who wrote the first book, daughter of the man who kept it, first cousin once removed of the man who wrote the second, a stranger to the third, and a correspondent to the fourth. Alma Nace's independent remark that the Bly County deposit "did not draw one single inquiry for twenty-four years" (1938 + 24 = 1962) confirms that Ruth's letter was the first.
# Answer sheet — Part 2 (Sections C and D)

## Section C — Math

### Values used, and why

Two figures are contested and everything in C1 turns on them.

**The movement rule.** Adela Rennick's field book, front matter: "one inch in three hundred feet for **forty** degrees." That line is transcribed independently by Ruth Frayne (1963, reading the book) and by the curator Merrit Sable (1970, transcribing the physical inside cover "exactly"). Harlan Pike and H. L. Quarles both give **fifty** degrees. I take forty, on three grounds: (i) a contemporaneous document quoted verbatim by two separate readers outranks two later recollections of office practice; (ii) Pike could not have consulted the drawings, since the construction record burned in 1911 on his own testimony; (iii) decisively, arithmetic — forty degrees to the inch puts the post-1909 binding point at exactly +6°F, which is precisely the threshold in Judd Rennick's night book, a record kept for forty-three winters by a man who had never done this arithmetic. Fifty degrees gives −14°F, and Quarles himself concedes his answer "is not readily reconciled with the local record" and declines to pursue it. Two independent records converging to the degree settles it.

**Sheet 11.** The field book entry of 12 September 1897 says "Sheet 11 calls for **three**." Ruth Frayne's report says the same, and the curator quotes both passages. Pike and Quarles say **four**. I take three, for the same reason plus one more: four inches is exactly the travel of the 1954 rocker bearings, and both men wrote after 1954 with those records to hand, which is a natural route for contamination. (Residual doubt: Quarles states that the contract drawings are held in his office, so he could in principle have read Sheet 11 — but he never quotes it, whereas the field book is quoted verbatim twice.)

### C1. Cold travel of the center span

Rule: 300-foot span, 1 inch of movement per 40 °F. So **1 inch = 40 degrees of temperature change**. Span closed 12 September 1897 at **66 °F**.

**(a) As specified on Sheet 11 — three inches.**
3 in × 40 °F/in = 120 °F of cooling available.
66 − 120 = **−54 °F (54 degrees below zero).**

**(b) As actually set in 1897 — two inches.**
2 in × 40 = 80 °F of cooling available.
66 − 80 = **−14 °F (14 degrees below zero).**

**(c) After the August 1909 work.** The gang jacked both shoes and re-seated them to the same two-inch mark, with the steel at 86 °F. The reference temperature is now 86, not 66.
86 − 80 = **+6 °F (6 degrees above zero).**

**(d) Margin lost to the 1909 work.**
−14 °F → +6 °F = **20 degrees of margin lost**, which is exactly the 20-degree difference between the two seating temperatures (86 − 66).
For completeness: the total loss against the drawing is 60 degrees (−54 → +6), of which 40 degrees was the 1897 setting error and 20 degrees the 1909 reseating. Neither alone would have been heard; together they put the binding point inside the valley's ordinary winter.

### C2. The afternoon of the wager (14 January 1936)

**(a) Hour of crossing.** Threshold is *below* 6 °F. At four o'clock the thermometer read 38 °F and was falling 4 °F per hour.
38 − 6 = 32 degrees still to fall.
32 ÷ 4 = 8 hours.
4:00 p.m. + 8 hours = **about midnight.** (Judd Rennick did exactly this sum at the counter and said she would be under six about midnight. She spoke at 1:10 a.m., a little after the crossing, as the mechanism requires — the release comes at the binding point, not at the instant the threshold is passed.)

**(b) Actual fall, and the second condition.** The day's high was **41 °F** (at noon); the night's low was **−3 °F**.
41 − (−3) = **44 degrees.**
The second condition requires a fall of at least 30 degrees. 44 ≥ 30, so **yes, it was satisfied**, with 14 degrees to spare. Both conditions were met and the bridge spoke.

### C3. From the night book

**Which winters the count runs over.** Forty-three winters, **1911–12 through 1953–54 inclusive** (1911–12 is winter 1; winter 43 is 1953–54).

- *First winter fixed by*: the first night heard was 17 January 1912 — Kettle Bench that night, in Peter Wexler's hand, "High 34. Low −2 at daylight. Clear, still" — and Judd ruled up the book the following morning. Ruth Frayne and Corwin Athey both give this date; Athey's independent remark that Judd "had written things down for twenty-four years" before January 1936 confirms it arithmetically (1912 + 24 = 1936). Judd's own spoken "the winter of 1913," and his separate claim to have started the book in 1907, are memory slips sixty years after; his book beats his recollection.
- *Last winter fixed by*: the last night entered as heard is 9 February 1954, and the bearings were replaced the next month, March 1954. 1911–12 + 42 = 1953–54, which is 43 winters. Judd's spoken "spring of 1953" for the rockers is a slip, and his own final note disproves it: the note is dated 2 March 1956 and calls that the "second winter since the rockers went in," which works only from March 1954.

**Totals used**: 61 nights heard; 34 winters with at least one; 9 winters with none (34 + 9 = 43); 5 qualifying nights with no sound. These are the figures on the summary page at the back of the book, transcribed verbatim by the curator and repeated by Judd in interview. (Dorsey Tice and Athey give 57 / 30 / 13; Lettie Wexler and Quarles give 8 silent nights instead of 5. See E1.)

**(a) Average nights heard per logged winter.**
61 ÷ 43 = 1.4186… → **1.4 nights per winter.**

**(b) Total nights meeting both conditions, sound or no sound.**
61 heard + 5 silent = **66 nights.**

**(c) Average per winter counting only winters with at least one occurrence.**
61 ÷ 34 = 1.7941… → **1.8 nights per speaking winter.**

**Nights excluded, and why.** Three nights are excluded from all three figures: the nights in the two winters after the rebuild (1954–55 and 1955–56) that met both conditions and produced nothing, recorded in Judd's closing note of 2 March 1956 and confirmed second-hand by Quarles. They are excluded because they fall outside the book's own forty-three-winter count and, more importantly, because they belong to a physically different bridge — the roller nest was gone and rocker bearings with four inches of travel each way were in its place. Including them would answer a different question. Note the trap: 66 + 3 = 69, which is numerically the same as Lettie Wexler's 69 (61 + 8), but the two totals are not the same set of nights and the coincidence is meaningless.

Also excluded, necessarily, are any qualifying nights before 17 January 1912 — the seven nights below 6 °F in 1897–1909 recorded at Kettle Bench, four of them at the bottom of 30-degree falls. Nothing was heard on any of them and the book did not yet exist. They are evidence (see F1) but not entries.

### C4. The locomotive

**(a) Age when cut up.** Built by the Rowan Locomotive Works in **1889**; cut up at Sixmile in **May 1929**.
1929 − 1889 = **40 years old.**

**(b) Percentage of original cost realized by the sale to her second owner.**
Cost new to the Cadder Valley Railroad: **$8,600**. Sold 19 June 1901 to the Tolliver Lumber Company for **$2,150** (the seller's stock-book entry, quoted verbatim by Harlan Pike from his father's hand and held as a photostat by the Society).
2,150 ÷ 8,600 = 0.25 → **25.0 percent.**
Working note: Alma Nace and the *Sentinel* both give $2,510. I take $2,150 on the documentary quotation, and the exact quarter is a mild corroboration — $2,510 gives 29.2 percent. A digit transposition between 2,150 and 2,510 is the obvious mechanism. If $2,510 were right the answer would be **29.2 percent**; I flag this as the one figure in Section C carrying real residual doubt, because Alma is quoting her own family's books rather than gossip.

**(c) Years worked for her second owner.**
Bought June 1901; set out behind the shop when the eight-coupled engine arrived in September 1928; cut up May 1929.
June 1901 → September 1928 = **27 years of work** (Alma Nace independently states "twenty-seven years she worked for us"). She was in Tolliver's ownership 27 years 11 months, the last eight months of it standing dead under a tarpaulin.
This arithmetic also settles the purchase year: Alma says the sale was "in 1902," but 1902 → 1928 is 26 years, not her own 27, and she elsewhere calls the engine "twelve-year-old" at purchase, which from an 1889 build gives 1901. The documents say 19 June 1901 and her own numbers agree with them.

### C5. The line and the span

**(a) Total length of the viaduct.**
120 + 300 + 120 = **540 feet.**
Given as three spans by Pike and by Quarles, and stated by both as 540 feet overall. The *Sentinel* and Athey both say "six hundred feet"; that is a round-number approximation and loses to the component figures, which are given twice and sum exactly.

**(b) Fall of the line from the quarry to the viaduct.**
Quarry at milepost 31, viaduct at milepost 22 (mileposts given identically by Pike and by Judd Rennick) → **9 miles**, at a grade of 2.2 percent held the whole way.
9 × 5,280 = 47,520 feet.
47,520 × 0.022 = **1,045.44 feet ≈ 1,045 feet of fall.**
Quarles says the grade is "maintained over eight miles"; eight miles is the quarry-to-Cadder-Falls distance (31 − 23), not quarry-to-bridge, and Judd states "nine miles off the quarry" explicitly. If eight miles were used the answer would be 929.3 feet.

**(c) Movement of the center span across the recorded temperature range.**
Extremes of the Kettle Bench record: high **101 °F**, July 1936; low **−11 °F**, January 1918.
Range = 101 − (−11) = **112 degrees.**
At 1 inch per 40 degrees: 112 ÷ 40 = **2.8 inches.**
On the record low: Lettie Wexler, who kept the record, says 11 below and the depositor's index sheet in the Society's accession says 11 below; Ruth Frayne and the *Sentinel* say 13 below. I take −11 because the keeper of the book and her own deposited index sheet outrank a figure spoken in a talk and a newspaper that was reporting that talk. (With −13 the answer is 114 ÷ 40 = 2.85 inches — the difference does not change any conclusion.)
Remark worth the ink: 2.8 inches is the whole movement the valley's weather has ever demanded of that span. The two-inch nest could not deliver it; the three inches of Sheet 11 could have; the four-inch-each-way rockers of 1954 cover it with room to spare, which is why the sound stopped for good.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. Necessary, sufficient, both, or neither?

**Necessary but not sufficient.**

*Necessary*: in sixty-one logged occurrences over forty-three winters there is not one exception. Judd Rennick states that he went through the book specifically looking for one and did not find it — never at seven degrees, never at six and a half, always below six, and always at the bottom of a fall of at least thirty degrees from that day's high. This is not one man's word: Lettie Wexler took a dozen of his dates at random and turned to them in her own weather pages, kept three miles off by a woman who had never met him and never wrote down a sound in her life, and they agreed to the degree; Ruth Frayne checked all sixty-one against the same record. Two records with no occasion to be compared, agreeing on every night, is as strong as this kind of evidence gets.

*Not sufficient*: five nights met both conditions and gave nothing. That is the keeper's own count, on the summary page at the back of his book — 61 spoke, 5 did not, 66 qualifying nights in all. A condition that is met on 66 nights and produces the effect on 61 of them is not sufficient.

*What the missing term is*: Judd worked it out from the book and would only call it a guess — she would not speak twice inside one freeze; every one of the five silent nights sits in a cold spell where she had already spoken and where nothing had risen above forty degrees in between. Quarles derived the same thing from mechanics without having seen the book: once the shoe has released it stands at the cold end of its seat with nothing left to release, and only a daytime thaw of some hours above about forty degrees returns it toward the warm end. So the sufficient condition is the two logged conditions **plus** an intervening thaw above roughly 40 °F since the last release. With that third term added, the record has no exceptions at all — which is the strongest possible sign that the third term is real and not a patch.

One honest caveat: the two conditions were *derived from* the same book that is the evidence for them, so strictly they are necessary within the sample. What lifts them beyond that is the independent mechanical account, which predicts both conditions from the physics without reference to the record.

### D2. Three independent reasons it cannot have been Locomotive No. 9

**1. The engine was two hundred miles away, in writing, for the whole period.** The Cadder Valley stock book records her sold on 19 June 1901 to the Tolliver Lumber Company of Bly County and "delivered on her own wheels" — she left under her own steam. Tolliver's shop book records "No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile this month" in May 1929. The sound began in January 1912 and was last heard in February 1954: for the first seventeen of those winters the engine was hauling logs in another county, and for the last twenty-five she had been scrap metal.

**2. Nobody was ever killed and nothing ever went into the gorge.** The runaway of 6 March 1898 put No. 9 on her side at the *north approach*, in daylight, at 7:20 in the morning; two of the gang were hurt (Lidell's arm, Sherrod scalded) and she was rerailed in nine days. This is attested by the *Ninestone Sentinel* of 8 March 1898, by Adela Rennick's field book entry of the same week, and by Emil Rennick, who was on the ground that morning and told his nephew so directly. The ghost story requires a dead crew and a submerged engine; neither ever existed.

**3. The chronology is wrong by fourteen years, and the silence proves it.** The sound was first heard on 17 January 1912. In the twelve years between the opening of the line and the August 1909 re-decking there were seven nights at Kettle Bench below six degrees, four of them at the bottom of thirty-degree falls — including nine below on 4 February 1899 — and nothing was heard on any of them, which nobody at the Falls has ever disputed. If a wrecked engine in the creek were the cause, the valley would have heard it in 1899. Instead the sound waited for a summer afternoon in 1909.

**4. The sound stopped when the bearings were changed.** March 1954: rocker bearings in, roller nest out, and nothing since. No work was done to the creek bed, and a locomotive lying in the water is indifferent to what is bolted under a bridge deck ninety-seven feet above it.

**5. The character of the sound is wrong.** One report, deep, of short duration, never repeated within a night, arriving up the continuous rail so that the telegraph sounder rattled in its bracket before the air carried anything to the room. That is not an exhaust, not a bell, and not iron settling in gravel.

**Why these are independent.** Each rests on a different body of evidence, in different hands, gathered for different purposes: (1) on two commercial ledgers kept by a lumber company two hundred miles north and deposited in 1938 by people who had never heard the story argued; (2) on a newspaper of 1898 and a field book of 1898, plus a first-hand eyewitness; (3) on two record series — the night book and the Wexler weather record — kept independently by unrelated people, neither knowing what the other was for; (4) on the railroad's own 1954 rehabilitation record; (5) on the physical behaviour of the sound as described by everyone who heard it, including the storekeeper who did not believe in bridges. Falsifying any one of them would leave the others untouched.

**Reasons that stand without the 1962 survey**: (1), (2), (3), (4) and (5) all do. Only the "there is no iron in the creek bed" finding depends on the survey — and it is the *least* necessary of the arguments, because Harlan Pike had already demolished the story in 1958 from his father's ledger entry alone, and Athey's schoolbook was corrected by a letter from Bly County rather than by anything found in the gorge. The 1962 survey did not disprove the ghost; it explained it.

### D3. Did Adela Rennick's objection have anything to do with her leaving?

**Cannot be determined from the sources.** No document connects the two, and the two people who assert a connection and deny one are each arguing rather than reporting.

What the sources *do* establish is a sequence and nothing more. On 12 September 1897 she reported that the rollers had been set at two inches against Sheet 11's three, was overruled by the resident engineer, and entered the fact in her own book. On 30 April 1901 the railroad wrote to her: "The engineering party is reduced entire on the 15th May next, and your time is paid to that day. Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card." Folded inside that letter, and kept by her for the rest of her life, was a printed slip of 20 July 1901 re-engaging two men of the same party — J. Corliss, rodman, and F. Wain, chainman — from 1 August for the Ashby extension. She was not re-engaged.

The affirmative case is Harlan Pike's: an entry got back to Tice, "Tice never forgave that entry, and she was gone inside four years." Pike states plainly that he has nothing in writing and is reporting the temperature of a room he sat in as a boy. That is atmosphere, not evidence, and a four-year lag is a long fuse for retaliation.

The exculpatory case is Wendell Frayne's: the party was reduced entire, she was in it, the two taken back were a rodman and a chainman doing field work, and she was "the only computer the road ever had and it never had another one." But his own account undercuts him — he says himself that his mother "ran a rod for the party as well," and Ruth Frayne describes her as "computer and rodman." So the road re-engaged a rodman and did not re-engage a woman who could run a rod. Wendell also concedes he is answering a charge he has heard made inside his own house, which is the posture of a defender, not a witness.

Two further items point in opposite directions and cancel: Tice asked that his recommendation be entered on her card, which is not what a man does to someone he is punishing; and Emil Rennick's report of Tice's retirement-dinner remark — "we set that nest a mark shy and I have listened to it ever since" — shows a man who knew he had been wrong, but reaches us at two removes and is flatly denied by Dorsey Tice, who was in the room.

The one silent fact worth weighing: she kept that letter and that slip folded together for fifty-eight years, which suggests she thought the juxtaposition meant something. What she thought it meant, she did not write down, and the papers do not say.

So: the objection may have cost her the re-engagement, and the sources are consistent with that. They are equally consistent with a small railroad disbanding a survey party after its bridge was finished and never needing a computer again. Nothing in the record discriminates between the two, and I decline to choose.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What produced the sound

**The theory.** The center span of the Sallow Creek Viaduct was a 300-foot pin-connected truss, fixed at its north end and free at its south, where it rode on a nest of rollers seated in a cast pedestal. All of its thermal movement had to be taken at that one bearing. The governing rule, from the field book of the party's own computer, is one inch of movement per 300 feet per 40 degrees of temperature change.

When the span was closed on 12 September 1897 at 66 °F, Sheet 11 of the contract drawings called for three inches of cold travel in the south nest. The nest was set instead at a two-inch chisel mark cut in the roller seat, the resident engineer having ruled that the pedestal could not be recast before the fall haul and that two inches was more than the valley would ever ask for. Two inches from 66 °F exhausts at 14 below zero — a temperature this valley has never reached — so for twelve years a wrong bridge behaved exactly like a right one.

In August 1909 a bridge gang re-decked the structure and, in the ordinary course of careful work, jacked both shoes and re-seated them to the same two-inch mark. They did it at four o'clock on a summer afternoon with the thermometer on the north pier reading 86 °F. The same two inches of travel now started from a reference temperature twenty degrees higher: 86 − 80 = **+6 °F**. From that afternoon the bridge had six degrees above zero of cold in hand and no more.

**The character of the sound.** As the span cools it shortens and the south shoe travels toward the cold end of its seat. When the travel runs out the shoe binds, but contraction does not stop; strain accumulates in the truss members and at the pins and is stored there. The bearing then releases suddenly and completely, and the span takes up its new position in a single movement. Because the release is sudden and total, the stored energy is discharged at one stroke instead of being spent by degrees — hence one deep report of short duration, never a rattle, never a roll, never repeated within a night. Because steel carries it better than air, it ran twenty-two feet of rail and a mile of track more efficiently than it crossed the gorge: at the depot the telegraph sounder rattled in its bracket first, then the floor, then the noise — an order that never changed in forty-three winters. In the houses, people felt it through the floorboards before they heard it. It was, in Ruth Frayne's phrase, "a bridge letting go of itself, one inch late."

**The threshold, and why that particular number.** Below 6 °F, and never once above — not at seven, not at six and a half. Six above zero is not a coincidence and not a local peculiarity: it is 86 minus (2 inches × 40 degrees). The number is the arithmetic of the 1909 reseating, and the night book's empirical threshold reproduces it to the degree, having been arrived at by a station agent who was counting, not calculating.

**The second condition.** A fall of at least 30 degrees from that day's high. A slow decline lets the shoe creep — the friction of the nest is overcome repeatedly, in small increments, at low stored strain, so nothing accumulates and there is nothing to release. The valley knew this empirically: three weeks at zero produced nothing, and a soft day followed by a clear hard night produced everything. A drop of thirty degrees or more within the day carries the bearing past its freedom faster than it can relieve itself, so the strain is stored instead of spent.

**Why some qualifying nights were silent.** Five of the sixty-six qualifying nights gave nothing. Once the shoe has released, it stands at the far end of its travel with nothing left to release, and it is returned toward the warm end only by warming — in practice a daytime thaw of some hours above about 40 °F. Every one of the five silent nights sits inside a cold spell in which the bridge had already spoken and in which nothing had risen above forty in between. The two logged conditions are therefore necessary and not sufficient; the missing term is an intervening thaw. Judd Rennick worked this out from his own columns and would only call it a guess; a county engineer derived the identical rule from mechanics without ever seeing the book.

**Why it began when it did.** Not with the 1898 runaway, and not with the opening of the line. The as-built error of 1897 alone put the binding point at 14 below zero, and in the twelve years between the opening and the re-decking there were seven nights at Kettle Bench below 6 °F, four of them at the bottom of thirty-degree falls, with nothing heard on any — the coldest being 9 below on 4 February 1899, well short of what the bridge as built would have required. The two winters after the re-decking never went below +9 °F. The first night under 6 °F after August 1909 was **17 January 1912** — high 34, low −2, clear and still — and that is the night the sound began and the night the night book was started. The silence of those seven earlier nights is not missing data; it is the proof.

**Why it stopped permanently.** In March 1954 the road was rehabilitated for diesel power. The roller nest was taken out and rocker bearings giving four inches of travel each way were substituted; the old cast pedestal was pitched over the abutment into the creek. Four inches each way cannot be exhausted at any temperature this valley has recorded — the whole observed range, 101 °F to 11 below, demands only 2.8 inches of movement in total. The last night heard was 9 February 1954, one month before the work. Three nights in the two following winters met both conditions and produced nothing. The cure was entirely incidental: the bearings were changed because the traffic was changing and the nest was old, and the men who ended a fifty-year local legend did not know they had done it.

### The competing explanation, and why it fails

The sources themselves offer one rival: **the forty tons.** The 1909 deck was forty tons heavier than the one it replaced, and a roller nest takes that increase directly on the rollers. Harlan Pike states it flatly — "Forty tons is forty tons… I say the weight did it" — and H. L. Quarles records the same opinion, holding it "at least as probable that the added dead load, rather than any question of travel, was what brought the bearing to bind."

It fails on four counts, and the last is fatal.

1. **It cannot produce the threshold.** A bearing overloaded into binding has no reason to bind at 6 °F rather than at 20 or at 20 below. The record has a hard, never-violated edge at six above, and that number falls straight out of the reseating arithmetic (86 − 2 × 40). The weight theory has nothing to say about why six.
2. **It cannot produce the second condition.** Dead load is constant; it does not care how fast the temperature falls. The thirty-degree rule is a rate effect and only a friction-and-creep mechanism explains it.
3. **It cannot produce the silent qualifying nights.** Weight does not reset itself with a thaw.
4. **It is refuted by the 1954 cessation — in Quarles's own memorandum.** He records in paragraph 15 that "the deck of 1909 was not disturbed in that work and stands over the span today; nothing was altered in March 1954 but the bearings themselves." If the forty tons had been the cause, the forty tons were still there in 1955, and the bridge would have gone on speaking. It never spoke again. The rival explanation is defeated by the file it was written on.

A fifth point disposes of Pike's version specifically: he dates the re-decking to 1908 and says the noise "began somewhere about that time and not before it." It began in January 1912, two and a half years after the actual work of August 1909 — a gap the weight theory must call a coincidence and the thermal theory explains exactly (the first qualifying night did not arrive until then).

### The physical evidence recovered in 1962

**The cast-iron south pedestal**, recovered from the creek bed forty feet below the south abutment where the 1954 gang threw it over — a casting the size of a kitchen stove, broken at one corner, with **the chisel setting mark on the roller seat at two inches**, measured three times with a steel tape and independently recorded on the accession card. Sheet 11 called for three. That mark is the only surviving physical record of the setting of 1897, the construction papers having burned in 1911, and it converts the whole account from inference into measurement.

The survey's second physical finding is negative and equally load-bearing: **eight hundred feet of dry creek bed containing no locomotive** — no frame, no boiler, no wheels, nothing of iron larger than a spike, and no hole where any of it had been.

## Section G — Summary

### G1. (120 words)

A branch-line viaduct at Sallow Creek was closed in September 1897 with its south roller nest set at two inches where the drawing called for three; the resident engineer overruled the party's computer, Adela Rennick, who wrote it down. Re-set in August 1909 at 86 degrees, the span ran out of travel at six above zero, and from January 1912 it let go each freeze with one deep report. The valley blamed a locomotive it believed lay in the gorge. Four records settle it: Adela's field book, the station agent's 43 winters of columns, a farm family's weather ledger, and a lumber company's books showing the engine scrapped 200 miles north in 1929. New bearings in 1954 silenced it.
