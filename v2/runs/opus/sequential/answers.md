# Answer Sheet — Cadder Falls / Sallow Creek

Answered from the twelve retellings, read once each in order, without re-reading.

**Standing resolutions used throughout** (justified in Section E):

- The movement rule for the 300-ft center span is **one inch per 40°F** (Adela Rennick's field book), not the office's 50°F rule.
- **Sheet 11 called for three inches**; the nest was set at **two**.
- The re-decking was **August 1909**, not 1908 or 1911.
- The sound began **17 January 1912**, not with the 1898 wreck.
- The night book's own totals govern: **61 nights, 43 winters, 34 winters with sound, 9 without, 5 silent qualifying nights, 66 qualifying nights.**
- The bearings were changed in **March 1954**, not spring 1953.
- Record low at Kettle Bench: **11 below, January 1918**. Record high: **101°, July 1936**.
- No. 9 was sold **19 June 1901 for $2,150.00**.

## Section A — Reconstruction

### A1. The building of the Sallow Creek Viaduct

The Cadder Valley Railroad was thirty-one miles of branch line built to bring stone down from the Ashby quarry, at milepost 31, to Ninestone — county seat, shops and office — at milepost 0. Cadder Falls, the mill town, stood at milepost 23; the Sallow Creek Viaduct at milepost 22, one mile down the line from the Falls. Everything else the company owned was sidings. The grade from the quarry to the viaduct ran nine miles at a constant 2.2 percent, which for a small road with second-hand brakes was a great deal of falling.

Work on the viaduct began in April 1896. It was three spans — 120 feet, a pin-connected truss of 300 feet across the gorge proper, and 120 feet again: 540 feet overall, 97 feet above the water. The resident engineer was Warren Tice, later the company's chief engineer at Ninestone. Emil Rennick of Cadder Falls, a carpenter, built the falsework — the timber staging the iron was assembled on and then knocked down. The engineering party's *computer* — the person who did the arithmetic, and who also ran a rod in the field — was Emil's younger sister Adela Rennick, born 1874, who came to the road in 1895. She kept her own duplicate book, in black oilcloth, separate from the party's, and entered in it what she thought as well as what she measured.

The governing problem was that steel moves. The center span was pinned solid at its north end; the whole of its movement was taken at the south end, which rode on a nest of rollers seated in a cast pedestal. The working rule in the front of Adela's book was *one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees* — for a span of exactly 300 feet, one inch per 40°F.

The span was closed on 12 September 1897 at 3:40 p.m. with the thermometer at 66°. The rollers were set at the two-inch chisel mark. Sheet 11 of the contract drawings called for three. Adela reported it to Tice, who answered that the pedestal could not be recast before the fall haul and that two inches was more than the valley would ever ask for. She entered the exchange in her own book "so that it is somewhere." The line opened to Cadder Falls on 14 November 1897.

Two later events belong to the structure's history. In August 1909 a bridge gang spent three weeks on it: 85-pound steel in place of 60, new ties throughout, both shoes jacked and re-set to the same mark, with the north-pier thermometer reading 86° at four o'clock when the south nest was seated. The finished deck was forty tons heavier. And on 4 April 1911 the roundhouse and office at Ninestone burned, taking the railroad's entire construction record with them — after which, as Adela put it flatly, there was nothing left about that bridge but her book.

### A2. The night book and what was heard from the bridge

On the night of 17 January 1912 — high 34, low 2 below at daylight, clear and still — the valley heard, for the first time, what it came to call the boom. It was one report: deep, short, not a rattle and not a roll, and never repeated within a night. It arrived in a fixed order. At the depot the telegraph sounder rattled in its bracket first, then the floor moved, then the noise reached the room; in the houses at Cadder Falls people felt it come up through the floorboards into their feet before they heard anything. It came only in the small hours, between midnight and four, and only on what the Falls called killing nights — a soft, almost shirtsleeve day, then a hard clear sky at dark and the bottom out of the night.

Judd Rennick, born 1888, Emil's son and Adela's nephew, had gone to the depot as night operator in 1909 and was made station agent in 1915, staying until he retired in June 1954. He was alone in the office that first night, thought a car had got away in the quarry, went out with a lantern and found nothing wrong, and in the morning ruled up a book. Five columns: the date, the day's high, the night's low, whether she spoke, the hour. He kept it for forty-three winters and deliberately entered no opinion, on the ground that a man who writes down his opinion once will write it down instead of the reading the next time.

Out of the columns he later drew two conditions that never failed: the night's low had to go below six degrees above zero — never at seven, never at six and a half — and the day before had to have been at least thirty degrees warmer than the night that followed. His totals, written on a page at the back: sixty-one nights she spoke; thirty-four winters she spoke at least once and nine she never spoke at all; five nights answered both conditions and gave nothing, making sixty-six that answered them in all. For the five he offered his one guess — she would not speak twice inside a freeze until a real daytime thaw above forty had intervened.

The valley's own explanation was a ghost. On 6 March 1898 a work train had come down off the quarry grade without brakes enough to hold her and No. 9 had left the iron at the north approach at 7:20 a.m. The town's story made that a drowned crew lying under the gravel, the engine working the grade she could not stop on. It was not true — nobody was killed and the engine never entered the gorge — but it fitted the sound, the place and the season, and it was two generations old before anyone tested it. A second belief held that the bridge rang for a death. Judd did not argue; he counted. In the years of the book there were a hundred and ninety deaths at Cadder Falls and seven fell within the week after a night she spoke. He put the two numbers in the back of the book and never said them at the store.

The last night entered as heard was 9 February 1954. In March 1954 the road was rehabilitated for diesel power and the roller nest replaced with rocker bearings. Three nights in the two following winters met both conditions and produced nothing, and on 2 March 1956 he wrote his last entry: forty-three winters kept, and he did not know now what it was he had been listening to.

### A3. The locomotive's second life

Cadder Valley No. 9 was a 4-4-0 American type from the Rowan Locomotive Works, built 1889, works number 1142. She cost the company $8,600 new — for that road a sum spoken of in a lowered voice — and she was the best machine it owned. On 6 March 1898 she came off the iron at the north approach of the new viaduct while running away from the quarry with a work train. Nobody was killed; Lidell's arm was broken and Sherrod was scalded at the deck; it took nine days to rerail her. She then went back to work.

Two hundred miles north, in Bly County, Silas Tolliver had built a lumber business around the Sixmile tram — named for the creek, not its length, and grown by the end to nineteen miles of thirty-pound rail up three drainages. What he had were geared engines that could climb anything and could not be hurried; what he wanted in 1901 was a road engine with big wheels that would bring a made-up train down the main stem faster than a man could walk. His daughter Pearl Tolliver Nace, the company's bookkeeper from the age of eighteen, found the advertisement — a Pell County branch line selling off a twelve-year-old American — and put it on his desk without a word.

The stock book entry is dated 19 June 1901: No. 9 sold to the Tolliver Lumber Company for two thousand one hundred and fifty dollars, delivered on her own wheels. She came north under her own steam, eleven days and four railroads, with a Pell County crew who handed her over at the interchange, drank coffee in the Tolliver kitchen and went home as passengers. The shop painted out the 9 and painted on a 4, because the company numbered engines in the order it bought them, and she was the Four thereafter.

She was a disappointment for one season, put on grades she was never built for; kept afterward on the straight main stem she became an institution. Boiler retube 1909, new tires 1913, a cracked frame welded in 1919 and again in 1922, a full shopping in 1924. Twenty-seven years of work, all but the first on the main stem, with a whistle every child at Sixmile could place on the hill with their eyes shut. In September 1928 a second-hand eight-coupled engine arrived and she was set out behind the shop under a tarpaulin. In May 1929 she was cut up: *No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile this month. Scrap to Ravel Brothers, $470.* Ravel took the tires and brass separately; the boiler shell went into the mill and served out its life as a stationary tank for wash water.

The cut ran out in 1936. In 1938 Pearl drove the stock book, the shop book, the timber ledgers and eleven boxes of correspondence to the Bly County Historical Rooms herself, because she did not care to have a thing end twice. They were catalogued on arrival and drew no inquiry at all for twenty-four years.

### A4. The 1962 survey and what it established

In September 1962 the state's flood-control cut took Sallow Creek out of its bed for eleven weeks and left the gorge dry to the gravel. Ruth Frayne, born 1934, three years into the Pell County highway department and Adela Rennick's granddaughter, went down to inspect the piers. The inspection stopped being routine on the second afternoon.

She walked some eight hundred feet of bed, above and below the viaduct. There is no locomotive in Sallow Creek: no frame, no boiler shell, no driving wheel, nothing of iron larger than a spike, and no hole where any of it had been. Photographs were taken.

Forty feet below the south abutment, half in the gravel where the 1954 gang had pitched it, lay a cast-iron block the size of a kitchen stove — the old south pedestal that had carried the roller nest. Cut into the roller seat was the setting mark. She measured it three times with a steel tape and got two inches each time. The rocker bearings that had replaced the nest she also measured: four inches of travel each way.

Three written records then converged in her hands. Her father, Wendell Frayne, took his mother's black oilcloth field book off the shelf where it had sat for fifty-eight years and put it into them; it carried the 40-degree movement rule in its front matter and, on page 62, the entry of 12 September 1897 recording that the rollers were set at two inches where Sheet 11 called for three, and that W.T. had overruled the report. Judd Rennick lent her his night book at his kitchen table and let her take it away. Lettie Wexler at Kettle Bench, no relation to anybody in the story, handed over the family weather record in four cardboard boxes without asking what it was for.

The arithmetic followed. An inch per forty degrees: three inches would have bound at 54 below, two inches at 14 below — neither reachable in this valley, which is why a wrong bridge behaved like a right one for twelve years. But the August 1909 gang re-seated the same two-inch mark with the steel at 86°, and 86 less 80 is six above zero — the exact figure at which the night book says the bridge spoke sixty-one times and never once above. The Wexler pages then closed it: seven nights below six degrees between November 1897 and August 1909, four at the bottom of thirty-degree falls, nothing heard on any of them; the coldest of those years 9 below on 4 February 1899; the first sub-six night after the re-decking 17 January 1912. Two records kept by people with no occasion to compare them agreed on every one of the sixty-one nights.

She wrote to the Bly County Historical Rooms and had photostats by return, establishing that No. 9 left the valley alive in 1901 and was scrapped at Sixmile in 1929. Her report closed: the chisel mark is at two inches, Sheet 11 calls for three, there is no locomotive in this creek and there never was one, and what the valley heard for forty-three winters was a bridge letting go of itself, one inch late.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Brother and sister — Adela was Emil's younger sister. Stated independently by Judd Rennick ("my father had a younger sister, Adela"), by Wendell Frayne ("my uncle Emil Rennick… my mother, Adela Rennick, was his younger sister"), by the curator's note, and by the school pamphlet; no source disputes it. Emil was born 1866, Adela 1874.

**B2.** Judd was Adela's **nephew**; she was his paternal aunt. Established by the sibling link in B1 plus Judd's own statement that Emil was his father, and confirmed by the curator: "Judd Rennick's father, Emil, and Adela… were brother and sister." Two things point elsewhere and both fail. First, the curator's wrappers attribute the *field* book to "J. Rennick" and the *night* book to "A. Rennick" — reversed; the field book is signed A.R. inside and contains the 1897 survey entries, the night book is a station ledger in a different, blunter hand, and the curator's own closing query flags the wrappers as sitting oddly with the initials. Second, Ruth Frayne calls Judd "my father's uncle," which would make him Adela's brother; her father corrects that in writing, noting she called him uncle as a courtesy and that he never bothered to correct her.

**B3.** **First cousins once removed.** Derivation: Adela and Emil were siblings; Wendell Frayne was Adela's son and Judd was Emil's son, so Wendell and Judd were first cousins; Ruth is Wendell's daughter, which puts her one generation further along the same line. Wendell states exactly this conclusion. Rejected: Vesta Cobb's "grand-niece" (which would require Judd to be Adela's brother or a generation up) and Ruth's own "uncle."

**B4.** **Warren Tice** — resident engineer on the viaduct and afterward the railroad's chief engineer — was Dorsey Tice's father. Dorsey says so in the first person and describes his father taking him out on the deck at six and telling him he had closed the span himself in '97; Wendell Frayne records independently that "his son Dorsey kept the store." Vesta Cobb and Corwin Athey both make Dorsey a *nephew*; both are town-level hearsay against a first-person account and a family record.

Why it matters to January 1936: the man who bet five dollars that the bridge would not speak was the son of the engineer who had overruled Adela Rennick and let the nest stand at two inches. Every entry in the book he was betting against existed because of his father's decision. It also explains his behavior on tape — he opens by insisting his father "had no hand in that bridge" and was in the Ninestone office the whole time it was building, which he refutes himself twenty lines later, and he refuses to repeat the story of what his father is said to have admitted at his retirement dinner. He is not a neutral witness about that bridge, which is a reason to discount his book totals and his death-ratio claim while still crediting his account of his own wager.

**B5.** Chain of custody into Ruth Frayne's hands in 1962:

- **Field book.** Written by Adela Rennick 1895–1901; kept by her from 1901 until her death in 1959, on a shelf over her chair, never opened again but never allowed to be moved; passed to her son Wendell Frayne; he took it down and put it into Ruth's hands himself in 1962 when she said she was going to the creek. He deposited it with the Historical Society in 1963.
- **Night book.** Written by Judd Rennick at the depot, 43 winters, last entry 2 March 1956; lent by him personally to Ruth in 1962 at his kitchen table and returned to him afterward; it reached the Society only in 1969 from his estate, so any earlier citation of it is a citation of the loan.
- **Weather record.** Begun by Peter Wexler at Kettle Bench and continued by his daughter Lettie from 1926; lent whole to Ruth in 1962 in four cardboard boxes, without a receipt and without her asking what it was for; every box returned; deposited with the Society in 1967, the year Lettie died.
- **Bly County ledgers.** Kept by Pearl Tolliver Nace as the lumber company's bookkeeper; driven by her to the Bly County Historical Rooms in 1938 and catalogued on arrival; consulted by nobody for twenty-four years until Ruth wrote in 1962, whereupon they sent photostats of the 1901 stock-book and 1929 shop-book entries by return. The originals stay in Bly County; only the two photostats came south, left with her report.

The convergence is datable and self-checking: Alma Nace's "twenty-four years" of no inquiry, counted from 1938, lands on 1962. Three of the four records were handed over on trust by their keepers within weeks of each other, and none of the keepers had ever compared their book with any of the others.

## Section C — Math

### C1. Cold travel of the center span

Rule: one inch of movement per 300 feet per 40°F. The span is 300 feet exactly, so **1 inch = 40°F of cooling**. Span closed at **66°F**.

**(a) As the contract drawing specified (three inches):**
3 in × 40 °F/in = 120 °F of available cooling.
66 − 120 = **−54 °F**.

*(If Sheet 11 is taken at four inches, as Pike and Quarles have it: 4 × 40 = 160; 66 − 160 = −94 °F. Either figure is far outside the valley's recorded range, so the choice does not affect anything downstream.)*

**(b) As actually set in 1897 (two inches, from 66°):**
2 in × 40 = 80 °F.
66 − 80 = **−14 °F**.

**(c) After the August 1909 work (same two-inch mark, re-seated with the steel at 86°):**
2 in × 40 = 80 °F.
86 − 80 = **+6 °F**.

**(d) Margin lost:**
from −14 °F to +6 °F = **20 degrees**.

Note that (d) is simply the difference in seating temperature, 86 − 66 = 20, and is therefore independent both of the coefficient and of what Sheet 11 said. The number that is *not* independent is (c) — and (c) is the one that matches the observed threshold exactly, which is the reason for preferring the 40-degree rule. With the office's 50-degree rule the same three quantities give (a) −84 (or −134 at four inches), (b) −34, (c) −14 — and Quarles, who computed it that way, conceded his −14 "is not readily reconciled with the local record."

### C2. The night of the wager

**(a)** Threshold is a low *below* 6 °F. At 4:00 p.m. the thermometer read 38, falling 4 °/hour:
38 − 6 = 32 degrees to fall; 32 ÷ 4 = 8 hours; 4:00 p.m. + 8 h = **about midnight**.
So it would cross below six around midnight, leaving most of the night below the threshold. (It spoke at 1:10 a.m.; the low was 3 below.)

**(b)** Day's high 41 (at noon); night's low −3.
41 − (−3) = **44 degrees of fall**. The second condition requires at least 30, so **yes, comfortably satisfied** — as Judd worked out standing at the counter: "forty-one down to anything under six is a fall of better than thirty."

### C3. From the night book

Figures taken from the summary page at the back of the book, quoted verbatim by the curator and repeated by Judd himself: forty-three winters, sixty-one nights heard, thirty-four winters with at least one, nine with none, five silent qualifying nights, sixty-six qualifying nights in all.

**(a)** 61 ÷ 43 = 1.4186… → **1.4 nights per logged winter**.

**(b)** 61 heard + 5 silent = **66 nights met both conditions**.

**(c)** 61 ÷ 34 = 1.7941… → **1.8 nights per winter in which it was heard at all**.

**Which winters the count runs over.** The forty-three winters run from **1911–12 through 1953–54** inclusive (counting each winter by its January: 1912…1954 = 43, which is exactly the stated span, and is one of the reasons for fixing the endpoints where I do).

- *First fixed by:* the first entry, the night of 17 January 1912 — the night Judd ruled up the book. That date is given by Ruth Frayne from the Wexler page (high 34, low −2, clear, still), repeated by Athey, and independently required by the arithmetic: it was the first night under six degrees after the August 1909 re-seating. Judd's own spoken dates — "started the book" in 1907, "the first time she spoke was the winter of 1913" — are 50-year-old recollections that contradict each other and cannot both be right; neither survives against a dated page.
- *Last fixed by:* the last night entered as heard, 9 February 1954, with the new bearings going in the following month.

**Nights excluded.** The three nights in the winters of 1954–55 and 1955–56 that met both conditions and produced nothing. They are excluded because they fall outside the book's forty-three winters and after March 1954, when the roller nest — the thing being logged — no longer existed; Judd himself set them apart in a closing note dated 2 March 1956 rather than in the columns. Including them would give 8 silent nights and 69 qualifying nights, which is precisely the pair of figures Lettie Wexler and H. L. Quarles report. That is a difference of scope, not a disagreement about the book: both read or heard of it after the closing note was written and counted the post-rocker nights in.

Also excluded: Dorsey Tice's and Athey's totals of 57 nights / 30 winters / 13 silent winters, which are simply wrong (see E).

### C4. The locomotive

**(a)** Built 1889 (Rowan Works no. 1142); cut up May 1929.
1929 − 1889 = **40 years old** (39 years and some months if her build date fell late in 1889).

**(b)** Cost new $8,600.00; sold to Tolliver for $2,150.00.
2,150 ÷ 8,600 = 0.25 → **25.0 percent** of her original cost. (The exact quarter is a small check in favor of $2,150 over the $2,510 two narrators give.)

**(c)** Delivered on her own wheels after the sale of 19 June 1901; set out behind the shop in September 1928 when the eight-coupled engine arrived; cut up May 1929.
Working life for Tolliver: June 1901 → September 1928 = 27 years 3 months ≈ **27 years** — which is the figure Alma Nace gives from inside the family, and which fits a 1901 purchase rather than the 1902 she states.
Owned by Tolliver: June 1901 → May 1929 = 27 years 11 months ≈ 28 years.

### C5. Distances

**(a)** 120 + 300 + 120 = **540 feet**. (The 600 feet given by the *Sentinel* and copied by Athey is refuted by the component dimensions, which Pike and Quarles give identically.)

**(b)** Quarry at milepost 31, viaduct at milepost 22 → 9 miles at 2.2 percent.
9 × 5,280 = 47,520 feet.
47,520 × 0.022 = **1,045.44 feet ≈ 1,045 feet of fall**.
(Quarles's "eight miles" would give 929.28 ft; rejected on milepost arithmetic, the mileposts being given consistently by two narrators.)

**(c)** Recorded extremes at Kettle Bench: high 101 °F (July 1936), low −11 °F (January 1918).
Range = 101 − (−11) = 112 degrees.
112 ÷ 40 = **2.8 inches** of movement in the 300-foot span.
(On the office's 50-degree rule it would be 2.24 inches; if the low were taken as 13 below, 2.85 inches.)

Worth noting: 2.8 inches of required travel against a seat set with two inches of cold room is the whole story in one line. The bridge as built could not ride its own valley's recorded range without binding somewhere, and after 1909 the point at which it bound moved up into weather the valley got most winters.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. Necessary, not sufficient

Both conditions were **necessary and neither singly nor jointly sufficient**. Necessity: in forty-three winters of columns there is no night entered as heard whose low was not below six degrees and whose preceding day was not at least thirty degrees warmer — Judd went through the book specifically looking for an exception and found none, and Lettie Wexler, checking a dozen dates at random against a thermometer three miles away and kept for entirely different reasons, found the two books agreed to the degree on every one of the sixty-one. Insufficiency is settled by the book's own back page: sixty-six nights answered both conditions and only sixty-one produced anything, so five qualifying nights were silent. The gap is not noise — Judd identified the missing term himself, as a rule about *when* rather than a theory about *what*: she would not speak twice inside a single freeze, and every one of the five sits in a cold spell in which she had already spoken with nothing above forty degrees in the daytime in between. Add "the bearing has been returned toward the warm end of its seat by a thaw since the last release" as a third condition and the three together are necessary and sufficient across the whole logged record. A second and harder proof of insufficiency comes after the book closes: three nights in the two winters following March 1954 met both conditions exactly and produced nothing at all — because the conditions were never conditions on the weather as such, but on what a particular piece of iron would do in that weather, and that piece of iron had been thrown into the creek.

### D2. Why it cannot have been Locomotive No. 9

**Reason 1 — the engine left the valley alive and died elsewhere (Pike, Nace, the photostats).** The Cadder Valley stock book records her sold on 19 June 1901 to the Tolliver Lumber Company for $2,150, "delivered on her own wheels"; the Tolliver shop book records "No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile" in May 1929. Alma Nace's family memoir supplies the intervening twenty-seven years — eleven days and four railroads coming north, renumbering, a boiler retube, new tires, a welded frame, a whistle every child at Sixmile could place on the hill.

**Reason 2 — nobody drowned in 1898 and the engine was never in the gorge (Adela's field book, the newspaper, Emil Rennick).** Page 71 of the field book, written the same week: off the iron at the north approach at 7:20 a.m., nobody killed, Lidell's arm broken, Sherrod scalded, nine days to rerail her. The *Ninestone Sentinel* of 8 March 1898 says the same in the same particulars. Emil Rennick was on the ground that morning and told his nephew so directly. The ghost story's whole premise — a crew still down there — never happened, and the engine was on her side in daylight at the north end with half the county looking at her.

**Reason 3 — the chronology is fourteen years wrong (the night book and the Wexler record together).** The sound did not begin until 17 January 1912 — eleven years after the engine left the county and fourteen after the wreck. Between the opening of the line in November 1897 and August 1909 the Kettle Bench pages record seven nights below six degrees, four of them at the bottom of thirty-degree falls, and nobody at the Falls has ever claimed to have heard anything on one of them. A haunting that waits fourteen years, skips every qualifying night in the interval, and then starts within months of a bridge gang re-seating a bearing is not a haunting.

**Reason 4 — the sound behaved like a structure, not an engine (Judd's own senses).** It came up the rails before it reached the air: the sounder rattled in its bracket, then the floor moved, then the noise arrived, in that order, without exception, in forty-three winters. It was one report, never twice in a night, governed by a temperature threshold and a rate of fall. Nothing about a submerged locomotive predicts a six-degree threshold or a requirement that the day have been thirty degrees warmer.

**Reason 5 — the 1962 survey.** Eight hundred feet of dry creek bed walked, nothing of iron larger than a spike, photographs on the table.

*Why these are independent:* they come from different kinds of record, made by people who did not know one another and were not answering the same question. Reason 1 is company accounting in a county two hundred miles away, deposited in 1938 and untouched until 1962. Reason 2 is a contemporaneous field note plus a newspaper printed two days after the event plus an eyewitness. Reason 3 is two private registers — a station ledger and a farm thermometer — kept three miles apart for forty-three winters by people who never compared them. Reason 4 is sensory testimony from a man sitting in the room. Reason 5 is physical inspection. No one of them requires any other to be true.

*Which survive without the 1962 survey:* Reasons 1, 2, 3 and 4 — all of them. **Reason 1 is the cleanest**: two ledgers, one kept by the seller and one by the buyer, recording the same transaction from opposite ends, plus a scrapping entry twenty-eight years later. Nobody had to go into the creek to know the engine was not in it.

### D3. Did the objection cost Adela Rennick her position?

**Cannot be determined from the sources**, and the one contemporaneous document points away from it rather than toward it.

What the sources establish: she entered the objection on 12 September 1897 and was overruled; her time with the road ended on 15 May 1901; the letter of 30 April 1901 says the engineering party was reduced entire on that date; a printed office slip of 20 July 1901 re-engaged two of that party — J. Corliss, rodman, and F. Wain, chainman — from 1 August for the Ashby extension, and she is not named on it or on any other slip in her papers; and the same letter records that "Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card."

Harlan Pike asserts the causal link — Tice never forgave the entry, she was gone inside four years — but he immediately concedes he has nothing in writing, and grounds the claim in the temperature of a room he sat in as a boy. Wendell Frayne, who held the letter, argues the other way: the party was let go entire, the two men taken back were field men re-hired for field work, and the computer's post was not refilled at all because the road never had another computer. Nothing in the sources contradicts that. Against Pike also stands the recommendation clause — a man punishing an employee for an entry in her book does not volunteer to have his recommendation written on her card — and the four-year gap, which is a long fuse for a reprisal.

Against Wendell stands the fact that he is defending his mother's employer's ordinary conduct against a story told *in his own house*, and that the two re-engagements are real and she was not among them. And Adela's own silence — she never worked for a railroad again, never asked to, and would not speak of it even to her nephew when he asked twice — is compatible with either reading and settles nothing.

So: the layoff itself is documented and was collective. Whether the entry made her the one not asked back is exactly what no surviving paper says, and the only person who claimed to know declined to support it.

## Section E — Contradictions

### E1. Direct contradictions, resolutions, and method

**1. Was anyone killed in the 1898 wreck?**
*Claims:* a crew went into the gorge and is there yet (Vesta Cobb; the belief reported as the valley's by the *Sentinel* and by Athey's Chapter IV) vs. nobody killed, two hurt (Pike quoting the *Sentinel* of 8 March 1898; Adela's field book p. 71; Emil Rennick via Wendell; Athey's Chapter III).
*Resolution:* nobody was killed. *Method:* contemporaneous document — a field note written the same week naming both injured men, matching a newspaper printed two days after the event, plus an eyewitness. Folk memory does not survive that.

**2. Is the locomotive in the creek?**
*Claims:* she is there yet (Vesta; Athey Ch. IV) vs. she is not (the two ledgers; the 1962 walk of the dry bed).
*Resolution:* no locomotive; there never was one. *Method:* physical inspection plus paper from both sides of the sale. Note that Athey contradicts himself inside one pamphlet and says so honestly in a note added at press.

**3. When did the sound begin?**
*Claims:* the winter after the wreck, 1897–98 (Vesta, "seventy-odd years of it"; the *Sentinel*, "from the winter after the wreck") vs. 17 January 1912 (Ruth; Athey; the night book's first entry). Judd himself muddies it: he says he started the book in 1907 *and* that the first sound came in the winter of 1913.
*Resolution:* 17 January 1912. *Method:* two independently kept registers plus arithmetic. The Wexler pages show seven sub-six nights between November 1897 and August 1909 with nothing heard; the first sub-six night after the re-seating was 17 January 1912; and only a start in the winter of 1911–12 makes "forty-three winters" reach the last night heard on 9 February 1954. Judd's two spoken dates contradict each other and neither survives a dated page.

**4. How long did the phenomenon last?**
*Claims:* "seventy-odd years" (Vesta) vs. 1912–1954.
*Resolution:* forty-two years, forty-three winters. *Method:* dated first and last entries.

**5. When did Judd go to the depot?**
*Claims:* 1907 (Judd) vs. 1909 (Dorsey; Wendell).
*Resolution:* 1909. *Method:* two independent narrators against one man's recollection, reinforced by his own internal inconsistency — his "thirty-nine years at that depot" fits the agency of 1915–1954 exactly, not a career begun in 1907, which would have ended in 1946 while he was demonstrably still agent in 1954.

**6. When was the re-decking?**
*Claims:* 1908 (Pike) vs. "the summer of 1911" (Judd, speaking) vs. August 1909 (the note in the front of Judd's own book, transcribed identically by Judd, Ruth and the curator).
*Resolution:* August 1909. *Method:* a quoted document beating two spoken dates, one of which is its own writer's; corroborated by chronology — the two winters after the work never went below +9, and the first sub-six night after it was in January 1912.

**7. The movement coefficient.**
*Claims:* one inch per 300 feet per 40 degrees (Adela's field book, quoted by Ruth and by the curator) vs. per 50 degrees (Pike; Quarles, "the coefficient in use").
*Resolution:* 40 degrees. *Method:* predictive agreement with an independent data set. Only the 40-degree rule puts the binding point after 1909 at exactly six above zero, which is where sixty-one logged nights sit and above which not one of them sits. The 50-degree rule yields −14, and Quarles concedes his own figure "is not readily reconciled with the local record" and declines to pursue it. I note the residual: 40 degrees is the rule the party actually worked to and the one the bridge's behavior confirms; it is not thereby proven to be correct steel physics.

**8. What did Sheet 11 call for — three inches or four?**
*Claims:* three (Adela, p. 62, written the afternoon the span was closed) vs. four inches each way (Pike; Quarles, who says the contract drawings are in his office).
*Resolution:* three, on balance — but **not fully settled**. *Method:* a contemporaneous reading made at the moment of setting, corroborated by the language two separate chains use for the shortfall — Ruth's "one inch late" and the Tice retirement remark passed down as "a mark shy," neither of which works if the deficit were two inches. Against that, Quarles claims the drawings themselves; but the railroad's construction papers burned on 4 April 1911, so any Sheet 11 in a county office is a copy, and both later men wrote after 1954, when rockers giving "four inches of travel each way" went into that very span — an obvious source of contamination. Nothing downstream depends on the answer.

**9. Length of the viaduct.**
*Claims:* 540 feet (Pike's 120+300+120; Quarles identical) vs. 600 feet (*Sentinel*; Athey).
*Resolution:* 540. *Method:* arithmetic on component spans given identically by two engineering-side sources.

**10. Length of the quarry grade.**
*Claims:* nine miles (implied by mileposts 31 and 22, given by both Pike and Judd) vs. "eight miles" (Quarles).
*Resolution:* nine miles. *Method:* milepost arithmetic.

**11. The night book's totals.**
*Claims:* 61 nights / 34 winters / 9 silent winters / 5 silent qualifying nights / 66 qualifying (the summary page, quoted verbatim by the curator and by Judd) vs. 57 / 30 / 13 (Dorsey; Athey) vs. 61 nights but 8 silent qualifying and 69 qualifying (Lettie; Quarles at second hand).
*Resolution:* the book's own page. *Method:* quoted document. And the second discrepancy dissolves rather than being overruled: 5 + the 3 post-rocker nights recorded in the closing note = 8, and 66 + 3 = 69. Lettie read the book in 1962, after that note existed, and counted them in.

**12. The death ratio.**
*Claims:* 7 of 190 within the week (Judd; Athey) vs. "better than a third of the burying" (Dorsey, attributing it to Judd).
*Resolution:* 7 of 190, about 3.7 percent. *Method:* the counter's own figures, written in the back of his book and repeated to a schoolmaster in two afternoons of interview; Dorsey is repeating a number he did not compute.

**13. The date of the wager.**
*Claims:* 14 January 1936 (Dorsey, unhesitating; the *Sentinel*; Athey) vs. January 1937 (Judd).
*Resolution:* probably 14 January 1936, **weakly held**. *Method:* internal consistency — Dorsey says the agent "never once brought it up again in twenty-five years," and 1936 + 25 = 1961, the year of the recording; he also supplies the day. Judd gives only a month and year, from memory twenty-eight years later. But the apparent 3-to-1 majority is worth nothing: the *Sentinel* and Athey both got the story from the Falls, which is to say from Dorsey. It is really one man against one man, both of whom agree on every figure of the afternoon (41 at noon, 38 at four, four degrees an hour since one, three below, ten past one, five dollars), which is the signature of a well-preserved story with a date slipped. The night book would settle it in a second and nobody quotes it.

**14. Where the wager was made and how it was watched.**
*Claims:* in Dorsey's store in mid-afternoon, with Dorsey in bed upstairs when it spoke (Dorsey; Judd, independently) vs. in the depot, with both men sitting up for it (*Sentinel*; Athey).
*Resolution:* the store; they did not sit up together. *Method:* two participants against a reporter and a compiler who used the paper's back files.

**15. How the five dollars was paid.**
*Claims:* next morning before opening (Dorsey) vs. next day in the store in front of everybody (Judd).
*Resolution:* **cannot be settled**, and the two may not even conflict if the payment happened in the store early with people about. Vesta's account confirms only that Dorsey afterward told it on himself.

**16. When the bearings were changed.**
*Claims:* spring 1953 (Judd) vs. March 1954 (Pike, Quarles, the curator, the *Sentinel*, Athey, Dorsey, Ruth).
*Resolution:* March 1954. *Method:* documentary sequence plus Judd's own self-refutation — the last sound is entered 9 February 1954, and his closing note describing "the second winter since the rockers went in" is dated 2 March 1956, which only works if they went in in 1954.

**17. Who set the roller nest?**
*Claims:* Anders Berg, "everybody knew it" (Dorsey) vs. Cass Nolan, "who set the rollers" (Athey).
*Resolution:* **cannot be determined from the sources.** Two names, no document behind either, and the construction record burned in 1911. What the sources do establish is when it was set (12 September 1897, 3:40 p.m., 66°), to what mark (two inches), and whose decision it was to leave it there (Warren Tice's) — which is what actually matters and which is documented.

**18. Warren Tice's relationship to Dorsey.**
*Claims:* father (Dorsey; Wendell) vs. uncle/nephew (Vesta; Athey).
*Resolution:* father. *Method:* first-person testimony plus an independent family record.

**19. Warren Tice's part in the bridge.**
*Claims:* "My father had no hand in that bridge. He was in the Ninestone office the whole time it was building" (Dorsey, opening) vs. resident engineer on the work (Pike, Wendell, Athey, and Adela's "Reported to W.T.") — and vs. Dorsey himself twenty lines later, describing his father closing the span in '97 and taking him out on the deck.
*Resolution:* resident engineer. *Method:* internal inconsistency inside one narrator, plus three independent narrators and a contemporaneous entry. This is the clearest case in the set of a witness refuting himself.

**20. The retirement-dinner remark.**
*Claims:* Tice said at his 1931 dinner, "we set that nest a mark shy and I have listened to it ever since" (Wendell, explicitly at two removes: Emil → Adela → Wendell) vs. he said nothing of the kind (Dorsey, who was at the dinner).
*Resolution:* **cannot be settled.** Wendell grades his own evidence honestly and names the alternative — that a sentence had settled into its final shape in his mother's mouth. Dorsey was present, which is strong, but he is the same witness who had just denied his father's role outright, which is disqualifying on this exact topic. The remark fits the physical evidence perfectly, and that is a reason to want it to be true, not a reason to believe it.

**21. Adela Rennick's birth year.**
*Claims:* 1874 (Ruth; Athey) vs. 1876 (the curator; Wendell) — with Ruth and Wendell both giving her age at death in 1959 as eighty-five.
*Resolution:* 1874, on balance. *Method:* arithmetic against the agreed death year and age (1874 + 85 = 1959; 1876 + 85 = 1961), plus Wendell's internal inconsistency in giving both 1876 and eighty-five in one paragraph. Not airtight: if the *age* is the slip, 1876 stands.

**22. When the Wexler weather record began.**
*Claims:* 1884 (Lettie: her father began it in 1884, kept it to 1926, "eighty-one years of it, now" in 1965) vs. 1 January 1894 (Ruth, citing what "Miss Wexler puts"; the curator, citing "the depositor's own index sheet").
*Resolution:* 1884 more likely, but **not settled.** *Method:* the keeper's own arithmetic — 1884 + 81 = 1965, and 1884–1926 is the 42-year stretch she attributes to her father; 1894 would make her total 71 years, which she does not say. Nothing in the case depends on it, since every night at issue falls after 1897.

**23. The Wexler succession.**
*Claims:* father and daughter, Peter then Lettie (Lettie; Ruth; Wendell) vs. "two brothers named Wexler, the younger of whom carried it on" (the curator).
*Resolution:* father and daughter. *Method:* the keeper's own account plus two independent corroborations against a provenance note the curator wrote in one week with two other accessions waiting and openly marked as guesswork where he remembered to mark it.

**24. The record low at Kettle Bench.**
*Claims:* 11 below, January 1918 (Lettie; the curator, transcribing the depositor's index sheet) vs. 13 below, January 1918 (Ruth; the *Sentinel*).
*Resolution:* −11 °F. *Method:* the keeper of the record plus the index sheet accessioned with the boxes.

**25. The sale price and date of No. 9.**
*Claims:* $2,150.00 on 19 June 1901 (the Cadder Valley stock book quoted by Pike; the Tolliver stock-book photostat quoted by the curator) vs. $2,510 in 1902 (Alma) vs. $2,510 in 1901 (*Sentinel*).
*Resolution:* $2,150.00, 19 June 1901. *Method:* the same transaction recorded in the seller's book and in the buyer's book, transcribed verbatim in two separate places, against two later recollections; digit transposition is the obvious mechanism. Alma's own "twenty-seven years she worked for us" also fits 1901–1928, not 1902–1928.

**26. Was it heard at Ninestone?**
*Claims:* "We never heard it at Ninestone. My father would have written it down" (Pike) vs. "Old men here remember hearing it at Ninestone in the winter of 1918" (*Sentinel*).
*Resolution:* leans to Pike, **not fully settled.** *Method:* the mechanism — transmission along the continuous rail, registered at a depot one mile down the line — makes twenty-two miles implausible, and Pike lived there. The paper is repeating old men, in the same paragraph in which it gets the 1918 low wrong.

**27. The curator's attributions of the two books.**
*Claims:* wrapper of the field book says "J. Rennick," wrapper of the night book says "A. Rennick" vs. the initials and contents inside, and every other source.
*Resolution:* Item 1 is Adela's, Item 2 is Judd's. *Method:* internal evidence, and the curator's own closing query flagging exactly this.

**28. Ruth Frayne's occupation, per the newspaper.**
*Claims:* "Miss Frayne — the county's weather observer, who has kept the valley's temperatures for years" (*Sentinel*) vs. county highway engineer, three years in the department (Ruth; Wendell), the weather observer being Lettie Wexler.
*Resolution:* the paper has conflated two women. *Method:* two first-hand sources plus Lettie's own memoir.

**29. Ruth's and Vesta's descriptions of the Rennick–Frayne kinship.**

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What produced the sound

The Sallow Creek Viaduct's center span is a pin-connected steel truss three hundred feet long, pinned solid at its north end. The whole of its thermal movement — everything the steel does as it lengthens in July and shortens in January — was taken at one place: the south end, which rode on a nest of rollers seated in a cast pedestal. The party's working rule was one inch of movement in three hundred feet for forty degrees, so for this span, one inch of travel per 40 °F.

On 12 September 1897, at 3:40 in the afternoon, with the steel at 66°, the nest was seated at the two-inch chisel mark instead of the three inches the contract drawing called for. Adela Rennick, the party's computer, reported it. Warren Tice answered that the pedestal could not be recast before the fall haul and that two inches was more than the valley would ever ask for, and she entered the exchange in her own book "so that it is somewhere." As set, the span would exhaust its cold travel at 66 − 80 = **14 below zero**. Tice was, in the narrow sense, right: the deepest reading the valley has ever recorded is 11 below. For twelve years a wrong bridge behaved exactly like a right one, because the difference between them lay in weather that never came.

In August 1909 a bridge gang re-decked the structure — 85-pound steel for 60, new ties, both shoes jacked and set again to the same mark — and seated the south nest with the thermometer on the north pier reading 86° at four o'clock. Setting the same two inches from steel twenty degrees warmer moves the binding point twenty degrees warmer: 86 − 80 = **six above zero**. Nobody changed the mark; nobody appears to have considered it. The margin the valley's climate had quietly been covering was gone, and the men who removed it were doing careful work in good faith.

**Character of the sound.** When the span cooled past its binding point the shoe ran out of seat and stuck. Contraction did not stop on that account: the truss went on shortening against a locked bearing and stored the strain in its members and at its pins. When friction was finally overcome the shoe let go all at once and completely, and the span took up its new position in one movement. Because the release was total rather than gradual, the stored energy discharged at a single stroke: one report, deep in pitch, of very short duration — not a bell, not a rumble, not the exhaust of a locomotive, and never twice in a night, because after one release there was nothing left to release. And because the release happened *in* the structure, which was continuous with the rail, it traveled through steel far better than through air. Hence the invariable order at the depot a mile down the line: the sounder rattled in its bracket, then the floor, then the noise — and hence the thing everyone at the Falls said and nobody outside believed, that you felt it in your feet before you heard it.

**The threshold, and why six.** Six above zero is not a round number and not a coincidence: it is 86 minus 80, and 80 is two inches at forty degrees to the inch. Three quantities recorded independently — the movement rule in the front of a field book, a chisel mark on a casting recovered from a creek, and a thermometer reading on a pier one August afternoon — produce it, and a fourth record made by a man who knew none of that arithmetic then shows sixty-one nights below six degrees and not one above.

**The second condition.** A fall of at least thirty degrees inside the day was required because a slow cooling lets the bearing creep. Friction is overcome repeatedly, a small increment at a time, at low stored strain, so nothing accumulates and nothing releases; the valley could sit three weeks at zero and hear nothing. A rapid fall carries the bearing past its freedom faster than it can relieve itself, and the strain is stored rather than spent. That is why it took a soft day — mud in the road, wash on the line — and then a hard clear sky at dark.

**Why some qualifying nights were silent.** Once released, the shoe stands at the far end of its travel with nothing left to give, and only warming returns it toward the warm end of the seat — in practice a daytime thaw of some hours above about forty degrees. Every one of the five silent qualifying nights in the book sits inside a cold spell in which the bridge had already spoken and in which nothing had risen above forty in between. Judd Rennick derived that from his own columns and offered it as the one guess he would allow himself; the county engineer states the same mechanism a decade later and regrets that nobody ever told the keeper of the book he was right.

**Why it began when it did.** Not with the wreck of 1898, and not in 1899, when the valley reached 9 below — well short of the 14 below the bridge as built required. Between the opening of the line in November 1897 and the work of August 1909 the Kettle Bench record shows seven nights under six degrees, four of them at the bottom of thirty-degree falls, and nothing was heard on any of them. The two winters immediately after the re-decking never went below +9. The first night under six degrees after August 1909 fell on 17 January 1912 — high 34, low 2 below at daylight, clear and still — and that is the night the sound began and the night the night book was ruled up. The silence of those seven earlier nights is not missing data; it is the proof. Had the error of 1897 been sufficient by itself, the valley would have heard the bridge in 1899.

**Why it stopped permanently.** In March 1954 the line was rehabilitated for diesel power and the roller nest was replaced with rocker bearings giving four inches of travel each way — which cannot be exhausted at any temperature this valley has ever recorded. The last night entered as heard is 9 February 1954, a month before the gang arrived. Three nights in the two following winters met both conditions exactly and produced nothing. The cure was entirely incidental: the bearings were changed because the traffic was changing and the nest was old, and the men who ended a fifty-year phenomenon went home without knowing they had touched it.

**The competing explanation the sources offer, and why it fails.** Harlan Pike states it flatly — "I say the weight did it." The 1909 deck was forty tons heavier than the one it replaced, a roller nest takes that increase directly upon the rollers, and the noise began about that time and not before. H. L. Quarles, working from the drawings twenty years later, records the same opinion: that it is "at least as probable that the added dead load, rather than any question of travel, was what brought the bearing to bind."

It fails on four counts, of which the third and fourth are decisive.

*First*, it is not really a rival on date. The re-decking and the re-seating were the same three weeks of the same August; the timing is confounded by construction, and pointing at 1909 does not distinguish the two.

*Second*, it produces no number. Extra dead load raises friction; it does not place a threshold at six above zero rather than at zero, or ten, or twenty below. The travel account predicts six exactly, from quantities recorded by three people who never met, and is then checked against sixty-one nights and no exceptions.

*Third*, it cannot explain the second condition or the silent nights. A heavier deck is heavier in a slow cold as well as a fast one, and it is just as heavy on the second night of a freeze as on the first. Running out of seat, and then having nowhere left to go until a thaw, explains both without adding anything.

*Fourth, and fatal*: the 1909 deck was not disturbed in March 1954 and stands over that span today. Nothing was altered in that work but the bearings themselves — and the sound stopped and never returned. A cause that survives the cure is not the cause. Quarles, to his credit, half-knew this; his own computation on the office's fifty-degree coefficient gave 14 below, and he conceded in writing that his figure "is not readily reconciled with the local record," offered no reconciliation, and declined to pursue the point.

**The physical evidence recovered in 1962.** The discarded south pedestal — a cast-iron block the size of a kitchen stove, broken at one corner, thrown over the abutment by the 1954 gang and found forty feet below it in the dry bed, with the setting mark cut into its roller seat **at two inches**, measured three times with a steel tape. The railroad's construction papers burned at Ninestone on 4 April 1911, so that chisel mark is the only surviving physical record of what was done on 12 September 1897 — a thing the field book asserted for sixty-five years and the metal then confirmed.

## Section G — Summary

### G1. (119 words)

In 1897 the roller nest under the Sallow Creek Viaduct's long span was set at two inches instead of three; Adela Rennick, the computer who objected, was overruled and wrote it down. The error only bit below −14°F, so nothing happened for twelve years. In August 1909 a gang re-seated the same mark at 86°F, moving the binding point to +6°F. From January 1912 the bridge boomed on cold, fast-falling nights; the valley blamed a drowned locomotive — though No. 9 was sold north in 1901 and scrapped in 1929. Judd Rennick logged 61 nights over 43 winters; in 1962 Ruth Frayne matched his book to a farm weather record and a two-inch chisel mark. No one record showed it.
