## Section A — Reconstruction (30 points)

### A1. The building of the Sallow Creek Viaduct

The Cadder Valley Railroad contracted to build a viaduct across Sallow Creek in April 1896 to carry stone from the Ashby quarry (milepost 31) down to the county seat at Ninestone (milepost 0), a distance of 31 miles on a 2.2% descending grade. The structure was three spans totaling 540 feet: 120 + 300 + 120, with the center truss 97 feet above the creek bed.

Emil Rennick, a carpenter from Cadder Falls, built the timber falsework — the staging on which the iron was assembled before it could carry itself. His younger sister Adela Rennick served as a computer (arithmetician) and rodman for the engineering party (1895–1901), keeping two books: the company's official record and a black oilcloth pocket book of her own observations. Warren Tice was the resident engineer.

On 12 September 1897, the center span was closed at 66°F with the south bearing pedestal set at a 2-inch chisel mark. The contract drawing (Sheet 11) specified 4 inches of travel. Adela entered an objection in her field book that day, reporting the discrepancy to Tice, who overruled her, saying the pedestal "cannot be recast before the fall haul" and "two inches is more than the valley will ever ask for." She wrote the objection down "so that it is somewhere."

The bridge opened to Cadder Falls on 14 November 1897. In August 1909, the deck was renewed with heavier 85-pound steel, new ties, and both bearing shoes were re-jacked and reset to the same 2-inch mark at 86°F, adding 40 tons to the structure. 

On 4 April 1911, a fire at Ninestone roundhouse destroyed all construction records—contract files, erection timebooks, and bridge construction accounts. Adela's field book became the only surviving primary record of the engineering details.

### A2. The night book and what was heard from the bridge

From January 1912 through February 1954, the valley heard an unexplained sound on certain cold nights. It was a single deep report, one per night at most, in the small hours (typically 1–3 AM), transmitted first through the structure, felt in floors and telegraph sounders before being heard in the air. 

Judd Rennick, Emil Rennick's son, came to the Cadder Falls depot as night operator in 1909 and became station agent in 1915. Starting winter 1912–1913 (the first night of the phenomenon: 17 January 1912), he kept a ruled ledger book recording the date, day's high, night's low, whether the sound occurred, and the hour—43 winters unbroken until his retirement June 1954. The book recorded 57 nights of sound across 30 winters; 13 winters produced no sound. 

The sound occurred only under two precise conditions: (1) a night low below 6°F, and (2) a fall of at least 30° from the previous day's high. Both conditions were necessary but not independently sufficient; once the bearing released on a qualifying night, subsequent nights in the same cold spell remained silent until a warming thaw above 40°F reset the mechanism.

The valley believed Locomotive No. 9 lay in the creek, either a dead crew knocking or the engine settling with the cold. This belief grew from the coincidence that the engine had derailed at the north approach on 6 March 1898. However, Judd's careful counting over 43 years showed that of 190 deaths in Cadder Falls during his recording period, only 7 fell within a week of a night the bridge spoke—3.7%, close to the random expectation of 3%, refuting the "death omen" legend.

The last sound occurred 9 February 1954. In March 1954, the railroad rebuilt the bridge with new rocker bearings providing 4 inches of travel each way, and the sound has never been heard since.

### A3. The locomotive's second life

Locomotive No. 9, a 4-4-0 built by Rowan Works in 1889 (works number 1142), cost $8,600 new and was owned by the Cadder Valley Railroad for 12 years. After derailing at the north approach on 6 March 1898 (injuring two crew members but killing none), she was repaired and continued in service.

On 19 June 1901, she was sold to the Tolliver Lumber Company in Bly County, 200 miles north, for $2,150. She was delivered under her own steam in 11 days on 4 separate railroads by a Pell County crew (the fireman bore a burn on his wrist he refused to discuss). At Sixmile, she was renumbered No. 4, as the company numbered engines in order of purchase, not merit.

For her first season she proved a disappointment—put on grades unsuitable for her fast wheels, she slipped and boiled water away. Her owner remarked he'd "bought a racehorse to pull a stump." Thereafter she was kept on the level main stem and became an institution. Every child raised at Sixmile could locate her on a hill with eyes shut from her distinctive whistle; you could feel her in the house floor before hearing her.

She underwent boiler retube (1909), new tires (1913), two cracked-frame welds (1919, 1922), and a complete overhaul (1924, from which she emerged "sounding exactly like herself"). In September 1928, the company acquired a larger eight-coupled engine; No. 4 was set behind the shop with a tarpaulin over the stack. In May 1929 she was cut up and sold as scrap to Ravel Brothers for $470. Her boiler shell was installed as a stationary wash-water tank at the mill and remained there until the mill itself came down.

She worked 27 years for her second owner, paying for herself many times over.

### A4. The 1962 survey and what it established

In September 1962, a flood-control project by the state required drawing down Sallow Creek for 11 weeks. Ruth Frayne, a civil engineer with the Pell County highway department (granddaughter of Adela Rennick), conducted a survey of the bridge and creek bed.

Ruth walked 800 feet of creek bed—from above the north pier to below the south pier—and found no locomotive, no engine frame, no boiler, no wheels, no rods, no cab iron, no tender, and nothing larger than a rail spike. Photographs were taken documenting the empty creek bed. She recovered the cast-iron bearing pedestal that had been thrown from the bridge abutment during the 1954 rehabilitation work, broken at one corner, 40 feet below the south abutment.

The recovered pedestal bore a chisel mark on its roller seat at exactly 2 inches—the physical evidence of the 1897 setting error that Adela Rennick had documented 65 years earlier in her field book. Ruth verified that Adela's measurements were correct to the degree.

Ruth measured the bearings, applied the thermal expansion rule (1 inch of movement per 40°F change for a 300-foot span), and concluded that the phenomenon heard for 43 winters was not a locomotive but the bridge itself. The span's bearing had a binding point of 6°F after the August 1909 reseat work; below that temperature, thermal contraction of the span exhausted the available roller travel and the bearing locked. Strain accumulated until the bearing suddenly released, causing the span to drop into position with one deep report.

The 1962 survey proved:
1. No locomotive had ever been in Sallow Creek
2. The 2-inch pedestal mark was the sole physical evidence of the setting error
3. The thermal mechanism explained both the sound and why it ceased in March 1954 (when new 4-inch rocker bearings were installed)

The survey report was filed with Pell County Highway Department (September 1962) and deposited with Pell County Historical Society (1963).

## Section B — Relationships (10 points)

### B1. What was the relationship between Adela Rennick and Emil Rennick?

Adela was Emil's younger sister. Emil was born in 1866 and was a carpenter; Adela was born in 1874 (confirmed by the fact that she died in 1959 at age 85, making 1874 the correct birth year). Wendell Frayne (Adela's son) states: "My uncle Emil Rennick was born in 1866 and was a carpenter... My mother, Adela Rennick, was his younger sister."

Judd Rennick was Adela's nephew. Wendell Frayne (Adela's son, r10) states: "Judd Rennick was born in 1888. He was Emil's son, which made him my mother's nephew and my first cousin, though he was sixteen years older than I was." The accession note (r07, prior notes) confirms the family tree: "Judd Rennick's father, Emil, and Adela, who wrote the field book, were brother and sister." No source contradicts this; all sources are consistent.

### B3. What was Ruth Frayne's relationship to Judd Rennick? Derive it.

Ruth Frayne was Judd Rennick's first cousin once removed (or Judd's grand-niece). Derivation: Ruth is Wendell Frayne's daughter (born 1934). Wendell is Adela Rennick's son. Adela is Judd's aunt (Emil's sister). Therefore: Judd is Ruth's great-uncle. Equivalently, Ruth is Judd's first cousin once removed. Wendell states (r10): "Ruth is my mother's granddaughter. Judd and I were first cousins, so Ruth and Judd stood as first cousins once removed. She used to call him uncle and he never corrected her."

### B4. Who was Dorsey Tice's father, and why does that matter to the events of January 1936?

Dorsey Tice's father is not named in the sources, but Dorsey identifies him as an unnamed railroad worker who participated in closing the bridge span in 1897. Dorsey distinguishes his father from Warren Tice (the resident engineer and later chief engineer). Dorsey states (r08): "My father had no hand in that bridge. He was in the Ninestone office the whole time it was building" (clarifying a town misunderstanding). Later: "He took me out on the deck when I was six and told me he had closed the span himself in ninety-seven."

This matters to January 1936 not directly through Dorsey's father but through Dorsey's relationship to Judd Rennick: Dorsey was the skeptical storekeeper who held that "a man could not tell in the afternoon what a bridge would do at midnight." Judd, the station agent with 24 years of recorded data, bet $5 that he could. The distinction matters historically because it establishes that Judd's predictive power came from his record, not from Dorsey's family role or knowledge.

(Athey r12 states "Warren Tice, whose nephew Dorsey keeps the store today," which may be accurate or may confuse the relationship; r08's Dorsey is more authoritative on his own family.)

## Section C — Math (20 points; show working)

### C1. Thermal movement of center span

**Given:**
- Movement rule: 1 inch of travel per 40°F change (per Adela's field book inside cover, prior notes r01–r08)
- Center span: 300 feet
- Span closed: 12 September 1897 at 66°F
- Contract specification (Sheet 11): 4 inches of travel each way
- Actual setting on pedestal: 2 inches (per chisel mark, recovered 1962)

**(a) With contracted 4 inches of travel:**

4 inches of travel = 4 inches × 40°F per inch = 160°F of thermal range

From 66°F closing temperature:
- Cold end: 66°F − 160°F = −94°F (binding point for cold)
- Warm end: 66°F + 160°F = +226°F (binding point for heat)

**Answer: −94°F** (Binding would occur at −94°F, which never occurs in the valley's record; coldest on record is −11°F)

**(b) As actually set in 1897 (2 inches):**

2 inches of travel = 2 inches × 40°F per inch = 80°F of thermal range

From 66°F closing:
- Cold binding point: 66°F − 80°F = −14°F

**Answer: −14°F** (Binding would occur at −14°F, which is below the coldest night in 1897–1909; coldest was −9° on 4 Feb 1899. So the span remained free even though set incorrectly.)

**(c) After August 1909 reseat (2 inches, set at 86°F):**

The August 1909 work reset both shoes to the 2-inch mark, but the ambient temperature during reset was 86°F (per Judd's front-matter note: "Thermometer on the north pier read 86 at four o'clock when they seated the south nest").

2 inches of travel = 80°F of thermal range

From 86°F reseat:
- Cold binding point: 86°F − 80°F = +6°F (or simply 6°F below zero = 6°F)

**Answer: +6°F** (equivalently, 6°F; binding occurs when temperature drops below this threshold)

**(d) Margin lost by 1909 work:**

Margin before 1909 reseat: −14°F binding point
Margin after 1909 reseat: +6°F binding point

Difference: 6°F − (−14°F) = 6°F + 14°F = 20°F

**Answer: 20 degrees** (The 1909 work moved the binding threshold 20 degrees higher, consuming the margin that had protected the valley for 12 years.)

### C2. The wager afternoon (14 January 1936)

**Given (per Dorsey Tice r08):**
- Time of reading: 4 PM
- Temperature at 4 PM: 38°F
- Rate of fall: 4°F per hour
- Threshold temperature: 6°F (from C1c)
- Actual night's low: −3°F by daylight
- Sound was heard: at "ten minutes past one" (1:10 AM)
- Day's high: 41°F at noon

**(a) When would temperature cross 6°F threshold?**

From 38°F (at 4 PM) to 6°F (threshold):
- Temperature drop needed: 38°F − 6°F = 32°F
- At 4°F per hour: 32°F ÷ 4°F/hour = 8 hours
- 4 PM + 8 hours = 12:00 AM (midnight)

More precisely, using the night's actual low of −3°F:
- From 38°F at 4 PM to −3°F by daylight (approximately 10–12 hours)
- Temperature drop: 38°F − (−3°F) = 41°F
- At 4°F per hour: 41°F ÷ 4°F/hour = 10.25 hours
- 4 PM + 10.25 hours ≈ 2:15 AM

The sound occurred at 1:10 AM, which fits between these estimates.

**Answer: The threshold was crossed between midnight and 1:10 AM** (or more broadly, "around midnight to 1 AM")

**(b) Day's high, night's low, and satisfaction of second condition:**

Day's high: 41°F (noon, per Dorsey: "It had made forty-one at noon")
Night's low: −3°F (by daylight next morning, per Dorsey: "it went to three below")

Temperature fall: 41°F − (−3°F) = 44°F

Condition requires: 30° or more fall from previous day's high to night's low

Does 44°F ≥ 30°F? Yes.

**Answer: The fall was 44 degrees. Yes, it satisfied the second condition (30° minimum fall).**

## Section D — Logic (15 points)

### D1. Necessary vs. sufficient conditions

**Question:** Were the two conditions (1) low below threshold and (2) 30°+ fall, **necessary**, **sufficient**, **both**, or **neither**?

**Answer: Both necessary but neither alone is sufficient; together they are necessary but not sufficient (due to the bearing release/reset cycle).**

**Justification:**

Both conditions were necessary: Every night the bridge spoke, both were met. Per Lettie Wexler (r06): "Every night his book says the bridge spoke, my book says the night went under six degrees. Not one exception in what I looked at." Dorsey Tice (r08): "It only ever came in the hard sudden cold—the kind of day that is decent at dinner time and murderous by dark."

Neither alone was sufficient: Low below 6°F could occur without a 30°+ fall; a 30°+ fall could occur without reaching below 6°F. But crucially, even when both were met, sound did not always occur. Merrit (r07) cites 5 nights (Lettie cites 8) that answered both conditions and produced no sound.

Together they were necessary but not sufficient: The mechanism explains why: once a bearing releases after the first qualifying night, it stands at the far end of its travel. Subsequent qualifying nights find the bearing already deployed; it cannot release again without resetting. Resetting requires a warming thaw above approximately 40°F. In a cold spell with multiple nights below 6°F and 30°+ falls, only the first night speaks unless a warming occurs between them.

Quarles (r09) confirms explicitly: "Once the bearing has released, it stands at the far end of its travel and cannot release again, having nothing left to release. It is returned toward the warm end of the seat only by warming—in practice by a daytime thaw of some hours above about forty degrees. Successive nights within a single cold spell are therefore silent, however cold they may be, unless such a thaw has intervened. The conditions set down at the depot are necessary conditions. They are not sufficient ones."

### D2. Three independent reasons why the sound cannot have been Locomotive No. 9

**Answer:** The sound cannot have been Locomotive No. 9 because:

**Reason 1 (Documentary evidence of sale and delivery away):** The engine was sold to Tolliver Lumber Company 19 June 1901 and delivered under her own steam 11 days later after traveling 4 railroads north, 200 miles to Bly County. A Bly County shop book entry (May 1929) confirms she was cut up at Sixmile, worked 27 years for the lumber company, and was never in Sallow Creek. Documentary records (Harlan Pike r02, accession photostats r07, shop book entry r12 postscript) establish this independently. This reason requires no 1962 survey.

**Reason 2 (Mechanical incompatibility):** The sound exhibits a precise thermal threshold (6°F) and responds to a specific weather pattern (30°+ degree fall from previous day). No locomotive lying in a creek bed would have such a mechanism. The thermal binding of a structural bearing pedestal is mechanically explicable and predictable from known engineering principles; an engine's mysterious thermal responsiveness and weather-dependent voice is not. This reason is independent of any physical evidence.

**Reason 3 (Physical evidence: the recovered pedestal):** The 1962 survey recovered the cast-iron bearing pedestal bearing a chisel mark at exactly 2 inches—corresponding to the computed 6°F thermal binding point. This pedestal is the mechanism that produced the sound. No engine remains were found in 800 feet of creek bed searched, despite searching for physical evidence. This reason requires the 1962 survey but not the complete absence of engine (absence is weaker evidence than presence of the actual cause).

**Independence:** These three reasons are independent of one another:
- Reason 1 is documentary (sale records, shop books)
- Reason 2 is mechanical (bearing system vs. engine behavior)
- Reason 3 is physical evidence (actual cause identified)

None logically depends on the others. Reason 1 would hold even if the survey had never found the pedestal or confirmed the absence of engine.

### D3. Did Adela's objection cause her departure in 1901?

**Answer: No. Adela's 1901 departure had nothing to do with her 1897 objection. She left because the entire engineering party was "reduced entire" (laid off entirely) on 15 May 1901, as a normal consequence of completing the viaduct construction.**

**Justification:**

**Timing and separation:** The objection was 12 September 1897; the departure was 15 May 1901—a gap of three years, eight months. While the objection predates the departure, no causal chain is documented.

**Documentary evidence (r10, prior notes r01–r08):** Wendell Frayne provides the exact letter ending Adela's employment (30 April 1901): "The engineering party is reduced entire on the 15th May next, and your time is paid to that day. Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card." The phrase "reduced entire" means the entire party was laid off, not that Adela was singled out.

**Reemployment of some but not all (r10):** Wendell notes that two members (rodman Corliss and chainman Wain) were rehired in August 1901 for the Ashby extension work, but Adela was not. This might suggest retaliation, but Wendell explains: "The two of them were a rodman and a chainman and the work was field work; she was the only computer the road ever had and it never had another one. The party was let go entire and she was in it. There was nothing else in it."

**No documentary retaliation:** The letter itself is neutral in tone. Tice's request that "his recommendation be entered upon your card" suggests a positive reference, not punishment. No source documents any disciplinary action.

**Wendell's explicit rebuttal (r10):** Wendell directly addresses the town story: "There has been a notion going about that she was let go over something she wrote in her book, that she had crossed the engineer and was made to pay for it... I know what gets put next to it, and I will set it down here myself rather than have it sprung on you... The party was let go entire and she was in it. There was nothing else in it."

**Conclusion:** The objection was in 1897; the departure was in 1901. The entire engineering party was reduced, not Adela alone. She was the only computer the railroad had and it never had another. No evidence of retaliation exists in the primary sources. The story of connection between the objection and her departure is a town myth, not a historical fact.

## Section F — Theory (10 points)

### F1. Causal theory of what produced the sound

**The Phenomenon and Its Cause:**

For 43 winters (January 1912 through February 1954), the bridge at Sallow Creek produced a sound on certain cold nights—a single deep report, one per night, in the small hours, felt through the structure before heard in air. The sound was caused by thermal binding of the center span's roller-nest bearing pedestal.

**The Original Setting Error (September 1897):**

When the center span was closed on 12 September 1897 at 66°F, the south bearing pedestal was set to a 2-inch chisel mark instead of the 4-inch mark specified in the contract. Adela Rennick objected to resident engineer Warren Tice, who overruled her, claiming "two inches is more than the valley will ever ask for." The pedestal was never recast.

**The 1909 Trigger:**

In August 1909, the railroad renewed the bridge deck with heavier 85-pound steel and new ties, adding 40 tons of dead load. During this work, both bearing shoes were "jacked and seated again to the mark"—reset to the same 2-inch setting, but at an ambient temperature of 86°F. This seemingly routine maintenance work moved the bearing's thermal binding point into the valley's normal winter temperature range for the first time.

**The Thermal Mechanism:**

The span's center truss, 300 feet long, contracts and expands with temperature at a rate of 1 inch per 40°F change. With only 2 inches of roller travel available (80°F of thermal range) and the bearing reset at 86°F, the span would run out of cold travel at 86°F − 80°F = +6°F (i.e., 6°F below zero). 

When the valley's temperature fell below 6°F on a night following a 30°+ degree temperature drop from the previous day's high, the span's contraction exhausted the available travel. The bearing locked. Continued thermal contraction generated strain in the truss pins and members. The strain accumulated until the moment the temperature stopped falling; at that instant, the bearing suddenly released and the span dropped into its cold position—producing one sharp report transmitted with far greater efficiency along the rails than through the air, explaining why the sound was felt in the floor first, in telegraph sounders, and heard at the depot a mile away before reaching nearby houses.

**Why the Conditions Were Necessary:**

1. **The 6°F threshold** was mathematically inevitable from the combination of 2-inch setting, 40°F coefficient, and 86°F reseat temperature.

2. **The 30°+ degree fall** was necessary because a slow temperature decline allowed the bearing to creep and slip, dissipating strain incrementally without accumulation. A rapid fall forced the bearing past its friction point faster than friction could move it, causing strain to pile up instead of dissipate.

**Why Some Qualifying Nights Were Silent:**

Once the bearing released on a qualifying night and the span moved to the cold extreme, the bearing stood at the end of its travel with nothing left to release. Subsequent nights in the same cold spell, even if they met both conditions (below 6°F with 30°+ falls), found the bearing already deployed. Only a warming thaw above approximately 40°F could return the span to the center of its travel and reset the bearing for the next release. Five to eight silent nights in Judd's record all occurred within cold spells where the first qualifying night triggered release but no warming occurred before the next cold night arrived. The conditions were necessary but not sufficient.

**Why the Sound Began in January 1912, Not Before:**

The error was set in 1897 with a binding point around −14°F (2 inches setting, original 66°F closure). For 12 years, the coldest night on record that met both conditions (30°+ fall + low below threshold) was −9°F on 4 February 1899—above −14°F, so the span never bound. On 17 January 1912, the valley's weather record (Lettie Wexler) shows high 34°F, low −2°F, a 36° fall—the first night in the recorded history where both conditions were met *after* the 1909 reseat had moved the binding point to +6°F. That night, the span bound and released with a report for the first time.

**Why the Sound Stopped Permanently in 1954:**

In March 1954, the railroad rebuilt the bridge for diesel power. The old roller-nest bearing was replaced with new rocker bearings providing 4 inches of travel each way (160°F of thermal range). At any normal operating temperature in the valley, the span could move 160°F before binding. Even from the warmest 86°F reseat: 86°F − 160°F = −74°F—a binding point never reached in the valley's 80+ year temperature record (coldest is −11°F). The physical mechanism that could bind was eliminated. Three qualifying nights in the two winters after 1954 produced no sound, confirming the mechanism was gone. The phenomenon ceased permanently.

**Competing Explanation (Locomotive No. 9) and Why It Fails:**

The valley believed Locomotive No. 9 lay in the creek bed, either a dead crew knocking or the engine settling as it shifted in cold water. This explanation fails because:

1. **Documentary evidence:** The engine was sold 19 June 1901 to Tolliver Lumber Company 200 miles north and delivered under her own steam. A Bly County shop book (May 1929) confirms she was cut up at Sixmile, never in Sallow Creek. This alone refutes the locomotive theory without needing the 1962 survey.

2. **Mechanical impossibility:** No engine in a creek bed would have a precise 6°F threshold or require a 30°+ degree fall to operate. The mechanism is explicable only through structural bearing physics, not engine behavior.

3. **Physical evidence:** The 1962 survey recovered the bearing pedestal with its 2-inch chisel mark and found no engine remains in 800 feet of creek bed—the positive evidence of cause (the pedestal) and the absence of the alternative (no engine) together conclusively disprove the legend.

**Physical Evidence Confirming the Theory:**

The recovered cast-iron pedestal (1962 survey) bears a chisel mark at precisely 2 inches on the roller seat—the "one satisfactory feature of the whole business" (Quarles). This mark, set in 1897, proves the bearing was installed 1 inch short of specification and directly corresponds to the calculated 6°F binding point. The mark is hard evidence, independent of testimony. It confirms Adela Rennick's field book entry of 12 September 1897, written 65 years before recovery, to the exact fraction of an inch.

## Section G — Summary (5 points)

### G1. Summary in 120 words or fewer

A railroad bridge at Sallow Creek was built in 1897 with its bearing set 1 inch too short (2 inches instead of 4), an error the resident engineer refused to correct. For 12 years the error was silent because winter temperatures never reached the binding threshold of −14°F. In 1909, a deck-strengthening project moved the binding threshold to +6°F—into the valley's observable range. For the next 43 winters, on cold nights following 30°+ degree daily falls, the span cooled past 6°F, the bearing bound and released suddenly, producing one sharp report felt through the structure. The town believed Locomotive No. 9 lay in the creek, but it had been sold north in 1901. A 1962 survey found the bearing pedestal (with its 2-inch mark) and no engine. New bearings installed in March 1954 provided enough travel to prevent binding, and the sound ceased permanently.
