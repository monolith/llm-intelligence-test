# Comprehensive Notes: Retellings r01–r08

## Timeline & Locations

**Cadder Valley Railroad**: 31-mile line
- Ninestone: milepost 0 (county seat, shops, HQ, chief clerk's office)
- Sallow Creek Viaduct: milepost 22 (the bridge)
- Cadder Falls: milepost 23 (mill town)
- Ashby quarry: milepost 31
- Grade from quarry to viaduct: 2.2% throughout

**Sixmile Tram** (Bly County, 200 miles north)
- Founded by Silas Tolliver (born 1849, died 1922)
- Eventually 19 miles of 30-pound rail
- Ran up three drainages with switchbacks
- Cut ended 1936; company wound up 1938

**Kettle Bench** (Wexler farm)
- Three miles up valley from Cadder Falls
- On shoulder above creek where frost sits longer
- Weather record kept there (Peter Wexler, then Lettie)

**Bridge Construction & Opening**
- Began: April 1896
- Opened to Cadder Falls: 14 November 1897
- Three spans: 120 ft + 300 ft (center, truss) + 120 ft = 540 ft total
- Height above water: 97 feet
- Center span pinned at north end, south end free on rollers

**The Crash & Aftermath**
- 6 March 1898, 7:20 AM: Locomotive No. 9 derailed at north approach
  - Two hurt (Lidell's arm broke, Sherrod scalded at deck), nobody killed (contradicts valley legend)
  - Took nine days to rerail
  - Entry in field book (page 71): "March 6, 1898. No. 9 off the iron at the north approach, 7:20 a.m., running away from the quarry. Nobody killed. Lidell's arm broke, Sherrod scalded at the deck. Nine days to rerail her."
  - Matches Ninestone Sentinel 8 March 1898 exactly

**No. 9 After Derailment & Sale**
- 19 June 1901: Sold to Tolliver Lumber Company (Bly County, 200 miles north)
  - **CONFLICT on price**: Alma Nace (r05) says $2,510; Harlan Pike (r02), prior notes, and accession photostats say $2,150. **$360 difference.**
  - Delivered under own power ("steamed away north"), 11 days, 4 railroads, Pell County crew
  - Fireman had burn on wrist, wouldn't talk about it (per Alma)
  - Renumbered to No. 4 at Sixmile
  - Worked 27 years on main stem
  - Boiler retube 1909, new tires 1913, cracked frame welded 1919 and 1922, overhaul (shopping) 1924
  - Distinctive whistle; could feel her in house floor; every child could locate by sound
  - Sat behind shop Sept 1928, cut up May 1929
  - Shop book entry: "No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile this month. Scrap to Ravel Brothers, $470."
  - Boiler became stationary wash-water tank at mill (Alma says it was "still there when the mill came down")

**Destruction of Records**
- 4 April 1911: Fire at Ninestone roundhouse/office destroyed all construction records (contract files, erection timebooks, bridge construction accounts)

**Bridge Maintenance**
- August 1909: Re-decking project
  - 3 weeks duration
  - 85-pound steel (replaced 60-pound) per both Alma (r05) and Judd's entry
  - New ties throughout
  - Both shoes jacked and reset to the mark
  - Thermometer on north pier: 86° at 4 PM when south nest was seated
  - New deck: 40 tons heavier than original (per Alma's shop-book quotation and Judd's note)
- Spring 1953–1954: Rehabilitated for diesel power
  - Old cast pedestals under center span removed, thrown into creek
  - New rocker bearings installed (4 inches travel each way)
  - March 1954 (per accession): new bearings went in; March 1953–1954 per prior notes
  - Last night bridge spoke: 9 February 1954 (per accession note)

## The Bridge's Thermal Movement & Engineering

**Technical Rule**: One inch of travel per 40 degrees of temperature change (three-hundred-foot span)

**Original Design (Sept 12, 1897 Closure)**
- Thermometer reading: 66°F
- Sheet 11 called for 3 inches of travel on south nest
- Chisel mark (setting mark) set at 2 inches
- Adela Rennick's field book entry (page 62): "Sept. 12, 1897. Span closed 3:40 p.m., thermometer 66°. Rollers set at the two-inch mark. Sheet 11 calls for three. Reported to W.T., who answers that the pedestal cannot be recast before the fall haul and that two inches is more than the valley will ever ask for. Entered here so that it is somewhere. — A.R."
- Reported discrepancy to resident engineer Warren Tice
  - Tice's response: pedestal cannot be recast before fall haul, "two inches is more than the valley will ever ask for"
  - Entered "so that it is somewhere"

**August 1909 Re-setting**
- Shoes jacked and reset to the same 2-inch mark, but with steel at 86°F
- This created binding point at 6°F (86 minus 80 = 6°)

**Ruth Frayne's Calculation**
- Rule: 1 inch per 40°
- 2-inch setting = 80° of movement range
- Closed at 66°; 66 − 80 = −14°F (valley never reached this)
- After Aug 1909 re-setting at 86°: 86 − 80 = +6°F (the exact threshold)
- Nov 1897–Aug 1909: 7 nights below 6° (per Lettie's count of 1897–1909 period); coldest was −9° (4 Feb 1899); no sound heard
- First sound: 17 Jan 1912 (low −2°), after Aug 1909 reset made 6° the binding point

## The "Sound" or "Boom"

**Physical Cause**: Bridge span cooling past its binding point (6°F), south shoe hung up, span dropped suddenly with one deep report that ran along rails

**Characteristics** (all sources consistent):
- One deep report, not rattle or roll
- Came up rails first (sounder rattled before sound reached room)
- Then floor moved
- Then audible noise
- Did not travel; was "there and then done"
- Only on killing nights: midnight to 4 AM (usually ~2 AM)
- Only when night's low was below 6°F AND preceded by 30°+ degree drop from previous day
- Like floor coming up to meet you (Dorsey Tice, r08)
- Only in sudden cold (warm day then murderous by dark), never in slow cold (could go 3 weeks at zero, no sound)

**Judd Rennick's Night Book** (43 winters, started winter 1907, recorded 1913–1956)
- Five columns: date, day's high, night's low, whether sound occurred, hour of occurrence
- Total nights sound occurred: 61 (per accession note summary in Judd's book)
  - **CONFLICT**: Dorsey Tice (r08) says "Fifty-seven nights"
- Winters with at least one: 34 (per prior notes); 30 (per Dorsey r08 saying "Thirty winters she spoke in")
- Winters with none: 9 (per prior notes); 13 (per Dorsey saying "thirteen she never opened her mouth")
  - **Math check**: 30 + 13 = 43 winters (Dorsey's count is self-consistent), but different from 34 + 9 = 43 (prior notes' count)
  - One or the other (or Dorsey) is misremembering or misquoting
- Nights meeting both conditions but no sound: 5 (per accession note, which directly quotes Judd's summary)
  - **CONFLICT**: Lettie Wexler (r06) read the same book and says "eight nights" answered conditions but gave nothing
  - She writes: "His summing was at the back. Sixty-one nights she spoke, he had, over the winters he kept it. And eight nights answered both the conditions and gave nothing at all — cold enough, and the fall steep enough, and no sound out of her."
  - Merrit Sable (r07) cites the same summary page: "Five nights answered both the conditions and gave nothing, which makes sixty-six nights that answered them in all."
  - **Direct eyewitness conflict**: Lettie (reading in 1962) vs. Merrit (reading in 1970, with quote directly from book); same book, different readings (5 vs. 8)
  - Total conditions-met nights: 66 (per Merrit citing Judd's summary: 61 + 5 = 66); 69 (per Lettie's count: 61 + 8 = 69)
- Front matter note on 1909 bridge gang: "Bridge gang here three weeks, August 1909. New 85-lb. steel, new ties, both shoes jacked and set again to the mark. Thermometer on the north pier read 86 at four o'clock when they seated the south nest. Mr. Coyle says the deck is forty ton heavier than it was."
- Back of book: summary—61 nights spoke, 34 winters with sound, 9 winters silent, 5 nights with conditions met but no sound (per accession transcription)
- Last entry: "Second winter since the rockers went in. Three nights under six degrees, three falls of better than thirty, and not a sound out of her either winter. I have kept this book forty-three winters and I do not know now what it was I was listening to."
  - Dated 2 March 1956

**Judd's Theory on Silent Nights**
- Won't speak twice in single freeze; needs real thaw (above 40° daytime) between occurrences
- All 5 (or 8?) silent nights occurred in cold spell following earlier sound, before thaw above 40°

**Judd's Death Count**
- Total deaths at Cadder Falls during his recording years: 190
- Within a week after sound: 7
- Percentage: 3.7%
- Contradicts "death omen" legend
- Dorsey Tice adds: Judd counted/told him "better than a third of the burying in this town came inside the week after a night she spoke," which would be ~63+ deaths (more than Judd's specific count of 7 in all years)

**Wager Incident** — **MAJOR DATE CONFLICT**
- **Dorsey Tice (r08) says: 14 January 1936**. He states this "without hesitation" and has "told it a hundred times and it does not change."
- **Prior notes (from r03) label it: 29 January 1937**
- Dorsey's account (r08): Judd came ~4 PM for kerosene gallon can. "It had made forty-one at noon. It was thirty-eight at four o'clock. And it had been coming down four degrees an hour since one." Judd quoted all three figures like "a man reading a bill of lading." Dorsey bet $5 it wouldn't speak. Judd took the bet. Low that night −3°, spoke at "ten minutes past one" (1:10 AM). Judd won. Dorsey paid next morning before opening store. Judd never brought it up again in 25 years.
- Judd's temperature math (per Dorsey): 41° noon, 38° at 4 PM, 4°/hour drop since 1 PM. By 1 AM (9 hours later) = 38 − 36 = 2°. Low −3° by daylight fits. Day high 41° to low −3° = 44° drop (or higher if actual high before noon was above 41°).
- Dorsey repeated: "Five dollars was five dollars in 1936."
- This date appears nowhere in the accession notes or r06 or r07.

**Wexler Family Weather Record**
- Location: Kettle Bench, 3 miles up valley from Cadder Falls
- **CONFLICT on start date**: 
  - Lettie Wexler (r06, prose): "My father was Peter Wexler. He began the weather record in 1884 and kept it until 1926"
  - Merrit Sable (r07, accession note from Lettie's own index sheet): "beginning of the record as 1 January 1894"
  - Prior notes cite: "Jan 1, 1894–ongoing"
  - **10-year discrepancy; accession authority (Lettie's index sheet) says 1894, but Lettie herself in r06 says 1884**
- Record keepers:
  - Lettie says: father Peter (1884–1926), she continued (1926–1965, 39 years)
  - Merrit says: "kept by two brothers named Wexler, the younger carried it on after elder gave it up"
  - **Conflict**: Lettie's account (father then daughter) vs. Merrit's (two brothers, younger inherits)
- Daily: high, low, precipitation (snow measured melted)
- Thermometer on north wall of milk house, never moved
- Reading at daylight and 4 PM; night low unless "strange night"
- Lettie never missed a morning since 1926 (record unbroken)
- Highest temp: 101° (July 1936) — verified by Lettie taking twice
- Lowest temp: −11° (January 1918) — per Lettie and accession note
- No mention of bridge or sound in 80+ years (unrelated to story)
- In 1962, Frayne girl (Ruth) came, requested whole record, Lettie gave four cardboard boxes, all returned
- Ruth asked first for 17 Jan 1912: Lettie found it in father's hand: "Jan. 17. High 34. Low −2 at daylight. Clear, still."
- Lettie's analysis of 1897–1909 (12 years after line opened): 7 nights below 6°, only 4 came after 30° drops, coldest −9° (4 Feb 1899)
- 1909–1910 and 1910–1911: never went below 9° above zero, mildest pair of winters
- 1917–1918: deepest cold, −11° Jan 1918 (lowest in all series)
- Lettie compared Judd's night book to her record (1962): every night he recorded sound, her book showed below 6°, not one exception; took dozen dates at random, all agreed to the degree; never met Judd; thought he was a man she would have gotten along with
- Lettie's reading of Judd's summary: "sixty-nine nights in all that answered them, of which sixty-one spoke and eight did not" — **vs. Merrit's reading (from same page): 66 nights total, 61 spoke, 5 silent**
- Sound stopped "some years back," Lettie couldn't tell year within two, no theory
- Lettie advised: take reading same hour, write in ink, don't correct yesterday, don't skip days, record empty days too

**Sound & Deaths**: Judd counted 190 deaths at Cadder Falls during recording years; 7 within week after sound (3.7%, expected ~3% random). Contradicts "death omen" legend.

## The Engine: Locomotive No. 9 / No. 4

**Build & Original Service**
- Builder: Rowan Works, 1889, works number 1142
- Type: 4-4-0 American-type
- Cost new: $8,600 (per Pearl Tolliver's marginal note in advertisement, r05)
- Original owner: Cadder Valley Railroad
- Twelve years old when sold (1889 to 1901 = 12 years)

**Tolliver Lumber Company Purchase**
- Date: 19 June 1901
- **PURCHASE PRICE CONFLICT**:
  - Alma Nace (r05): "two thousand five hundred and ten dollars"
  - Harlan Pike (r02): "two thousand one hundred and fifty dollars"
  - Accession photostats (r07, stock book entry from Bly County Historical Rooms): "$2,150.00"
  - Prior notes: "$2,150"
  - **Discrepancy: $2,510 (Alma) vs. $2,150 (everyone else). $360 difference.**
- Condition: "Delivered on her own wheels" (under own steam)
- Delivery: 11 days, 4 railroads, Pell County crew
  - Crew included fireman with burn on wrist (wouldn't talk about it per Alma)
  - Crew ate in Tolliver kitchen (Pearl fed them per Alma)
- Renumbered: From No. 9 to No. 4 at Sixmile (Tolliver numbered engines in order bought, not order of merit; by 1902, No. 4 was in the company's numbering)
- Nickname: "the Four"; younger people didn't know she'd been anything else

**Service at Sixmile**
- First season: disappointment (slipped, sulked, boiled water away on grades not designed for her; grandfather said he'd "bought a racehorse to pull a stump")
- Thereafter: kept on main stem (level ground); became "an institution"
- Duration: 27 years, nearly all on main stem
- Maintenance:
  - 1909: Boiler retube
  - 1913: New tires
  - 1919 and 1922: Cracked frame welded (shop foreman regarded second weld as personal insult)
  - 1924: Full shopping (overhaul down to bones, reassembled, came out "sounding exactly like herself" per Pearl)
- Sound: Distinctive whistle, recognizable from any other in county; could feel her in house floor before hearing her; every child raised at Sixmile could locate her on hill with eyes shut
- Retirement:
  - Sept 1928: Company took delivery of second-hand eight-coupled engine; No. 4 set out behind shop with tarpaulin over stack, snow banked on running board
  - Men walked out to look at her (so did Alma); she sat there through winter
  - May 1929: Cut up
  - Shop book entry (Pearl's): "No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile this month. Scrap to Ravel Brothers, $470."
  - Ravel Brothers knew what they were about; tires and brass taken separately; boiler shell became stationary wash-water tank at mill; still there when mill came down (Alma wondered if anybody filling bucket knew what they were filling it from)

**Return on Investment**
- Cost: $2,150 (or $2,510 per Alma) in 1901
- Proceeds: $470 scrap in 1929
- "Two thousand five hundred and ten dollars she cost us, and four hundred and seventy she fetched, and in between she paid for herself so many times over that my grandfather stopped counting, which for him was a form of praise." — Alma Nace (r05)

**Connection to Cadder Valley Noise**
- Alma (r05) notes: "The county the Four came from had some noise on a bridge down there — a sound in the winters that they made stories about. I never knew the rights of it and I never inquired. It was two hundred miles away and it had nothing to do with our engine, who spent those years hauling logs off three drainages in Bly County with a whistle you could hear at the schoolhouse."
- Alma explicitly disclaims any connection to the bridge noise

## Summary of Mechanism

**The Bridge Flaw**: On 12 September 1897, the center span's south bearing (roller nest pedestal) was set at 2 inches of travel instead of the contracted 3 inches. Adela Rennick reported this discrepancy to resident engineer Warren Tice, who refused to stop work, claiming the pedestal could not be recast before fall haul and "two inches is more than the valley will ever ask for."

**The Trigger**: In August 1909, a construction gang re-decked the bridge, adding 40 tons to the structure and resetting both shoes to the "mark" they had been on—which was 2 inches. Critically, they performed this work at 86°F. The thermal calculation revealed this reset created a binding point: with 80° of thermal range (2 inches × 40°/inch), the span would bind at 86 − 80 = 6°F.

**The Manifestation**: From 1897 through 1908, winter temperatures in the valley never dropped below 6°F when conditions (30°+ degree fall) were met; the coldest on record was −9°F (4 Feb 1899), but that occurred without a preceding 30°+ fall. Thus the 1897 error remained silent. On 17 January 1912, the valley experienced its first night meeting both conditions *after* the binding point was established: high 34°, low −2°, a 36° fall. The span cooled to 6°F and beyond, the south shoe hung up on travel exhausted, the span carried load in bending stress, the shoe suddenly released, and the span dropped into position with one deep report that resonated along the rails.

**The Paradox of Silence**: The bridge was "wrong" (2 inches instead of 3) from 1897, but behaved correctly because required thermal conditions never aligned. It remained "wrong" through forty-six winters undetected. The 1909 work, performed in good faith in heat, inadvertently finalized the error by resetting the mechanism to a binding point that the valley would reach. The mechanism spoke 61 times (or 57, per Dorsey) in 43 winters until 1954, when rockers with 4 inches of travel each way were installed, providing enough slack that 6°F no longer mattered.
