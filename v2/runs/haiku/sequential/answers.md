# Complete Answer Sheet: Cadder Falls Retelling Test

## Section A — Reconstruction (30 points)

### A1. The building of the Sallow Creek Viaduct

The Cadder Valley Railroad commissioned a viaduct across Sallow Creek to connect the Ashby quarry (milepost 31) with Ninestone (milepost 0) and the mill town of Cadder Falls (milepost 23). Construction began in April 1896 and took a year and a half. The resident engineer was Warren Tice. Emil Rennick, a local carpenter, built the falsework—the temporary timber scaffolding supporting the iron during assembly.

The bridge comprised three steel spans: 120 feet, 300 feet (the center truss), and 120 feet again, totaling 540 feet and rising 97 feet above the creek bed. The grade descending from the quarry to the viaduct was held at 2.2 percent over nine miles. The center span was pinned at its north end and free to move at the south end, carried by a nest of rollers seated in a cast pedestal to allow thermal expansion and contraction.

On September 12, 1897, the center span was closed (final connection made) at a temperature of 66 degrees. The contract drawing (Sheet 11) called for three inches of travel in the south nest; however, the resident engineer decided the nest was set instead to a two-inch mark, with the justification that two inches "is more than the valley will ever ask for."

The movement rule for the bridge was one inch of travel per 40 degrees of temperature change—established by Adela Rennick, a railroad computer (mathematical engineer) who worked for the engineering party and kept duplicate records of all measurements. Adela Rennick, Emil's younger sister, entered her objection to the two-inch setting in her field book when it was made, noting that Sheet 11 specified three inches.

The line opened to Cadder Falls on November 14, 1897. On March 6, 1898, locomotive No. 9 derailed at the north approach while descending from the quarry without adequate braking—one incident that would later generate the valley's central legend, though no crew member was killed, only two injured.

In August 1909, the deck was renewed with heavier rail (85-pound steel instead of 60-pound) and new ties. Both shoes were jacked and reset to the same two-inch mark they had occupied since 1897—with the steel temperature standing at 86 degrees. This action, combined with the 40-ton increase in deck weight, would later prove consequential.

The bridge was last rebuilt in March 1954 when the railroad retrofitted it for diesel engines, replacing the roller nest bearings with rocker bearings capable of four inches of travel in each direction. A fire at Ninestone in April 1911 had destroyed the railroad's construction records, leaving Adela's field book the only surviving documentation of the original decisions.

### A2. The night book and what was heard—including the valley's belief

Beginning in January 1912, the valley heard a deep sound on certain winter nights. It came in the small hours—between midnight and four o'clock, most commonly around two—felt through the floorboards of houses before reaching the ears, a single report rather than a sustained or repeated noise. The sound had no resemblance to a train whistle or to a bell.

The Cadder Falls station agent, Judd Rennick (son of Emil, who built the viaduct's falsework), began recording these occurrences on January 17, 1912, in a ruled notebook. He entered five columns for each night: the date, the day's high temperature, the night's low temperature, whether the sound came, and the hour. He kept this record for 43 consecutive winters, documenting 61 nights on which the sound occurred.

From examining the pattern, Judd discovered two conditions that were necessary (though not sufficient) for the sound to occur:

1. The night's low temperature had to fall below six degrees above zero.
2. The day's high had to be at least 30 degrees warmer than that night's low.

These conditions had to occur together. Five nights met both conditions but produced no sound—always within a cold spell where no thaw above 40 degrees had intervened since a previous occurrence.

The valley's explanation was that the sound was the locomotive No. 9, which had derailed into the gorge 14 years before Judd began his record. According to this belief, the engine lay underwater and emitted sound as the cold worked upon it, or as the crew—also supposedly in the gorge—knocked. The belief carried folkloric weight: many old women of Cadder Falls held that the sound "rang for a death" in the town—that someone would be buried within a week of hearing it. Judd counted: in 190 deaths recorded during his book-keeping years, only seven occurred within a week of a night the bridge spoke. The ratio contradicted the legend.

In January 1936 (or 1937—sources conflict), Judd wagered five dollars with Dorsey Tice, the storekeeper, that he could predict the sound. Having calculated that the temperature would fall below six degrees after a 30-degree drop from noon (41 degrees) to evening (38 degrees, falling at four degrees per hour), Judd predicted the bridge would speak before morning. It did, at 1:10 a.m., with the low reaching three degrees below zero. Judd collected.

The sound ceased permanently after the railroad replaced the bridge's bearings in March 1954.

### A3. The locomotive's second life

Locomotive No. 9 was a 4-4-0 American-type engine built by the Rowan Locomotive Works in 1889 (works number 1142) at a cost of $8,600—a significant sum for the Cadder Valley Railroad. After derailing on March 6, 1898, at the north approach of the newly opened Sallow Creek Viaduct, the engine was rerailed within nine days.

Rather than scrapping or abandoning the engine after the accident, the railroad company sold it on June 19, 1901, to the Tolliver Lumber Company in Bly County, 200 miles to the north, for $2,150. The locomotive was delivered under its own power, steaming north on its own wheels with a Cadder Valley crew who handed it over at the interchange and returned home by passenger car.

The Tolliver company renumbered the engine as No. 4 (numbering in the order acquired, not by merit), and it became their primary hauling locomotive for the Sixmile tram—a 19-mile, lightweight-rail operation running three drainages from the lumber company's territory. Working primarily on the main stem of the tram, No. 4 proved ideal for the work: fast enough to move loads at a pace faster than walking, with distinctive sound and performance. Over 27 years of service with Tolliver, the engine underwent a boiler retube (1909), new tires (1913), frame welding (1919 and 1922), and a complete shopping (1924) that took it down to its bones and reassembled it. It was never in the gorge at Cadder Falls; during those same years it worked continuously in the woods of Bly County.

In September 1928, Tolliver acquired a second-hand eight-coupled (more powerful) engine and set No. 4 behind the shop for retirement. In May 1929, after sitting through the winter under tarps and snow, the Tolliver company shop book recorded: "No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile this month. Scrap to Ravel Brothers, $470."

The engine fetched $470 as scrap—less than 6 percent of its original cost. The Ravel Brothers separated the tires and brass, and the boiler shell was installed at the Tolliver mill as a stationary wash-water tank, where it remained until the mill came down decades later.

### A4. The 1962 survey and what it established

In September 1962, Ruth Frayne, a civil engineer with the Pell County highway department, conducted an inspection at Sallow Creek while the creek bed was dry—drawn down for 11 weeks by the state's flood-control cut. She walked the creek bed for over 800 feet above and below the viaduct, documenting conditions for the county's records. This routine structural inspection became the watershed event for understanding the bridge's history.

**Finding One:** There is no locomotive in Sallow Creek. No frame, no boiler shell, no wheels, no driving rods, no cab iron, no tender, and no discernible hole where any of it had been. Frayne's photographs, taken systematically, show a creek bed with a bridge and gravel, nothing more.

**Finding Two:** Forty feet below the south abutment, in the gravel where the 1954 bridge gang had thrown it over when replacing the bearings, lay the cast-iron south pedestal—the ancient shoe that had cradled the roller nest. Frayne measured it three times with a steel tape. The chisel mark on the roller seat, the setting mark used at erection in 1897, was at the two-inch position. The contract drawing (Sheet 11) specified three inches; Sheet 11 called for four inches according to office records, but two inches was what had been set.

**Finding Three:** Her grandmother Adela Rennick's field book, kept by her father Wendell, contained the rule for the bridge's movement (one inch of travel per 40 degrees of temperature change) and Adela's own dated objection to the two-inch setting. Frayne used this rule to establish the critical temperatures at which the span would bind.

**Finding Four:** The night book kept by Judd Rennick for 43 winters, lent to Frayne by Judd himself, showed 61 nights on which the bridge was heard, all of them occurring when the night's low fell below six degrees and the day's high had been at least 30 degrees warmer. No nights heard before August 1909; no nights heard after March 1954.

**The Explanation:** When the center span cooled, it shortened. The south shoe, constrained to a two-inch setting (versus the four inches specified), could not accommodate the contraction. Once the binding point (6 degrees above zero, reached after the 1909 re-decking in summer heat) was crossed during cooling, the span accumulated strain until the bearing released suddenly, dropping the span into a new position with a single deep report transmitted efficiently along the continuous rails. The 40-ton heavier deck installed in 1909 increased the static load on a bearing designed for less, accelerating the binding.

The valley heard not a ghost train but a bridge arriving where it should have been from the start—with a chisel mark one inch shy of the proper setting, calling out on the coldest nights of the year for 43 winters.

## Section C — Math (20 points; show working)

### C1. Temperature and cold travel exhaustion

**Rule:** 1 inch of travel per 40 degrees of temperature change (per Adela Rennick's field book, confirmed by Ruth Frayne's calculations).

**Span closed:** 66 degrees (September 12, 1897).

**(a) As specified in Sheet 11 (3 inches of travel):**

Travel: 3 inches  
Temperature equivalent: 3 inches × 40 degrees/inch = 120 degrees  
Exhaustion point: 66° − 120° = −54°F (54 degrees below zero)

**(b) As actually set in 1897 (2 inches of travel):**

Travel: 2 inches  
Temperature equivalent: 2 inches × 40 degrees/inch = 80 degrees  
Exhaustion point: 66° − 80° = −14°F (14 degrees below zero)

**(c) After August 1909 re-decking (2 inches, re-seated at 86 degrees):**

When re-seated in August 1909, the steel temperature was 86 degrees.  
Travel available: 2 inches  
Temperature equivalent: 2 inches × 40 degrees/inch = 80 degrees  
Exhaustion point: 86° − 80° = +6°F (6 degrees above zero)

**(d) Margin cost of 1909 work:**

Margin lost = Exhaustion point (1897) − Exhaustion point (1909)  
Margin lost = −14°F − (+6°F) = −20°F

The 1909 work cost 20 degrees of safety margin. The span could now bind at 6 degrees above zero instead of not binding until 14 degrees below zero.

### C2. The wager temperature calculations

**Given:** 4 PM reading was 38°F and falling at 4°F per hour.

**(a) Time to cross below 6 degrees:**

Temperature drop needed: 38° − 6° = 32 degrees  
Rate of fall: 4°F per hour  
Hours needed: 32° ÷ 4°/hour = 8 hours  
Time: 4 PM + 8 hours = 12 AM (midnight)

The temperature would cross below 6°F around midnight.

**(b) Day's high to night's low, and condition check:**

High: 41°F (at noon, per Judd's and Dorsey's accounts)  
Low: 3°F below zero (−3°F, per Judd and Dorsey)  
Fall: 41° − (−3°) = 44 degrees  

Did it satisfy the second condition (≥30 degrees)?  
Yes. 44 degrees ≥ 30 degrees. ✓

## Section D — Logic (15 points)

### D1. Were the two conditions necessary, sufficient, both, or neither?

The two conditions—a night low below 6°F AND a day-to-night temperature fall of at least 30 degrees—were **necessary but not sufficient** for the bridge to be heard.

**Justification:** Judd's night book records 61 nights on which the bridge spoke. All 61 occurred when both conditions were met (night ≤6°F and ≥30° fall). There is no instance of a night heard when either condition was absent.

However, 5 additional nights met both conditions and produced no sound. Judd's own analysis, confirmed in his interview, identifies the reason: a bearing that has already released (during the same cold spell) cannot release again without warming above 40°F first. These 5 nights fell within cold spells where the bearing had already been triggered and not yet warmed enough to reset. Thus both conditions are necessary (absence of either means no sound) but not sufficient (presence of both does not guarantee sound if the bearing is already exhausted).

### D2. Three independent reasons the valley's sound cannot have been Locomotive No. 9

**Reason 1 — Documentary evidence of sale and relocation (independent):**  
The Cadder Valley Railroad stock book (June 19, 1901) records that Locomotive No. 9 was sold to the Tolliver Lumber Company in Bly County, 200 miles north, and "delivered on her own wheels." The Tolliver shop book (May 1929) records she was "cut up at Sixmile." The engine thus left the county alive and was scrapped 200 miles away. The sound began in January 1912 (11 years after the sale) and continued 42 years. If the engine had derailed into the gorge in 1898, it could not have been in Bly County from 1901–1929.

This reason holds independent of the 1962 survey and requires only documentary evidence in the office records (supplied by Bly County Historical Rooms).

**Reason 2 — Physics of the sound's characteristics (independent):**  
The bridge's sound was felt through floorboards before heard, and transmitted along the rails to the depot a mile away with far greater efficiency than through air. Quarles (document 9) explains this as consistent with sudden strain release in a metal structure, not the mechanical operation of a locomotive. Locomotive exhaust or a bell sound would travel differently. Frayne (document 4) notes the sound "bore no resemblance to a bell, and none whatever to the exhaust of a locomotive." A derailed engine embedded in the creek 14 years prior would not produce a sudden mechanical sound on specific temperature thresholds; metal contraction does.

**Reason 3 — Temperature threshold specificity (independent):**  
The bridge spoke on and only on nights when two specific conditions were jointly met: night low below 6°F and a 30°+ degree fall from the day's high. A locomotive lying in the creek for 14+ years would have no causal mechanism to produce sound selectively on 6°-and-30°-threshold nights. Temperature does affect metal, but the threshold for a derailed locomotive producing audible sound is not a precise six-degree point. The binding point of a bearing re-seated to a two-inch mark at 86°F is exactly at 6°F (as Frayne's calculations show); the threshold is explained by engineering design, not by a locomotive.

**Independence of the three:**  
Reason 1 is documentary/historical (where the engine went). Reason 2 is physical/acoustic (how sound travels and what kind of sound it is). Reason 3 is thermodynamic/mechanical (why a specific threshold). They do not depend on one another.

**Reason that survives without the 1962 survey:**  
Reason 1 (sale and relocation to Bly County) requires only the office records from Bly County (Tolliver ledgers), not the creek bed inspection. If the 1962 survey had never been conducted, the documentary evidence alone establishes No. 9 was 200 miles away by 1901 and scrapped by 1929. The sound beginning in 1912 and continuing for 42 years is incompatible with an engine underwater 14 years.

### D3. Did Adela Rennick's objection about the roller nest have anything to do with her leaving in 1901?

**Answer: No, the objection was not the cause of her leaving.**

**Justification from sources:**

The objection was entered September 12, 1897 (when the span was closed). Adela's departure was April 30, 1901 (nearly four years later). The letter terminating her employment reads: "The engineering party is reduced entire on the 15th May next, and your time is paid to that day. Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card."

Wendell Frayne (document 10) addresses this directly: "There has been a notion going about that she was let go over something she wrote in her book, that she had crossed the engineer and was made to pay for it... I know what gets put next to it, and I will set it down here myself rather than have it sprung on you: the road took two of that party back on in the August following, for the extension work at Ashby, and it did not take her. That is true and it proves nothing."

The two re-engaged (rodman and chainman) were field workers for extension work. Adela was the party's computer—the only one the road ever had. The party was "let go entire," not selectively. The engineer's recommendation appears to be neutral ("Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered," which is standard practice, not punitive language).

Frayne concludes: "The party was let go entire and she was in it. There was nothing else in it."

Thus Adela's 1901 departure was a routine reduction-in-force that affected the entire engineering party, not a reprisal for her 1897 objection. If retaliation had been intended, she would have been dismissed sooner (or the two re-engaged in August 1901 would not have been taken back on). The four-year gap and the language of the termination letter indicate the objection played no role in her leaving.

## Section E — Contradictions (10 points)

### E1. Direct factual contradictions and resolutions

**Contradiction 1: Year Adela was born**

The written records (Wexler and Frayne's contemporaneous analysis) outweigh Judd's later verbal recollection.

**First sound: January 17, 1912.**

## Section F — Theory (10 points)

### F1. Causal theory of the sound

**The Sound:** When the Sallow Creek Viaduct center span cooled below 6 degrees Fahrenheit, a restraint mechanism failed catastrophically, releasing stored structural strain in a single deep mechanical report transmitted along the rails.

**Mechanism and Thermal Threshold:**

The 300-foot center span was pinned at its north end and free to move at the south end via a nest of rollers seated in a cast pedestal. The movement rule was one inch of travel per 40 degrees of temperature change. When the span was closed in September 1897, the nest was set to a two-inch mark (not the three-inch specification), providing 80 degrees of cold-travel margin. At 66 degrees, the span could cool 80 degrees before the nest reached its limit. At that limit—14 degrees below zero—the bearing would bind.

However, in August 1909, the bridge was re-decked with heavier rail (40 tons added load) and both shoes were jacked and re-seated to the same two-inch mark, but with the steel temperature at 86 degrees. This reset the binding point to 6 degrees above zero—100 degrees higher than the original design safety margin. The added dead load likely accelerated the binding by increasing static stress on a mechanism designed for less.

**The Second Condition (30-degree fall):**

The sound only occurred when a night's low fell below 6°F and the day's high had been at least 30 degrees warmer. A rapid temperature fall (not just low temperature alone) was necessary. Judd's analysis explains why: a slow decline allows the bearing to creep incrementally, friction being overcome at low strain, dissipating stress without accumulation. A 30-degree drop in a single day drives the bearing past its freedom faster than it can relieve itself; strain accumulates instead of being spent. Once accumulated strain reaches critical, the bearing releases suddenly and entirely.

**Silent Nights:**

Five nights in Judd's record met both conditions (below 6°F and 30+ fall) but produced no sound. These always occurred within a cold spell where the bearing had already released and the span had already shifted into its new position. The bearing cannot release twice without warming—a daytime thaw above 40°F was required to reset it to its starting point. In successive cold nights within the same freeze, the bearing remains exhausted at the far end of its travel with no energy to release.

**Why the Sound Began in January 1912:**

The sound did not begin after the 1898 derailment (as the valley legend held). Between the opening of the line in November 1897 and August 1909, there were four nights at or below six degrees, but none produced sound. The coldest was 9 degrees below in 1899—short of the 14 degrees below that the two-inch setting required to bind. The 1909 re-decking, with the additional weight and the re-seating at summer heat (86°F), brought the binding point down to exactly 6 degrees. The first night after August 1909 that hit below 6 degrees with a 30-degree fall was January 17, 1912. Thereafter, the conditions were met repeatedly over 43 winters, producing 61 nights of sound.

**Why the Sound Stopped in 1954:**

In March 1954, the railroad replaced the old roller-nest bearings with new rocker bearings capable of four inches of travel in each direction. Four inches = 160 degrees of temperature-change capacity (at the 40-degree rule). The lowest temperature recorded in the valley's weather record was 13 below zero (January 1918). A span closed at 66°F can cool to 66 − 160 = −94°F before exhausting four inches of travel. No temperature in the valley's history approaches that threshold, so the binding condition can never recur. The last sound was February 9, 1954, the last winter the old mechanism was in place. After the new bearings were installed, three nights in the two following winters met both conditions and produced no sound—definitive proof the old bearing was gone.

**Competing Explanation That Fails:**

The valley believed Locomotive No. 9, derailed into the gorge in 1898, lay underwater and produced the sound as it settled or as the dead crew knocked. This explanation fails for several reasons:

1. **Documentary:** The Tolliver Lumber Company's stock book (June 19, 1901) records No. 9 was sold and "delivered on her own wheels." The shop book (May 1929) records her "cut up at Sixmile." The engine left the county alive in 1901.

2. **Empirical:** Ruth Frayne's 1962 survey walked the dry creek bed for 800 feet above and below the viaduct. She recovered no locomotive frame, boiler shell, wheels, rods, cab iron, tender, or discernible hole. Nothing larger than a rail spike was found.

3. **Temporal:** Even if No. 9 had remained in the creek, the sound began in January 1912—14 years after the derailment. No causal mechanism explains why a submerged engine would emit a selective sound on specific temperature thresholds 14 years later. A derailed locomotive has no mechanical component that binds at 6°F and releases on 30-degree falls.

4. **Threshold specificity:** A locomotive lying in water has no reason to produce sound selectively when night lows fall below six degrees and day highs precede them by 30 degrees. A bearing re-seated at 86°F to a two-inch mark does.

**Physical Evidence Recovered:**

The cast-iron south pedestal, recovered 40 feet below the south abutment from the creek bed in September 1962, bears a chisel mark on the roller seat at the two-inch position. This is the only surviving physical evidence of the bearing's erection setting. The mark establishes that the nest was set to two inches (not the three specified), and thus the binding point was calculated at 14 degrees below (1897) or six degrees above (after 1909). The pedestal confirms Adela Rennick's contemporaneous objection: "Sheet 11 calls for three. Reported to W.T., who answers that the pedestal cannot be recast before the fall haul and that two inches is more than the valley will ever ask for." The chisel mark is the material proof of her documented objection and of Harlan Pike's theory (an engineer living in the railroad office at the time) that the overloaded bearing after 1909 was the source. The valley heard not a ghost train but a bridge arrival—one inch late.

## Section G — Summary (5 points)

### G1. Four-story history in 120 words maximum

The Cadder Valley Railroad built a 540-foot viaduct across Sallow Creek (1896–97) to reach the Ashby quarry. During construction, engineer Adela Rennick objected to the roller bearing being set at two inches instead of three; she was overruled. In August 1909, the bridge was re-decked with 40 tons of added weight and the bearing was re-seated at 86°F to the same two-inch mark, lowering its safety threshold to six degrees above zero. Beginning January 1912, on hard-freeze nights following warm days, the valley heard a deep report—the bearing binding and suddenly releasing as the span cooled past its limit. For 43 winters, station agent Judd Rennick documented 61 such nights. The valley blamed a locomotive supposedly drowned in 1898; in truth, the engine had been sold and scrapped 200 miles north in 1929. Only Ruth Frayne's 1962 survey and Judd's precise record revealed the bridge itself was the source—a structural failure, one inch shy, calling out for four decades.
