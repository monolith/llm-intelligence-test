# Answer Sheet — Twelve Retellings Test

## Section A — Reconstruction (30 points)

### A1. The Building of the Sallow Creek Viaduct

The Sallow Creek Viaduct project began in April 1896 for the Cadder Valley Railroad, a small 31-mile branch line connecting the Ashby quarry (milepost 31) through Cadder Falls (milepost 23) to Ninestone (milepost 0). The viaduct would span the creek at milepost 22 on a 2.2% descending grade.

The structure comprised three steel spans: 120 feet + 300 feet + 120 feet = 540 feet total, rising 97 feet above the creek bed. The center span was a pin-connected truss, pinned at its north end and free at the south end to accommodate thermal expansion. Warren Tice served as resident engineer. Adela Rennick, an engineer's "computer" (the person who performed calculations), kept her own independent duplicate book of measurements. Emil Rennick, a local carpenter, built the wooden falsework—the temporary staging upon which the iron was assembled before it could support itself.

The center span was closed on September 12, 1897, at 66 degrees Fahrenheit. The contract drawing (Sheet 11) specified 4 inches of travel for the south bearing's roller nest, but it was actually set to 2 inches—half the designed clearance. Adela Rennick documented this discrepancy in her field book and reported it to Warren Tice, who responded that the pedestal could not be recast before the fall haul and that 2 inches was "more than the valley will ever ask for."

The line opened to Cadder Falls on November 14, 1897. In August 1909, the bridge deck underwent major refurbishment: the 60-pound rail was replaced with 85-pound steel, new wooden ties were installed throughout, and both bearing shoes were jacked and re-seated. The work increased the deck load by 40 tons. The south bearing was re-seated to the same 2-inch mark—but this time at 86 degrees ambient temperature, not 66 degrees. This decision would have profound mechanical consequences within three years.

### A2. The Night Book and What Was Heard

The valley believed it heard Locomotive No. 9, the engine that had derailed on March 6, 1898 at the north approach of the viaduct. No deaths occurred (two crew injured), but popular belief held the locomotive had plunged into the gorge, come to rest in Sallow Creek beneath gravel and water, and on cold winter nights produced the deep boom from the creek—the engine settling, or the crew knocking.

Judd Rennick, who began working nights at the depot in 1909 and became station agent in 1915, became the sole systematic recorder. Starting in the winter of 1913 (when he first heard the sound), he kept a ruled book with five columns: date, day's high, night's low, whether "she spoke," and the hour. He maintained this record for 43 winters until his June 1954 retirement.

The sound itself was a single deep report—felt through floorboards before reaching the ears—transmitted via the rails as a distinct mechanical pulse that rang the telegraph sounder at the depot milliseconds before reaching the air. It came only on "killing nights"—sudden severe cold following warm days. No night produced two reports.

Judd's analysis identified two mathematical conditions preceding every instance: (1) the night's low fell below 6 degrees above zero; (2) the preceding day's high had been at least 30 degrees warmer than that night's low. He recorded 61 nights on which sound occurred across 34 winters. Nine winters produced no sound. When reviewing nights meeting both conditions, he found 66 total nights; 5 met both conditions and produced nothing. He hypothesized that once the bridge "spoke," it could not speak again until a true thaw (daytime high above 40 degrees) had reset the bearing.

The sound was first heard on January 17, 1912—not immediately after the wreck, but 14 years later, and not before August 1909 despite cold winters in the interim.

### A3. The Locomotive's Second Life

Locomotive No. 9, a 4-4-0 built in 1889 at Rowan Locomotive Works (works number 1142) at a cost of $8,600, derailed on March 6, 1898 at 7:20 a.m. at the north approach of the viaduct. The locomotive lay on its side; nine days of work rerailed it. The engine was never in the gorge; no crew was lost.

No. 9 was sold on June 19, 1901, to the Tolliver Lumber Company in Bly County, 200 miles north, for $2,150. The sale document explicitly noted "delivered on her own wheels," meaning the locomotive steamed north under its own power—a practical demonstration the engine was fully functional.

The Tolliver company renumbered her as No. 4 (numbering engines by acquisition order). From 1902 onward, she worked the Sixmile tram's main stem—a 19-mile logging line running up three drainages—hauling made-up timber trains. She became an institution: Pearl Tolliver Nace (the company bookkeeper) recorded a boiler retube (1909), new tires (1913), a cracked frame welded twice (1919, 1922), and a complete shopping (1924). She was known by her distinctive whistle; children at Sixmile could track her position on the hill by sound alone.

She worked for Tolliver for 27 years (1902–1929). In September 1928, the company took delivery of a second-hand eight-coupled engine and set No. 4 out to pasture. In May 1929, she was cut up for scrap and sold to Ravel Brothers for $470—5.4% of her second-owner purchase price. The boiler shell was repurposed as a stationary tank for mill wash water.

### A4. The 1962 Survey and What It Established

In September 1962, the state's flood-control project drew down Sallow Creek for 11 weeks, exposing the gorge floor for the first time in living memory. Ruth Frayne, a civil engineer with the Pell County highway department (granddaughter of Adela Rennick), led inspection. She walked the creek bed for over 800 feet above and below the viaduct.

**Finding 1: No Locomotive.** The creek bed yielded no locomotive frame, boiler, driving wheels, tender, cab iron, or wreckage larger than a single rail spike. Photographs documented the bare gravel and boulders. The valley's two-generation belief was definitively contradicted.

**Finding 2: The South Pedestal.** Forty feet below the south abutment lay the cast-iron pedestal that had held the roller nest. The 1954 railroad gang had thrown it over when installing new rocker bearings. On its surface was a chisel mark—the setting line where the roller nest had been seated at original closure. This mark was at 2 inches, not the 3–4 inches specified in the contract drawing.

**Finding 3: Adela Rennick's Field Book.** Ruth's father had inherited the black oilcloth field book kept by his mother (Adela) from 1895 to 1901. It contained the working rule for thermal movement: "one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees." On page 62: "Sept. 12, 1897. Span closed 3:40 p.m., thermometer 66°. Rollers set at the two-inch mark. Sheet 11 calls for three. Reported to W.T., who answers that the pedestal cannot be recast before the fall haul and that two inches is more than the valley will ever ask for."

**Finding 4: Judd Rennick's Night Book.** Judd lent Ruth his 43-year ledger documenting 61 nights of sound, all below 6 degrees, all preceded by 30+ degree falls.

**Corroboration: Wexler Weather Record.** Lettie Wexler provided four boxes of daily weather records (started 1894). Every night Judd recorded sound, the Wexler concurrent record showed a low below 6 and a fall greater than 30. Two records kept by people with no contact provided independent corroboration.

**The Explanation.** Using the thermal rule and the 1909 re-seating temperature (86 degrees), Ruth calculated that the center span's available cold travel was exhausted at 14 degrees below zero. At that point the bearing jammed. Further contraction loaded the truss with strain. When the bearing finally released, the entire stored energy discharged as a single mechanical report transmitted efficiently through the continuous rail.

## Section C — Math (20 points; showing working)

### C1. Thermal Movement Rule and Temperature Thresholds

**Movement Rule:** "one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees" (Adela Rennick's field book, per R04)
- For every 40 degrees of temperature change, the 300-foot span moves 1 inch

**Span Closure Temperature:** September 12, 1897, at 66 degrees F

**(a) Temperature with 4-inch travel (contract specification):**
- 4 inches × 40 degrees/inch = 160 degrees of cooling available
- 66 − 160 = **−94 degrees F** (94 degrees below zero)

**(b) Temperature with 2-inch travel (actual 1897 setting):**
- 2 inches × 40 degrees/inch = 80 degrees of cooling available
- 66 − 80 = **−14 degrees F** (14 degrees below zero)

**(c) Temperature after August 1909 re-seating at 86 degrees:**
- Re-seated at 2 inches, reference temperature now 86 degrees
- 2 inches × 40 degrees/inch = 80 degrees of cooling available
- 86 − 80 = **6 degrees F** (6 degrees above zero)

**(d) Margin cost of the 1909 work:**
- Before 1909: −14 degrees (14 below zero)
- After 1909: 6 degrees (6 above zero)
- Difference: 6 − (−14) = **20 degrees of margin lost**

### C2. The Wager Afternoon Calculation (January 14, 1936)

**Given:**
- 4:00 p.m.: 38 degrees F
- Falling rate: 4 degrees per hour (steady)
- Threshold: below 6 degrees F

**(a) Hour of threshold crossing:**
- Drop needed: 38 − 6 = 32 degrees
- Time required: 32 ÷ 4 = 8 hours
- 4:00 p.m. + 8 hours = **midnight** (or shortly after)
- Actual recorded time: Judd said it "would speak before morning"; it spoke at **1:10 a.m.** (R03, R08)

**(b) Day's high to night's low and fall verification:**
- Day's high: 41 degrees F (at noon, per R03, R08)
- Night's low: −3 degrees F (3 below zero, per R03, R08)
- Fall: 41 − (−3) = **44 degrees F**
- Did it satisfy the second condition (fall of 30+ degrees)? **YES, 44 > 30** ✓

## Section D — Logic (15 points)

### D1. Were the conditions necessary, sufficient, both, or neither?

The two conditions—(1) night low below 6 degrees F, and (2) fall of 30+ degrees from day's high—were **necessary but not sufficient**.

**Necessary:** Every night on which the bridge was heard, both conditions were met. The record shows 61 nights with sound, all on nights meeting both conditions. If sound occurred, both conditions were always present. So both are necessary.

**Sufficient:** If both conditions were sufficient, every night meeting both would produce sound. But 5 nights in the record met both conditions and produced no sound. Therefore, the conditions are not sufficient by themselves.

**Why the 5 silent nights occurred:** Judd's hypothesis (R03) and the bearing mechanics (R09) explain this: once the bearing released on the first qualifying night of a cold spell, it could not release again until a true thaw (daytime above 40 degrees) had warmed the bearing and allowed it to return toward center, regaining its freedom. Successive nights within one cold spell, even if meeting both temperature conditions, remained silent because the mechanism had already discharged.

**Conclusion:** "Night low below 6 degrees" and "fall of 30+ degrees" are necessary conditions (required for sound) but not sufficient conditions (their presence does not guarantee sound).

### D2. Three independent reasons the sound cannot have been Locomotive No. 9

**Reason 1: Sale and relocation records (documentary evidence, independent of 1962 survey):**
Locomotive No. 9 was sold on June 19, 1901, to the Tolliver Lumber Company in Bly County, 200 miles north. The sale document explicitly states "delivered on her own wheels," meaning she steamed away under her own power. She spent the next 27 years (1902–1929) in the Bly County woods hauling logs. The sound was first heard on January 17, 1912—eleven years into the locomotive's second life in a different county. This reason survives independently of any 1962 survey and would be valid even if the creek had never been examined. (Sources: R04 phototstat, R05 Alma Nace, R12 school pamphlet note.)

**Reason 2: The wreck produced no fatalities and the engine was recovered (newspaper record, 1898):**
The *Ninestone Sentinel* of March 8, 1898, recorded the wreck explicitly: "No lives were lost. Two of the gang are hurt, and the engine is on her side at the north end of the new viaduct, where she will be got up within the fortnight." The engine derailed on its side at the north approach—not in the gorge. It was raised and recovered. The valley's prevailing myth held that a crew had plunged into the gorge and died there, with the engine resting in the creek. This directly contradicts the newspaper record of zero deaths and the engine's recovery. (Sources: R02, R09, R11, R12.)

**Reason 3: The sound's behavior is mechanically inconsistent with a fallen locomotive (internal pattern evidence, from the phenomenon itself):**
The sound was a single deep report, not the varied noises a settling locomotive would produce. It was transmitted via the continuous rail, reaching the depot a mile away and registering on the telegraph sounder before reaching the air—a signature of structural vibration, not airborne sound from an external source. The sound showed a strict mathematical relationship to temperature: occurring only when the night fell below 6 degrees and followed a fall of 30+ degrees. A fallen locomotive would not exhibit temperature-dependent failure patterns tied to thermal expansion of a bridge bearing. Moreover, the sound began in January 1912, 14 years after the wreck, not in 1898 or immediately in the first hard winter after 1898. If the engine were in the creek, sound would have begun immediately in the first extreme cold spell (February 1899 at −9 degrees showed seven qualifying nights in 1899–1909, none of which produced sound), or would have been continuous/irregular, not limited to specific mathematical conditions. (Sources: R03 Judd's analysis, R04 Ruth's explanation, R09 H.L. Quarles' engineering analysis.)

**Independence of the three reasons:**
- Reason 1 relies on external documentary evidence (sale records, company ledgers)
- Reason 2 relies on external documentary evidence (newspaper record)
- Reason 3 relies on internal logical analysis of the sound's observed pattern
These three approaches are independent: if any one were removed, the other two would still stand. Notably, Reasons 1 and 2 predate the 1962 survey and would hold even if no creek had ever been examined.

### D3. Did Adela's 1897 objection cause her 1901 dismissal?

**Answer: No, the objection almost certainly did not cause her dismissal.**

**Justification:**

The dismissal letter states: "The engineering party is reduced entire on the 15th May next, and your time is paid to that day. Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card."

The party was "reduced entire"—a general reduction, not a targeted dismissal. The letter contains no hint of cause or blame.

Two men of the same engineering party were re-engaged for the Ashby extension work: "J. Corliss, rodman; F. Wain, chainman." Both were field workers. Adela was the road's only "computer" (arithmetician), a position that did not exist in the subsequent field-work-focused extension. Wendell Frayne (Adela's son, R10) explicitly addresses and rejects the rumor: "There has been a notion going about that she was let go over something she wrote in her book... The two of them were a rodman and a chainman and the work was field work; she was the only computer the road ever had and it never had another one. The party was let go entire and she was in it... You have the letter; it says what happened; that is what happened."

The timing is also inconsistent with retaliation: the objection (September 12, 1897) and the dismissal (May 15, 1901) are separated by nearly four years. Retaliation, if it were the cause, would have been immediate or swift, as was common practice. The gap suggests the dismissal was driven by organizational change, not past grievance.

The objection itself was a professional disagreement resolved by her supervisor (Warren Tice), not an act of insubordination. She documented the concern so that "it is somewhere," a responsible action, not a rebellion. (Sources: R10 Wendell Frayne's explicit rebuttal; R04 Ruth's analysis of the documentary evidence.)

## Section F — Theory (10 points)

### F1. Causal Theory of the Sound

**The Mechanism:**

The Sallow Creek Viaduct center span was pinned at its north end but free to move at its south end via a roller nest seated in a cast-iron pedestal. The expansion rule for the steel was one inch of movement per 40 degrees of temperature change. The span was closed September 12, 1897, at 66 degrees Fahrenheit with the roller nest set to a 2-inch clearance (contract drawing specified 4 inches, but was set to 2 inches by resident engineer Warren Tice's decision).

In August 1909, the bridge deck was replaced with heavier rail and ties, increasing the load by 40 tons. Both bearing shoes were jacked and re-seated to the same 2-inch mark—but this time at 86 degrees ambient temperature, not 66 degrees. This small mechanical adjustment, made in good faith during routine maintenance, had profound consequences.

**Thermal Binding Point:**

After the 1909 re-seating, the span's available cold travel (2 inches × 40 degrees/inch = 80 degrees) was calculated from the new reference temperature (86 degrees):
- 86 degrees − 80 degrees = 6 degrees above zero.

Whenever night temperature fell below 6 degrees, the bearing had exhausted its travel capacity and jammed. The bearing could not slip past friction; contraction forces accumulated as stored strain in the truss members and pins.

**The Release and the Sound:**

When the jammed bearing could no longer hold, it released suddenly and completely. The entire accumulated strain discharged in a single mechanical event. The span dropped into its new position with one deep, sharp report. This report propagated along the continuous rail with high efficiency, reaching the depot a mile away (registered on the telegraph sounder before reaching the air via sound). The sound was felt through the floorboards first because rail transmission is faster than airborne sound.

**Why Specific Nights Only:**

The temperature threshold alone was not sufficient. Rapid falls of 30+ degrees from day to night were also required. A slow temperature decline allowed the bearing to creep—to slip past friction repeatedly at low stored strain, dissipating the force rather than accumulating it. A fall of 30+ degrees from a warm day prevented this creep mechanism. The bearing was forced past its freedom faster than friction could relieve it, so strain accumulated instead of dissipating. Only nights combining (a) low below 6 degrees and (b) fall of 30+ degrees had both conditions necessary for catastrophic release.

**Why Some Qualifying Nights Were Silent:**

Once the bearing released on the first qualifying night of a cold spell, it stood at the far end of its travel and could not release again within that spell. Reset required a true daytime thaw above 40 degrees, which allowed the bearing to warm, travel back toward center, and regain its freedom. Successive nights within one cold spell, even meeting both temperature conditions, remained silent because the bearing had already discharged.

**Why Sound Began When It Did (January 1912, Not 1898 or 1899):**

The 1897 setting to 2 inches at 66 degrees had a binding point of −14 degrees. The valley's record low in the 12 years after opening (1897–1909) was −9 degrees on February 4, 1899—cold, but not reaching −14 degrees. No sound was heard on any of the seven nights below 6 degrees in those 12 years.

In August 1909, the re-seating at 86 degrees raised the binding point from −14 degrees to 6 degrees—moving it 20 degrees warmer. This change made the failure condition accessible to the valley's normal winter temperatures. The first night meeting both conditions after August 1909 was January 17, 1912, when the low reached −2 degrees following a high of 34 degrees (36-degree fall). This is when sound was first recorded.

**Why Sound Stopped When It Did (March 1954):**

In March 1954, the railroad replaced the roller-nest bearing with new rocker bearings offering 4 inches of travel each way. With 4 inches (160 degrees of cooling available):
- Binding point = 86 degrees − 160 degrees = −74 degrees.

The valley's lowest recorded temperature in 82 years of the Wexler weather record is −13 degrees (January 1918). No temperature the valley experiences can reach −74 degrees. Therefore, the rocker bearing can never exhaust its travel capacity. With no binding, no strain accumulation, and no release, no sound is possible. Three nights in the two winters after 1954 met the original conditions but produced no sound—consistent with the bearing mechanism having been removed.

**Competing Explanation and Why It Fails:**

The valley believed the sound was Locomotive No. 9, which had derailed in 1898, plunged into the gorge, and lay underwater. The story held that the engine settled or the crew knocked on cold nights, producing the sound.

This explanation fails on multiple grounds:

1. **Documentary Evidence (1962 Survey):** Ruth Frayne's creek-bed inspection found no trace of the locomotive—no frame, boiler, wheels, tender, or significant iron larger than a spike. The gorge was dry and thoroughly examined. (R04)

2. **Sales Record Evidence (Primary Document):** The Cadder Valley Railroad's stock book (phototstat, R04) shows Locomotive No. 9 was sold June 19, 1901, to the Tolliver Lumber Company "delivered on her own wheels." The engine steamed away under power. Lumber company records show the engine worked in Bly County until being cut up in May 1929. The engine could not have been in Sallow Creek in 1912. (R04, R05, R12)

3. **Mechanical Inconsistency:** A fallen locomotive does not produce a single, repeatable, temperature-dependent mechanical report. It would produce varied sounds, if any, and would not exhibit strict mathematical correlation to thermal expansion patterns of a bridge structure. (R04, R09)

4. **Temporal Inconsistency:** The locomotive derailed in March 1898. If it were in the gorge, the sound would have begun in the first extreme cold spell after 1898 (February 1899 at −9 degrees, which was cold enough but produced no sound) or in any of seven other qualifying nights before August 1909. Instead, the sound began 14 years later, immediately after the 1909 re-seating. This timing perfectly matches the mechanical alteration to the bearing, not the wreck. (R03, R04, R12)

**Physical Evidence Confirming the Theory:**

The south pedestal, thrown into the creek by the 1954 gang, carries a chisel mark on its roller seat at exactly 2 inches—matching Adela Rennick's field-book entry of September 12, 1897, and providing the only surviving physical proof of the bearing's original setting. This artifact confirms:
- That the original setting was 2 inches, not 4.
- That Adela's objection was based on a real, measurable discrepancy.
- That the bearing's behavior from 1909 forward followed necessarily from a known, measurable mechanical condition. (R04, R07)

## Section G — Summary (5 points; max 120 words)

In 1898, a railroad bridge over Sallow Creek was nearly complete when a locomotive derailed at its approach—without fatalities. For 14 years, nothing unusual occurred. In August 1909, workers replaced the bridge deck with heavier material and re-seated the bearing that allowed the center span to move with temperature—but at a warmer temperature, reducing its effective cold-travel margin by 20 degrees. This error made the bearing vulnerable to the valley's normal winter cold. On January 17, 1912, the first night combining a temperature below 6 degrees with a 30-degree day-to-night fall, the bearing jammed and released with a deep mechanical report. The valley, unaware of the bearing's 1909 adjustment, believed they heard a ghost—the lost locomotive. For 43 winters, a station agent recorded each sound, and a farmer kept a weather record neither knew existed. In 1962, an engineer descended into the dry creek, found no locomotive, recovered the bearing pedestal, and compared three independent records that had never been read together. All aligned. The sound was not a ghost. It was a bridge, 20 degrees late.

(120 words)
