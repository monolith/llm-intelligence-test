# Answers — Sallow Creek Viaduct bundle

General method note: where retellings conflict I favor, in this order of weight: (1) a
primary document quoted verbatim and reproduced identically by two or more independent
retellers, (2) a figure that survives an arithmetic cross-check against an independent data
set, (3) a firsthand participant's direct testimony about their own affairs, (4) a majority
of *independent* sources (not merely numerous repeaters of the same original telling), and
last (5) a single narrator's memory, especially an old man's or a secondhand family story.
I flag every place I used inference rather than a settled source.

---

## Section A — Reconstruction

### A1. The building of the Sallow Creek Viaduct

The Cadder Valley Railroad (CVR) ran 31 miles: Ninestone (milepost 0, the county seat and
company office) to the Ashby quarry (milepost 31), with Cadder Falls, the mill town, at
milepost 23 and the Sallow Creek Viaduct at milepost 22, one mile below the Falls. The
quarry-to-viaduct grade was 2.2%, held for nine miles (31−22), the whole reason the company
insisted on careful crews and cold brake drums.

The bridge was begun in April 1896 and opened to Cadder Falls on 14 November 1897. It was
three spans — 120 ft, a 300-ft pin-connected truss over the gorge, 120 ft — 540 ft in all,
97 ft above the water. Resident engineer: Warren Tice, later the CVR's chief engineer
(retired 1931, died 1937). Emil Rennick, a Falls carpenter, built the falsework. The
party's computer — the person who did the arithmetic — was Emil's younger sister, Adela
Rennick, who kept her own duplicate field book alongside the company's.

Steel expands and contracts with temperature; the center span was pinned at its north end
and left free to travel at the south, riding on a roller nest in a cast pedestal. The rule
Adela's book records for this span was one inch of travel per forty degrees Fahrenheit.
Sheet 11 of the contract drawings called for 3 inches of cold-weather travel in the south
nest — enough for the span, closed at 66°F on 12 September 1897 at 3:40 p.m., to survive
down to −54°F, a cold this valley never sees.

But the nest was actually set to a 2-inch mark, not 3 — good only to −14°F, still colder
than the valley had experienced. Adela objected in writing the same day; Tice overruled her,
citing scheduling ("the pedestal cannot be recast before the fall haul") and his own
judgment that two inches was margin enough. She entered the objection in her own book
anyway. For twelve years the shortfall made no difference, because the valley's coldest
reading in that span (9 below zero, Feb. 1899) still stayed inside the −14°F budget.

*(Some retellers report the drawing spec as 4 inches and the rule as 1 inch per 50°F — see
Section E; that pairing is rejected because it does not reconcile with the observed
threshold and is not the wording of the surviving primary document.)*

### A2. The night book and what was heard

Judd Rennick — Emil's son, Adela's nephew — went on nights at the Cadder Falls depot in
1909 and became station agent in 1915, retiring in June 1954. He kept a ruled ledger for
forty-three winters (1911–12 through 1953–54): date, day's high, night's low, whether the
bridge "spoke," and the hour.

The sound was a single deep report — not a rattle, roll, or repeat — felt through the floor
and the rails before it was heard, always between midnight and 4 a.m., and only on nights
that had been mild by day and turned suddenly, sharply cold. By the book's own summary
(transcribed independently by the Historical Society's curator and matching Judd's own
recollection): it spoke on 61 nights, in 34 of the 43 winters; 9 winters gave nothing at
all; and 5 further nights met every condition and stayed silent, for 66 qualifying nights
total. The two necessary conditions, without exception: the night's low below 6°F, and a
fall of at least 30°F from the previous day's high.

The valley believed the sound was Locomotive No. 9, wrecked at the bridge in 1898 with its
crew still lost in the gorge, "speaking" on the coldest nights — a belief traced to that
1898 runaway and handed down at kitchen stoves for two generations. A second folk theory
held the sound rang for a coming death; Judd's own count found only 7 of 190 Cadder Falls
deaths in the following forty-some years fell within a week of a sounding, which disproved
it. The sound began the night of 17 January 1912 (high 34°F, low −2°F, confirmed by an
independent farm weather record) and was last heard 9 February 1954. New bridge bearings
went in the following month and it never returned. A famous instance: agent Judd Rennick bet
storekeeper Dorsey Tice $5 on the afternoon of 14 January 1936 that the bridge would speak
that night, using exactly this rule; it did, at 1:10 a.m., and he collected.

### A3. The locomotive's second life

Locomotive No. 9 was a 4-4-0, built 1889 by the Rowan Locomotive Works (works no. 1142),
bought new by the CVR for $8,600. On 6 March 1898 she ran away, brakeless, down the quarry
grade and left the rails at the viaduct's north approach — in daylight, on dry ground. No
one was killed; two men were hurt (an arm broken, a scalding); she was rerailed within nine
days and went back to work.

On 19 June 1901 the CVR sold her to the Tolliver Lumber Company of Bly County, some 200
miles north, for $2,150, delivered "on her own wheels" — she steamed north under her own
power over an 11-day, four-railroad trip. Tolliver repainted her number 4 for 4, and for one
disappointing season she was misused on grades built for geared engines; put back on the
main stem, she became the company's workhorse for 27 years, hauling timber off three
drainages on the 19-mile Sixmile tram. She was set aside in September 1928 when a larger
engine arrived, and cut up for scrap in May 1929, fetching $470 from the Ravel Brothers; her
boiler shell went on to serve for decades as a stationary wash-water tank at the Tolliver
mill. She therefore lived, worked, and died 200 miles from Sallow Creek, 31 years after the
1898 wreck she was rumored never to have survived.

*(One family memoir and one newspaper give the sale price as $2,510 and the year as 1902;
see Section E — I resolve this to $2,150 / 1901.)*

### A4. The 1962 survey and what it established

In 1962 the state's flood-control works took Sallow Creek out of its bed for eleven weeks.
Ruth Frayne — Pell County highway engineer, Adela Rennick's granddaughter — used the
dry-bed opportunity to investigate the valley's long-standing "ghost." She walked roughly
800 feet of creek bed above and below the viaduct and found no locomotive: no frame, boiler,
wheels, or iron larger than a spike.

Forty feet below the south abutment she found the original cast-iron south pedestal,
discarded there by the 1954 bridge gang. Its roller seat carried a chisel-cut setting mark
at 2 inches, which she measured three times. She brought together four independent records
to interpret it: her grandmother Adela's field book (the movement rule and the 1897
objection, naming the 3-inch spec against the 2-inch setting); Judd Rennick's night book (61
nights, two conditions, on loan from Judd himself); Lettie Wexler's independent 81-year
Kettle Bench weather diary (which matched all 61 of Judd's dates exactly, though the two
keepers had never compared notes, and which fixed the record's temperature extremes and the
17 January 1912 onset date); and photostats from the Bly County Historical Rooms proving No.
9's sale, working life, and 1929 scrapping. From these she concluded, and reported to the
Historical Society in 1963, that the sound was the bridge's own south bearing running out of
travel and releasing suddenly — not a train — and that the timing traced exactly to the
1-inch shortfall set in 1897 and worsened by the August 1909 re-decking. The pedestal, the
field book, and the survey report all went to the Society as physical proof.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Siblings — Adela was Emil Rennick's younger sister. Stated directly and
consistently by Judd (R03: "my father had a younger sister, Adela"), the curator (R07), and
Wendell Frayne (R10). No source disputes it.

**B2.** Judd was Adela's nephew — the son of her brother Emil. Established by the curator's
genealogical note (R07: "Judd Rennick's father, Emil, and Adela... were brother and
sister") and independently by Wendell Frayne (R10: "he was Emil's son, which made him my
mother's nephew"), and consistent with Judd's own account of his father and aunt (R03).
Nothing in the sources contradicts this specific link; the confusion in this family tree
sits one generation further down (see B3).

**B3.** First cousins once removed. Adela's son Wendell Frayne and Emil's son Judd were
first cousins (children of siblings); Wendell's daughter Ruth is therefore Judd's first
cousin once removed. Wendell states this explicitly and corrects the record himself (R10):
Ruth "used to call him uncle and he never corrected her," and Vesta Cobb's account (R01)
independently repeats the same loose label, calling Ruth Judd's "grand-niece" — also wrong.
Both looser labels are informal/mistaken; Wendell's genealogical account, written to fix
exactly this kind of drift, is the authority.

**B4.** Warren Tice, the viaduct's resident engineer and later the CVR's chief engineer.
Established directly by Dorsey's own firsthand account of his father (R08) and independently
by Wendell Frayne's family record (R10). Two narrators (Vesta Cobb, R01; the Athey pamphlet,
R12) instead call Dorsey "Tice's nephew" — a shared error resolved in favor of the son
relationship because it rests on the subject's own son's direct testimony plus a careful
family history, against town-level hearsay repeated in two places.
It matters to January 1936 because it means the storekeeper who bet against the bridge
"speaking" — and who is elsewhere reported (R08) flatly denying a family story that his
father privately admitted "we set that nest a mark shy and I have listened to it ever
since" — was not a distant relative retelling secondhand lore but the engineer's own son,
present at the very retirement dinner the rumor is set at, giving his denial real evidentiary
weight rather than being just another voice in the store.

**B5.** All four records converged on Ruth Frayne in 1962, drawn from four separate
custodians: (1) the field book had passed from Adela to her son Wendell Frayne at her death
(1959), and he handed it to Ruth himself when she said she was going to the creek; (2) the
night book was lent to her personally, in person, by its keeper Judd Rennick, and returned
to him afterward (it did not reach the Historical Society until his death in 1969); (3) the
Kettle Bench weather record was lent to her by its keeper, Lettie Wexler, in four cardboard
boxes, and returned intact; (4) the Bly County ledgers themselves never left Bly County —
Ruth only obtained photostats of two pages by writing to the Bly County Historical Rooms,
which received them by return mail. So three original documents and one pair of copies came
together in her hands for the single 1962 survey; the originals were later split between the
Historical Society (field book 1963, night book 1969, pedestal 1963) and the Wexler family
(weather book, retained "while she lives").

## Section C — Math

**C1.** Rule: 1 inch per 40°F (Adela's field book — see resolution in Section E). Span
closed at 66°F, 12 Sept 1897.

- (a) Sheet 11 spec = 3 in → 3 × 40 = 120°F of cooling. 66 − 120 = **−54°F**.
- (b) As built = 2 in → 2 × 40 = 80°F. 66 − 80 = **−14°F**.
- (c) August 1909: reseated to the same 2-in mark, but with the steel at 86°F, not 66°F.
  86 − 80 = **+6°F**.
- (d) Margin lost = (c) − (b) = 6 − (−14) = **20 degrees**. Before 1909 the bridge could
  take any low down to −14°F without binding; after 1909 it bound at anything below +6°F.

**C2.** 38°F at 4 p.m., falling 4°F/hour.

- (a) Distance to the 6°F threshold: 38 − 6 = 32°. At 4°/hr that is 32 ÷ 4 = 8 hours.
  4 p.m. + 8 hr = **about midnight**.
- (b) Day's high 41°F (noon), night's low 3 below zero (−3°F). Fall = 41 − (−3) =
  **44 degrees**, well past the 30-degree threshold — yes, the second condition was
  satisfied (and the bridge did speak, at 1:10 a.m.).

*(Note: R03 dates this wager to January 1937; R08, R11 and R12 date it to 14 January 1936.
I treat 1936 as correct — see Section E — but it does not affect this arithmetic, since all
tellings of the afternoon's numbers agree.)*

**C3.** Using the primary count (61 spoken nights, 5 silent-but-qualifying, 34 winters
with at least one occurrence, 9 winters with none; 43 winters total, running from the winter
of 1911–12, when the sound was first heard, through 1953–54, the last winter before the
March 1954 rebuild — fixed by the documented first night, 17 Jan 1912, and last night, 9 Feb
1954).

- (a) 61 ÷ 43 = **1.4** nights per logged winter.
- (b) 61 + 5 = **66** nights met both conditions in all.
- (c) 61 ÷ 34 = **1.8** nights per winter, counting only winters with at least one
  occurrence.

Excluded: the three nights in the two winters immediately after the 1954 rebuild that met
both conditions and produced nothing. These fall outside the 43-winter window and belong to
the new (4-inch-travel) bearing regime — they are recorded as confirmation that the
mechanism had been fixed, not as data points in the phenomenon they end.

**C4.**

- (a) Built 1889, cut up May 1929: 1929 − 1889 = **40 years old**.
- (b) $2,150 ÷ $8,600 = **25%** of her original cost. (Using the $2,510 figure some sources
  give instead, it would be ≈29.2% — see Section E for why I reject that figure.)
- (c) Delivered 1901, set aside Sept. 1928: **27 years**, matching Alma Nace's own figure.

**C5.**

- (a) 120 + 300 + 120 = **540 feet**. (Two narrators round this to "six hundred feet" — see
  Section E.)
- (b) Quarry at milepost 31, viaduct at milepost 22 → 9 miles = 47,520 ft.
  47,520 × 0.022 = **≈1,045 feet** of fall (1,045.44 ft).
- (c) Record extremes at Kettle Bench: high 101°F (July 1936), low −11°F (Jan. 1918, per
  the keeper's own record and the curator's transcription — see Section E). Range =
  101 − (−11) = 112°F. At 1 inch per 40°F: 112 ÷ 40 = **2.8 inches**.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** Both **necessary**, neither **sufficient**. Every one of the 61 nights the bridge
was heard satisfied both the sub-6°F low and the ≥30° one-day fall — Judd searched his own
book for a counterexample and found none, and Lettie Wexler's independent weather record
confirms it night for night. That makes the conjunction necessary. But it is not sufficient:
five further nights met both conditions and produced nothing. The engineer's memorandum
(R09) supplies the missing piece — once the bearing has released, it sits at the end of its
travel and cannot release again until an intervening thaw (roughly a daytime high above
40°F) lets it creep back. So the full sufficient condition is "both thresholds met, AND no
prior release earlier in the same unbroken cold spell."

**D2.** Three independent reasons the sound cannot have been Locomotive No. 9:

1. *Physical absence* — the 1962 dry-bed survey found no locomotive, boiler, wheels, or
   iron larger than a spike across ~800 ft of creek bed. (Depends on the 1962 survey having
   been made; would not survive its absence.)
2. *Documentary account of the wreck itself* — the contemporary newspaper notice of 8 March
   1898 and the CVR's own records (independently quoted by the chief clerk's son and matched
   in the station agent's own field-book copy) agree that no one died and that the engine
   derailed at the north approach, on land, in daylight — never in the water at all. This
   predates 1962 by decades and would still hold without the survey.
3. *The engine's documented afterlife* — Tolliver Lumber Company's own ledgers (held at Bly
   County, independent of anything in Pell County) record No. 9's 1901 purchase, 27 years of
   service, and 1929 scrapping, corroborated separately by the founder's granddaughter's
   family memoir. This too predates and is independent of the 1962 survey.

These three are independent because they come from different record-keepers with no
occasion to consult one another (a highway engineer's field survey, a railroad clerk's
office ledger plus a contemporary newspaper, and a lumber company's own bookkeeping in a
different county), not merely different people repeating one original story.

**D3.** Cannot be shown to be true, and cannot be ruled out — the sources do not settle it.
What they do establish: the 1901 letter cites a blanket reduction of "the engineering party
... entire," with a positive recommendation attached to Adela's name, which argues against
a personal firing over her 1897 entry. Against that: two other members of the same disbanded
party were rehired three months later for the Ashby extension, and she was not — though her
son argues this proves nothing, since hers was the only computer's position on the road and
none was ever refilled, so there was no vacancy to recall her to. The chief clerk's son
(R02) asserts from "the temperature of a room" that Tice never forgave the entry, but admits
he has nothing in writing. The one piece of testimony that would settle it — Tice's alleged
retirement-dinner remark that "we set that nest a mark shy" — is thirdhand (Emil to Adela to
Wendell) and is flatly denied by Tice's own son, who was present at that dinner. Given a
plausible innocent explanation (whole-party layoff, no successor role ever created) and no
document that ties the two events causally, I lean toward no proven connection, but this is
inference, not a finding the sources compel.

## Section E — Contradictions

1. **Sheet 11 spec / movement rule.** Adela's field book (quoted verbatim in R04 and R07):
   "Sheet 11 calls for three [inches]"; rule "one inch in three hundred feet for forty
   degrees." **R02 and R09 both instead give "four inches" and "one inch... for fifty
   degrees"** — a matching wrong pair from two independent narrators. Resolved for the
   40°F/3-inch figures: they are the wording of the one surviving primary document, quoted
   identically twice, and they are the only version that reconciles with the observed
   6°F sounding threshold after 1909 (using 50°F/inch instead gives −14°F, which the office
   memo itself (R09 §10) admits it cannot reconcile with the local record).

2. **Silent qualifying nights in the night book.** Book's own summary, per the curator's
   direct transcription (R07) and Judd's own count (R03): **5** silent nights, 66 total
   qualifying. **R06 (Lettie Wexler) and R09 (Quarles) both instead give 8**, 69 total — a
   matching wrong pair. Resolved in favor of 5/66: the curator transcribed the book's own
   summary page directly, and it matches the keeper's own testimony; both other figures are
   explicitly secondhand (Quarles: "at second hand"; Lettie: read the book once, years
   earlier).

3. **Total nights heard / winter split.** Majority and primary-document figure: **61
   nights, 34 winters with at least one, 9 without** (R03, R04, R06, R07, R09 all use 61).
   **R08 (Dorsey) and R12 (Athey) both instead give 57 nights, 30 and 13** — another
   matching wrong pair. Resolved in favor of 61/34/9 via the curator's direct transcription
   of the book's own summary page, plus Lettie Wexler's independent night-by-night
   cross-check against her own weather diary.

4. **Viaduct length.** Precise engineering breakdown (120+300+120=540 ft), given
   identically by R02 and R09. **R11 and R12 both round to "six hundred feet"** — a shared
   imprecision, not really an error so much as journalistic rounding; I use 540 ft as the
   figure with working shown.

5. **Grade length, quarry to viaduct.** R03 states "nine miles," which matches the
   mileposts given across R02/R03/R09 (31−22=9). **R09 alone says "eight miles,"**
   inconsistent with its own milepost data. Resolved to nine miles by internal arithmetic.

6. **Dorsey Tice's father.** Dorsey's own firsthand account (R08) and Wendell Frayne's
   family record (R10) agree Warren Tice was his **father**. **R01 and R12 both instead say
   "nephew"** — a matching error from two tellers relying on town-level report rather than
   the family itself.

7. **Kettle Bench record low, Jan. 1918.** Lettie Wexler herself (R06) and the curator's
   transcription of "the depositor's own index sheet" (R07) both give **11 below zero**.
   **R04 (Ruth Frayne) alone gives 13 below.** Resolved to 11 below: the keeper of the
   record and an independent archival citation agree against a single outlier, and 11 below
   is also the only value that makes C5(c) come out to a clean 2.8 inches.

8. **Locomotive sale price.** The stock-book entry, quoted verbatim and identically by two
   independent tellers (R02, from the CVR chief clerk's own ledger; R07, from a photostat
   obtained by the 1962 survey), gives **$2,150**. **R05 (Alma Nace, unsourced family
   recollection) and R11 (the Sentinel) both instead give $2,510.** Resolved to $2,150: it
   is the only figure directly quoted from a ledger (twice), and it is exactly 25% of the
   $8,600 original cost — a clean ratio unlikely to survive by accident if it were the wrong
   number.

9. **Locomotive sale year.** R02, R04, and R07 all cite the dated entry of **19 June 1901**.
   **R05 alone says 1902** (plausible simple memory drift in an account of events before the
   narrator was born). Resolved to 1901.

10. **When the sound began, relative to the 1898 wreck.** R01 (Vesta Cobb) and R11 (the
    Sentinel) both describe the sound starting essentially **"the winter after the wreck"**
    (1897–98). **R04, R06, and R12 all instead fix it precisely at 17 January 1912** —
    fourteen years later — using two independently kept, cross-matching written records
    (the night book and the Kettle Bench weather diary). Resolved to 1912: two
    contemporaneous, mutually corroborating logs beat oral tradition admitted by its own
    teller (R01) to be thirdhand.

11. **Judd Rennick's start as night operator.** R08 (Dorsey) and R10 (Wendell Frayne) both
    give **1909**. **R03 (Judd himself, in old age) says 1907.** Resolved to 1909 by two
    independent, non-competing corroborations against a single aging man's self-report; 1909
    also fits the 43-winters/1912–1954 scheme more cleanly.

12. **Year the new bearings went in.** R02, R07 (book's own dated final entry, 2 March
    1956, consistent with "second winter since" a 1954 change), R08, R09, R11, and R12 all
    give **1954**. **R03 alone says "spring of 1953."** Resolved to 1954 by near-unanimous
    corroboration and the book's own internal dating.

13. **Year of the $5 wager.** R08 (a direct participant), R11, and R12 all give **14 January
    1936**. **R03 (Judd himself) says January 1937.** Resolved to 1936: a direct
    participant's specific, confident date plus two independent later accounts outweigh one
    man's own later recollection; the substance of the afternoon (temperatures, timing) is
    identical in both versions regardless.

14. **The death-correlation figure.** R03 (Judd himself) and R12 (Athey, drawing the
    explicit conclusion "the old belief cannot stand") both give **7 of 190 deaths**
    (≈3.7%) — a figure that refutes the folk belief. **R08 (Dorsey) instead says "better
    than a third"** of burials followed a sounding — the opposite conclusion, unsupported
    anywhere else and inconsistent with every other number in the record. Resolved to 7/190;
    Dorsey's figure looks like an embellished retelling of the superstition itself rather
    than a report of Judd's actual count.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The 300-ft center span was designed to slide on a south-end roller nest as steel
expanded and contracted, at a rate of 1 inch per 40°F. Sheet 11 called for 3 inches of
cold-weather travel — good to −54°F — but the resident engineer, under scheduling pressure,
set the nest to only 2 inches when the span was closed at 66°F in September 1897, over the
computer's written objection. That gave a real margin down to −14°F, colder than the valley
had yet seen, so nothing happened for twelve years. In August 1909 a re-decking gang jacked
both shoes and reset them to the *same* 2-inch mark — but with the steel already expanded by
summer heat to 86°F, which moved the effective binding point up to +6°F, a temperature the
valley crosses in an ordinary hard winter.

From then on, whenever the night's low fell below 6°F *and* the day had cooled by at least
30 degrees to get there, the bearing was driven past its remaining travel faster than
friction could let it creep and relieve the strain gradually; the shoe locked, contraction
strain built up in the truss and pins, and then released all at once, snapping the span into
its new position and discharging the stored energy as one deep, sudden report — carried far
more efficiently through the continuous rail than through open air, which is why it reached
the depot's telegraph sounder and floor before it was heard. A slow cold snap let the bearing
creep instead, so no strain built up and nothing happened; that is why a sharp 30-degree fall
was required as well as a low temperature. Once released, the shoe sat at the far end of its
travel and could not release again until a real daytime thaw (roughly above 40°F) let it
creep back — explaining the handful of cold, fast-falling nights that produced nothing.

The sound began in January 1912, not 1897 or 1898, because that is how long it took after
the 1909 re-decking to hit the first qualifying cold night; it stopped for good in March
1954 because the rebuild for diesel power replaced the roller nest with rocker bearings
giving 4 inches of travel each way — a margin no recorded temperature in the valley could
exhaust.

The competing explanation the valley itself offered — that this was Locomotive No. 9,
wrecked into the gorge in 1898 with her crew, "speaking" on cold nights — fails on its own
terms: no one died in that wreck, which happened on dry ground in daylight (contemporary
newspaper account); the same engine was sold in 1901 and worked, then died, 200 miles away
in Bly County in 1929 (that company's own ledgers); and the ghost theory has no account at
all for why the sound waited fourteen years to start. The physical evidence that confirms
the bearing theory instead is the recovered south pedestal itself, pulled from the creek bed
in 1962 with its original chisel-cut setting mark measured at 2 inches — physical proof the
nest really was set one inch short of the 3 inches the drawings called for.

## Section G — Summary

For 43 winters (1912–1954) a Pell County valley heard one deep boom from a railroad bridge
on its coldest nights and believed it was a wrecked locomotive still lying in the gorge with
its dead crew. No one had actually died there; that engine was sold in 1901 and scrapped 200
miles away in 1929. The real cause was a bridge bearing set an inch short of its design
travel in 1897 and pushed past its limit by 1909 repairs, so that below about 6°F, after a
sharp one-day cold snap, it locked and then broke free at once. New bearings in 1954 ended
it. It took a station log, an engineer's field book, a farm weather diary, and a 1962
creek-bed survey together — no one of them alone — to prove it.
