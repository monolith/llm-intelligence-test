# Notes on Retellings 01–08 (the "Sallow Creek / Cadder Falls ghost sound" set)

All eight documents concern the same real-world puzzle: a mysterious deep booming sound heard at night near Cadder Falls, on the Cadder Valley Railroad (CVR), for roughly four decades, plus the aftermath (the sold locomotive's later life, and a historical society's cataloguing of the surviving records). Below: full cast, full timeline, all quotes verbatim, all numbers, and every conflict/oddity found — within and between the eight retellings. Sections carried forward from the notes after R01–R04 are preserved in full; new material from R05–R08 is added throughout and flagged where it creates new conflicts or resolves old ones.

---

## THE EIGHT SOURCES (narrator, format, date recorded/written)

1. **R01 — "What the Valley Heard."** Narrator: **Vesta Cobb**, a mill hand's daughter, born **1920** in Cadder Falls (second house up from the mill gate). Format: oral-history tape recorded at her kitchen table for the county's oral-history collection, **1974**. She heard the sound herself as a child; presents the town's ghost-story belief as fact.

2. **R02 — "A Clerk's Son Remembers" (chapter "The Sallow Creek Viaduct").** Narrator: **Harlan Pike**, son of the CVR's chief clerk. From his unpublished memoir *"The Company at Ninestone,"* written in his seventies, **1958** (earliest-written of the original four, predates R03/R04). Debunking/company-records account.

3. **R03 — "The Night Book."** Narrator: **Judd Rennick**, retired station agent, interviewed at his kitchen table for the Pell County Historical Society, **1965**. He is the one who logged the sound for 43 winters. Empirical/statistical account, admits he has no explanation.

4. **R04 — "What the Creek Gave Back."** Narrator: **Ruth Frayne**, civil engineer (Pell County highway department, 3 years' experience). A talk delivered to the **Pell County Historical Society**, read at Ninestone, **1963** (so chronologically this talk predates Judd's 1965 interview and Vesta's 1974 tape). Her creek inspection was "last September" relative to 1963, i.e. ~Sept 1962 (R07 confirms: survey report dated September 1962). Presents a full engineering explanation, corroborated by an independent weather record.

5. **R05 — "The Engines My Grandfather Bought."** Narrator: **Alma Nace**, granddaughter of Silas Tolliver (founder of the Tolliver Lumber Company, Bly County), age **63**. Memoir written at the editor's request for the **Bly County annual, 1970**. Covers the Sixmile tram and the later career (1902–1929) of the locomotive originally sold off by the CVR — i.e., the same engine at the center of the Cadder Falls ghost legend, renumbered and put to work 200 miles north. She explicitly disclaims knowledge of "the bridge" story.

6. **R06 — "A Farm Book of Weather."** Narrator: **Lettie Wexler**, keeper of the Kettle Bench weather record, age **76**. Reminiscence for a farm journal, **1965**, part of a series on family-kept farm records. Corroborates and elaborates the independent weather-record cross-check used in R04.

7. **R07 — "Accession notes."** Narrator: **Merrit Sable**, curator, Pell County Historical Society, Ninestone, **1970**. Draft catalogue entries for four collections and one object (the field book, the night book, the weather record, the recovered pedestal, and Ruth Frayne's 1962 survey report, plus two photostats). The most archivally authoritative of the eight sources — quotes primary documents directly and states provenance chains — but the curator is working fast ("worked up in a single week") and flags several of his own uncertainties.

8. **R08 — "Remarks at the store."** Narrator: **Dorsey Tice**, storekeeper, taped at the counter of his store in Cadder Falls, **1961**, age **68** (so born **1893**). First-person oral account; the earliest-recorded of all eight sources chronologically at the point of recording (1961), though describing events from decades earlier.

Chronological order of composition (all eight): R02 (1958) → R08 (1961, tape) → R04 (1963, talk) → R03 (1965, interview) → R06 (1965, farm journal) → R07 (1970, accession notes) → R05 (1970, memoir) → R01 (1974, tape).

Note R02 (1958) predates Ruth's investigation, so Pike never knew of / responds to her findings. R08 (1961) likewise predates Ruth's 1962 survey, so Dorsey's account of the bridge is pre-explanation, though he lived to see the sound's 1954 end.

## FULL CAST AND RELATIONSHIPS

- **Vesta Cobb** — narrator R01; born 1920, Cadder Falls; father worked the mill his whole life; mother's people at the Falls since the rail line was built (grandmother boarded the construction workers). Husband said she "could talk the paint off a porch."
- **Harlan Pike** — narrator R02; his (unnamed) father was **chief clerk of the Cadder Valley Railroad from 1893 to 1919** (26 years). Pike wrote the memoir in his 70s (1958).
- **Judd Rennick** — narrator R03; also central figure in R07 and R08. Born **1888** (per his own statement in R03). Station agent (39 years total at the depot per R01/R03; R07 gives the precise range **1915–1954**). Father: **Emil Rennick**, a carpenter who built the falsework (temporary timber supporting the iron until it could stand alone) for the Sallow Creek Viaduct — proud of it, never credited. Emil's younger sister (Judd's aunt): **Adela Rennick** — worked for the same railroad before Judd was born, in the engineering party; never discussed it with Judd. **R07 now states explicitly and archivally: Emil and Adela were brother and sister, and Judd Rennick and Wendell Frayne (Ruth's father) were first cousins** — this resolves (from an archival/curatorial point of view) the earlier ambiguity about the Rennick–Frayne genealogy, though it still does not match how either R01 ("grand-niece") or R04 (Ruth calls Judd "my father's uncle") describe the relationship; see GENEALOGY CONFLICT section below.
  - Judd's start-of-nights date: R03 says "1907" (contradicted internally by R03 itself, which elsewhere says the book started in 1913); **R08 (Dorsey) says Judd "went on nights at that depot in 1909"** — a third distinct date for this event. R08 also states Judd "made agent in 1915" (matches R07's 1915 start of the 1915–1954 agent range) and "retired in June of 1954" (matches R07's range end and the last logged sound, 9 Feb 1954, and the March 1954 bearing replacement).
  - Judd **died in 1969** (R07); the night book passed to the Historical Society from his estate in 1969, having earlier been lent by him personally to Ruth Frayne in 1962 and returned to him afterward (R07) — this refines earlier notes, which only knew Judd "lent" the book to Ruth "a while back" (R03) or "some years back" (R01); we now know it was lent in 1962 and returned, remaining Judd's own property until his 1969 death/estate donation.
  - Judd is described in R01 as living into his 80s, still in town when "the young Frayne woman" (Ruth) came round; R01 says she was his **"grand-niece."** In R04, Ruth calls him **"my father's uncle."** R07's curator states Judd and Wendell Frayne were **first cousins** (not uncle/nephew), making Judd actually Ruth's first cousin once removed — a third, and now archivally-grounded, version of this relationship. See GENEALOGY CONFLICT.
- **Ruth Frayne** — narrator R04; also central to R06, R07. Civil engineer, Pell County highway department. Father: **Wendell Frayne** (living as of 1970, per R07). Grandmother: **Adela Rennick** — Adela's field book passed to Wendell Frayne on her death, then to Ruth, then donated by Wendell to the Historical Society in 1963 (R07: "Given to the Society in 1963 by Mr. Wendell Frayne of Cadder Falls. It was his mother's book" — confirming Adela was Wendell's **mother**, i.e., Ruth's paternal grandmother, consistent with R04). So Ruth is Adela's granddaughter, and Judd Rennick (Adela's nephew) is Wendell's first cousin (R07) — genealogically making Judd Ruth's first cousin once removed, not "grand-niece" (R01) or "uncle" (R04's own wording). Ruth's 1962 field survey is preserved as Item 5 in the Historical Society (a typescript with a linen tracing, county highway department, dated September 1962); she also recovered the pedestal (Item 4) that September, both donated to the Society in 1963.
- **Adela Rennick** — engineering-party "computer" (and rodman, per R04) for the CVR while the viaduct was being built. Per R02: "did the arithmetic, and she did it better than the party did" (Pike's father's own phrase). Kept her own duplicate field book in black oilcloth, separate from the official party book — thought eccentric.
  - **Birth year CONFLICT:** earlier notes (from R04) gave Adela's dates as **born 1874, died 1959** (age 85). **R07 (curator, presumably working from an archival/family source) instead states Adela was "born 1876 and died 1959."** These do not agree (1874 vs. 1876); if she died in 1959, "born 1874" implies age ~85 at death (matching R04's explicit "age 85"), while "born 1876" implies age ~83 — a genuine, unresolved numeric conflict between R04 and R07 on Adela's birth year/age at death.
  - Her employment ended when the engineering party was reduced. **R07 now gives the precise mechanism and date via an actual letter, quoted in full:** a CVR letter to A. Rennick, dated **30 April 1901**: *"The engineering party is reduced entire on the 15th May next, and your time is paid to that day. Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card."* This shows the whole party (not just Adela) was let go on **15 May 1901**, and — notably — that Tice asked for a **positive** recommendation to be entered on her record. This is in tension with Pike's (R02) speculation that Tice "never forgave" Adela's critical field-book entry and that this cost her the rehire; the archival letter shows no sign of a grudge, only a routine reduction-in-force with a recommendation attached. Flagged as a discrepancy between Pike's secondhand suspicion and the primary document.
  - Two others from the party were rehired the following August for the Ashby extension; **R07 now names them via a quoted office slip, dated 20 July 1901:** *"Notice. Re-engaged from the 1st August next for the Ashby extension: J. Corliss, rodman; F. Wain, chainman."* Adela was not among them.
  - Per R02, Pike believes (without documentary proof) that an entry she wrote criticizing the bridge got back to resident engineer Warren Tice, who "never forgave" it, and she was gone within four years.
  - Per R04/R07, one such entry is preserved verbatim (see Quotes section) — she reported the pedestal was set to the wrong (2", not 3") mark and was overruled by "W.T." (Warren Tice).
  - **R07's attribution oddity:** the curator flags that the field book (Item 1) is attributed on its wrapper to "**J. Rennick**" even though its content and internal signature ("— A.R.") clearly belong to Adela Rennick, while the night book (Item 2) is attributed to "**A. Rennick**" even though its content (station-agent log of the sound, 43 winters) is clearly Judd's. The curator explicitly notes this oddity as unresolved: *"The pencilled attributions on the wrappers of Items 1 and 2 sit oddly with the initials inside them; both wrappers were marked before the material reached me and I have carried them over as they stood, having had no time to compare hands. Resolve before the accession is published."* This is a new, explicitly-flagged cross-item labeling conflict.
- **Warren Tice** — resident engineer on the viaduct construction, later the CVR's "chief engineer at Ninestone," described as "a considerable man in this county" (R02). Referred to as "W.T." in Adela's field-book quote (R04/R07). **Died in 1937** (new date, from R08, Dorsey's own account). Per R08, Warren Tice **personally closed the span in 1897** ("he had closed the span himself in ninety-seven") and took his son Dorsey out onto the deck once, at age six, to show him — a vivid personal anecdote not found in any other source.
  - **MAJOR CONFLICT — Warren Tice's relationship to Dorsey Tice:** R01 (secondhand, Vesta's account) calls Dorsey "**nephew** of 'old Warren Tice.'" **R08 (Dorsey's own firsthand words) states unambiguously that Warren Tice was his father**: *"My father had no hand in that bridge... Warren Tice, his name was... [he] finished as chief engineer of the Cadder Valley up at Ninestone, and he died in 1937."* This is a direct, irreconcilable conflict between a secondhand town account (R01, uncle/nephew) and the subject's own firsthand testimony (R08, father/son) about the same two people. R08, being firsthand, is presumably more reliable, but the notes do not resolve this authoritatively — it is left as a flagged conflict.
  - New anecdote (R08 only): a rumor has circulated in the valley about something Warren Tice supposedly said at his own **retirement dinner, spring 1931** — Dorsey, who was present, flatly denies it: "He said nothing of the kind, and he never in his life spoke to me about that bridge." The content of the rumored remark is never given — an unresolved narrative loose end.
- **Dorsey Tice** — storekeeper at "Dorsey Tice's" store by the tracks at Cadder Falls; narrator of R08. Born **1893** at Cadder Falls, in "the house that stood where the feed shed is now"; 68 years old at time of the 1961 recording. Per R01 (secondhand): "railroad people out of Ninestone, engineering people, big in the company"; storekeeping seen by his family as "a comedown," which he didn't mind. Known (R01 only) for the best hard candy in the county, weighed heavy for children. In his own words (R08), never wrote anything down: "There is nothing in this store on paper but the day book, and the day book says what people owed me and nothing about bridges. Judd was the writing one." Lost a $5 bet to Judd over the sound (see Wager below, now with a THIRD account and a new date conflict).
- **Mr. Coyle** — foreman of the August 1909 bridge-repair (re-decking) gang; quoted in Judd's night-book note (R03/R07) saying "the deck is forty ton heavier than it was."
- **Anders Berg** — **new name, R08 only.** Per Dorsey: "Anders Berg set that nest. Everybody knew it. That is not a thing anybody in this town ever had to look up." Presented as the person who physically set the south roller nest/pedestal — not mentioned in any other source (R04/R07's field book only names "W.T." as the engineer who overruled Adela's objection; Adela herself is the one who measured/reported it). Possibly the workman who executed Tice's specification, not necessarily contradicting the field-book account, but an entirely new, uncorroborated name and claim.
- **Lidell** and **Sherrod** — **new names, R07 only**, from the newly-quoted page-71 entry in Adela's field book: *"March 6, 1898. No. 9 off the iron at the north approach, 7:20 a.m., running away from the quarry. Nobody killed. Lidell's arm broke, Sherrod scalded at the deck. Nine days to rerail her."* These are the two gang members previously mentioned only anonymously as "two of the gang" hurt (R02's newspaper quote). The entry independently corroborates the 8 March 1898 *Sentinel* account in every particular (same hour, same north end, same two injuries, none killed) — R07's curator explicitly notes this cross-check.
- **Peter Wexler** — began the volunteer daily weather record (high/low/precipitation) at Kettle Bench. **Start-date CONFLICT:** R04 (prior notes) gave **1 January 1894**; **R06 (Lettie's own first-person memoir) states her father "began the weather record in 1884"** ("eighty-one years of it, now, in one hand or the other," written in 1965: 1965−1884=81, internally consistent with 1884). **R07 (curator, citing "the depositor's own index sheet") gives 1 January 1894**, matching R04, not R06. So two sources (R04, R07 — both citing/derived from an index sheet) say 1894; one source (R06, Lettie's own memoir) says 1884. This is a genuine, unresolved ten-year conflict, with Lettie's own account as the outlier against two archivally-sourced dates.
  - **Relationship CONFLICT:** R04 and R06 both describe Peter and Lettie as **father and daughter** (R06 is explicit and firsthand: "My father was Peter Wexler... I have kept it since"). **R07's curator instead states: "The record was kept by two brothers named Wexler, the younger of whom carried it on after the elder gave it up"** — describing them as **brothers**, not father and daughter. This directly contradicts Lettie's own first-person testimony in R06 (where she calls Peter "my father" and refers to herself throughout as female, "the keeper... at seventy-six," a woman with a farm journal). This is a major, unresolved conflict — likely a curatorial error in R07 (secondhand, "from one conversation," per the accession note's own caveat about limited time), but it stands unreconciled in the record as given.
- **Lettie Wexler** — Peter's daughter (per R04/R06); narrator of R06. Continued the weather record from **1926** onward (matches R04 and R06 exactly). Age 76 when writing R06 in 1965. Thermometer kept on "the north wall of the milk house," readings taken "at daylight and again at four." Never missed a morning since 1926, "not with a broken wrist," not "the morning after my father's funeral." Lent all four cardboard boxes of records to Ruth Frayne in **1962** without asking why; Ruth returned every box (R06 praises this: "which I will say for her, because not everybody does"). Explicitly stated as unrelated to anyone else in the story ("We were related to nobody in this business") and never recorded any sound. **Died in 1967, the same year her boxes came in [to the Historical Society]** (R07) — R06 was written in 1965, two years before her death and two years before her donation.
  - In 1962, Ruth brought both the night book and the Wexler weather boxes to Lettie's own kitchen table simultaneously; Lettie read Judd's book herself for an hour while Ruth worked — the only time in her life she looked into it (R06; a new physical/narrative detail not in R04).
- **Locomotive No. 9** — a 4-4-0 built by the **Rowan Locomotive Works in 1889**, works number **1142**, cost the CVR **$8,600** new. Central to the ghost legend (R01) and to its factual refutation (R02, R04, R07), and — new in R05 — to its full second-life story after sale.
- **Silas Tolliver** — **new figure, R05.** Founder of the Tolliver Lumber Company, Bly County. Born **1849**; came into the county "with a mill, a horse, and a conviction that the horse was temporary." Built the mill twice (second time from the first mill's lumber). Died **1922**, "in the chair by the office stove." His obituary "led with the horse" despite his lifelong efforts to get rid of it — a wry detail his granddaughter Alma highlights.

- **Merrit Sable** — narrator/curator of R07; Pell County Historical Society, Ninestone, working in **1970**.

## GENEALOGY CONFLICT (three-way, now with an archival "official" answer that still doesn't fully reconcile)

- R01 (Vesta, secondhand folk account): Ruth Frayne is Judd Rennick's **"grand-niece."**
- R04 (Ruth's own words, quoted): Ruth calls Judd **"my father's uncle."**
- R07 (Merrit Sable, curator, archival): *"The author of Item 5 [Ruth] is the granddaughter of the writer of the field book, Adela Rennick... Her father, Mr. Wendell Frayne... and Judd Rennick, who wrote the night book, were **first cousins**. Judd Rennick's father, Emil, and Adela, who wrote the field book, were brother and sister."*
- Working through R07's stated facts: Emil and Adela are siblings → their children (Judd and Wendell) are first cousins → Wendell's daughter (Ruth) is Judd's **first cousin once removed**. This matches neither R01's "grand-niece" nor R04's "father's uncle" (though "father's [cousin]" would have been correct; "father's uncle" is simply wrong on this reading, since Judd is Wendell's cousin, not his uncle). R07 itself does not comment on this discrepancy with R01/R04 — it simply states the archival relationship as fact. No source reconciles all three characterizations explicitly.

## THE PHYSICAL SETTING (consistent across sources)

- **Cadder Valley Railroad (CVR)** — 31 miles total (R02).
  - **Ninestone** — milepost 0; county seat; company office, the elder Pike's desk.
  - **Sallow Creek Viaduct** — milepost 22.
  - **Cadder Falls** — milepost 23 (mill town).
  - **Ashby quarry** — milepost 31 (the reason the railroad was built at all).
- Grade from the quarry down to the viaduct: **2.2%** ("two and two-tenths percent"), held constant the whole way (R02). R03 adds this is "down the nine miles off the quarry" — consistent, since milepost 31 (quarry) minus milepost 22 (viaduct) = 9 miles.
- **Sallow Creek Viaduct structure** (R02): begun April 1896; opened to Cadder Falls **14 November 1897**. Three spans: 120 ft + 300 ft (center, pin-connected truss, over the gorge proper) + 120 ft = **540 feet total**, **97 feet above the water**.
- Thermal mechanics: center span pinned solid at the **north** end; the **south** end free to travel, resting on a nest of rollers in a cast pedestal (the "south shoe"). Steel lengthens in heat, shortens in cold.
- **The span was closed 12 September 1897** at thermometer **66°F** (R02; R04's/R07's grandmother's book gives the precise time, **3:40 p.m.**, matching the date/temp exactly). R08 (Dorsey) independently confirms via family anecdote that his father Warren Tice "closed the span himself in ninety-seven."
- **Kettle Bench** (R06): the Wexler farm, **three miles up the valley from Cadder Falls**, "on the shoulder above the creek where the frost sits longer than it does in town" — a new precise distance/description, not given in R04.

## THE 1898 WRECK — AND THE GHOST LEGEND IT SPAWNED

- **R01's legend (folk belief, stated as fact by Vesta):** Locomotive **Number Nine** went into the gorge "in the bad winter when the bridge was new," her whole crew went in with her, all were killed, and none were ever recovered — "they are down there yet, under the gravel and water." The sound is supposedly Number Nine's ghost still running the grade and unable to stop. Onset: "that same winter, the first winter after they opened that bridge — that would be ninety-seven going into ninety-eight" (winter 1897–98), immediately after the wreck. Duration given: "seventy-odd years."
- **R02's factual account (Pike, using company/newspaper records) directly refutes this:**
  - Date: **6 March 1898**, 7:20 a.m. ("twenty minutes past seven") — in daylight, not night.
  - A **work train** (not a passenger/regular run) came down off the quarry grade without enough brakes and **went off the iron at the north approach of the new viaduct** — i.e., never went into the gorge at all.
  - **Nobody was killed.** Two of the gang were hurt. Took 9 days to rerail.
  - Verbatim newspaper quote, *Ninestone Sentinel*, 8 March 1898: *"No lives were lost. Two of the gang are hurt, and the engine is on her side at the north end of the new viaduct, where she will be got up within the fortnight."*
  - Locomotive No. 9 specs: 4-4-0, Rowan Locomotive Works, built 1889, works no. 1142, cost $8,600 new.
  - No. 9 was **sold 19 June 1901** to the **Tolliver Lumber Company**, Bly County (200 miles north), for **$2,150**, "delivered on her own wheels" (i.e., she steamed away under her own power). Verbatim ledger quote (Pike's father's handwriting): *"Locomotive No. 9, 4-4-0, Rowan Works 1889, works no. 1142, sold this day to the Tolliver Lumber Company, Bly County, for two thousand one hundred and fifty dollars ($2,150.00), delivered on her own wheels."*
  - Pike: "three years after she is supposed to have been lying at the bottom of Sallow Creek with her dead."
- **R04/R07 add the locomotive's final fate:** Ruth wrote to the Bly County Historical Rooms (200 miles north), which hold the Tolliver Lumber Company's books (deposited 1938 by the company's bookkeeper — actually, per R05, deposited personally by **Pearl Tolliver Nace**, not a "bookkeeper" in the impersonal sense R04 implies, though Pearl *was* the company's bookkeeper, so this is not a true conflict, just a naming/framing difference — "apparently unconsulted since" (R04) until R05 confirms it drew no inquiry "for twenty-four years," i.e., 1938–1962, matching Ruth's 1962 inquiry almost exactly). Photostats confirmed: stock-book entry of 19 June 1901 (matches R02 exactly), plus a **shop-book entry of May 1929 recording No. 9 cut up (scrapped) at Sixmile**. "She left this valley alive and she died two hundred miles from it."
- **R05 gives the full independent second-life account of the engine, from the buyer's side — a major new primary strand:**
  - Purchased **1902** (not 1901 — see PRICE/DATE CONFLICT below) by Silas Tolliver's Tolliver Lumber Company for use on the **Sixmile tram**, a 19-mile, 30-lb-rail logging line named for a creek, not its length.
  - **PRICE CONFLICT:** R02/R04/R07 (CVR-side ledger and photostat, quoted twice identically) state the sale price was **$2,150.00**. **R05 (Alma Nace, buyer's-side memoir, citing her mother's ledger) states: "We paid two thousand five hundred and ten dollars for her. That was in 1902."** — i.e., **$2,510**, not $2,150, and dated **1902**, not the CVR's **19 June 1901**. This is a direct, unreconciled numeric and date conflict between the seller's records (R02/R07 photostat) and the buyer's records (R05) for the same transaction. (Possible reconciliation not stated by any source: a 1901 sale date with delivery/final payment completed in 1902, or simple transcription error in one ledger — left as an open conflict.)
  - Delivered "on her own wheels" — confirmed and elaborated: eleven days, four railroads, handed over at the interchange by a Pell County crew who "drank coffee in our kitchen" (Pearl's kitchen) and rode home as passengers. The fireman had an unexplained burn on his wrist.
  - Renumbered: the shop "painted out the 9 and painted on a 4" (company numbered engines by purchase order, not merit); she became **"the Four,"** "No. 4" in later records — matching R04/R07's photostat notation "No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9)."
  - She was "a disappointment for exactly one season" on steep geared-engine grades ("a racehorse to pull a stump," per Silas), then thrived once kept on the straight main stem — **27 years** of service, nearly all on the main stem.
  - Maintenance history (R05, new detail): **boiler retube 1909; new tires 1913; cracked frame welded 1919, welded again 1922; a full "shopping" (rebuild) in 1924** that "took her down to her bones."
  - Distinctive whistle — every child at Sixmile could identify her by ear (thematically parallel to the Cadder Falls sound's distinctiveness, but this is just an ordinary train whistle, explicitly not the ghost sound).
  - **September 1928:** the company took delivery of a second-hand eight-coupled engine; No. 4 was set out behind the shop, tarpaulin over her stack, through that winter.
  - **May 1929: cut up (scrapped) at Sixmile.** Shop-book entry (quoted, matches R07's photostat exactly): *"No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile this month. Scrap to Ravel Brothers, $470."* Ravel Brothers took the tires and brass separately; the boiler shell became a stationary wash-water tank at the mill, still there when the mill came down. Alma: "I have wondered since whether anybody who filled a bucket from it knew what they were filling it from."
  - Financial framing (R05): cost $2,510 [or $2,150, per the conflicting CVR-side figure], scrap value $470 — "in between she paid for herself so many times over that my grandfather stopped counting."
  - The company itself: "The cut ran out in 1936 and the company wound itself up... In 1938 my mother put the stock book, the shop book, the timber ledgers, and eleven boxes of correspondence into the back of a truck and drove them herself to the Bly County Historical Rooms, where they were catalogued and shelved and did not draw one single inquiry for twenty-four years." Pearl's own explanation, quoted: "I did not care to have a thing end twice."
  - Alma explicitly disclaims knowledge of the Cadder Falls bridge legend: "The county the Four came from had some noise on a bridge down there... I never knew the rights of it and I never inquired. It was two hundred miles away and it had nothing to do with our engine... Somebody else can have the bridge." (Thematically significant: confirms from the far, unconnected side of the story that the engine's real fate — hauling logs in Bly County for 27 years, then being scrapped in 1929 — was completely disconnected from and oblivious to the ghost legend growing up around its old name at Cadder Falls.)
- **R03's separate refutation angle:** Judd counted deaths at Cadder Falls to test the folk theory that "she rang for deaths" (i.e., someone would die within a week of the sound). Over the 43 winters he kept the book: **190 total deaths** at Cadder Falls; only **7** fell within a week after a night the sound occurred (7/190 ≈ 3.7%). He notes this ratio without further comment, but it's presented to undercut the omen legend.
  - **MAJOR CONFLICT — R08 (Dorsey) directly contradicts this ratio, and in the opposite direction:** Dorsey claims: *"Better than a third of the burying in this town came inside the week after a night she spoke, he told me once, and he did not appear to think much of it either way."* "Better than a third" (>33%) is wildly different from Judd's own actual counted figure of 7/190 (≈3.7%) as reported in R03 — a roughly tenfold discrepancy, and one that reverses the intended point: Judd's own numbers were meant to *debunk* the death-omen myth, while Dorsey's recollected version of "what Judd told him" would actually *support* it. This is flagged as a significant, unresolved conflict — possibly Dorsey misremembering, exaggerating for storytelling effect, or conflating a different (smaller, less rigorous) sample.

## THE 1908/1909 RE-DECKING — CONFLICTING DATES, CONVERGING PHYSICS

- **R02 (Pike) says the re-decking was "in 1908":** bridge gang on structure "some weeks that summer"; 85-lb steel replacing 60-lb; new ties throughout; both shoes jacked and re-set; finished deck **40 tons heavier**. Pike's own theory (his only stated opinion in the chapter): the added weight overloaded the roller nest, and "the noise the Falls made so much of began somewhere about that time and not before it."
- **R03 (Judd's night-book quote, copied off the depot slate) says "August 1909":** *"Bridge gang here three weeks, August 1909. New 85-lb. steel, new ties, both shoes jacked and set again to the mark. Thermometer on the north pier read 86 at four o'clock when they seated the south nest. Mr. Coyle says the deck is forty ton heavier than it was."* — **Immediately after quoting this, Judd's own narration says "That was the summer of 1911,"** an internal contradiction within R03 itself (the note says 1909, his spoken recollection says 1911).
- **R04 (Ruth, citing Judd's book) also says August 1909**, matching the note's date, not Judd's spoken "1911."
- **R07 (curator) independently quotes the identical note from the actual night book (Item 2), confirming the "August 1909" date directly from the primary artifact itself** — the strongest evidentiary confirmation yet that 1909 (not 1908 or 1911) is correct for the re-decking.
- **CONFLICT (updated):** three different years for the same re-decking event: **1908** (R02), **1909** (R03's own written quote, R04, AND R07's direct quote of the primary document), **1911** (R03's spoken recollection, contradicting its own quoted note). 1909 is now supported by three independent citations of/from the primary source; 1908 and 1911 stand as the outliers.
- Both R02 and R03/R04/R07 agree: 85-lb steel replacing 60-lb, new ties, both shoes jacked/re-set, **40 tons heavier**.

## THE ENGINEERING EXPLANATION (Ruth Frayne's arithmetic, R04; corroborated by R07)

- Ruth's dry-creek inspection (creek diverted 11 weeks by a flood-control project) had **four findings**:
  1. **No locomotive in Sallow Creek.** Walked the bed 800+ feet above/below the viaduct — no frame, boiler shell, driving wheel, "nothing of iron larger than a spike." Photographs "on the table at the back."
  2. **The pedestal.** Found 40 feet below the abutment on the south side, in the gravel "where the 1954 gang threw it over" — the old south shoe/roller-nest pedestal, "the size of a kitchen stove." A chisel mark (the original erection "setting mark") on the roller seat measures **2 inches** (checked three times with a steel tape, same result each time). The 1954 replacement bearings are **rockers** (not rollers) measuring **4 inches** of travel each way (also measured by Ruth). **R07 adds:** the pedestal (Item 4) is now in the Historical Society's north store room, on the floor ("two men to move it"), **broken at one corner** (new physical detail), not yet assigned an accession number as of 1970.
  3. **Grandmother's field book** (Adela Rennick's, in black oilcloth) — passed Adela → Wendell Frayne (1959, on Adela's death) → Ruth → donated to the Society by Wendell in 1963 (R07). R07 gives its physical description: pocket-size, roughly 4½ by 7 inches, sewn, corners rubbed through, ~120 pages used in pencil.
  4. **The night book** — lent to Ruth directly by Judd Rennick himself in **1962** (74 years old at the time, living three streets from the depot), returned to him afterward, and only came to the Society in 1969 from his estate (R07).
- **Ruth's math, using her grandmother's stated rule** (see CONFLICT below re: which rule):
  - Rule: 1 inch of travel per 300 ft of span per **40 degrees** F of temperature change.
  - Sheet 11 (the design spec) called for **3 inches** of cold-weather travel = 120°F of allowable cooling.
  - Span closed at 66°F → as *designed*, the bridge would only run out of travel at **66 − 120 = −54°F** (never reached in the valley) — so the design, if built correctly, would never have failed.
  - But it was actually built to the **2-inch** chisel mark (not 3) = 80°F of allowable cooling → **66 − 80 = −14°F** — still colder than the valley reaches, which is why nothing happened for the first 12 years (1897–1909).
  - **August 1909:** the gang re-jacked the shoes back to the *same* 2-inch mark, but did so with the steel at **86°F** (not the original 66°F). New threshold: **86 − 80 = 6°F above zero.**
  - This **6°F threshold matches Judd's night-book finding exactly** (sound only occurs when night's low falls below 6°F). Mechanism: when the span cools past its now-miscalibrated binding point, the south shoe "hangs up" (can't slide further), stress builds in the truss, then the shoe "lets go all at once" and the span drops into position — producing one sharp report that travels along the rails. Ruth's line: **"It was not a train. It was a bridge, arriving where it should have been."**
- **Quoted entries from Adela Rennick's field book (R04, R07 — R07's version is the direct archival transcription):**
  - Front matter (R07 calls this Item 1's front matter, "a single ruled line evidently kept there for reference"): *"Rule for the long span: one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees."*
  - Page 62 (R07 confirms this page number and calls it "the most substantial entry in the book, and the one the survey of 1962 turns on"): *"Sept. 12, 1897. Span closed 3:40 p.m., thermometer 66°. Rollers set at the two-inch mark. Sheet 11 calls for three. Reported to W.T., who answers that the pedestal cannot be recast before the fall haul and that two inches is more than the valley will ever ask for. Entered here so that it is somewhere. — A.R."*
  - **New — Page 71 (R07 only):** *"March 6, 1898. No. 9 off the iron at the north approach, 7:20 a.m., running away from the quarry. Nobody killed. Lidell's arm broke, Sherrod scalded at the deck. Nine days to rerail her."* (Names the two injured gang members for the first time; independently corroborates the 8 March 1898 newspaper account R02 quotes.)
  - **New — loose letter and slip (R07 only):** CVR to A. Rennick, 30 April 1901 (reduction notice, quoted above), and the 20 July 1901 re-engagement slip naming J. Corliss and F. Wain (quoted above).
- **Closing quote of Ruth's report** (written on the tailgate of her truck; R07 confirms this is the closing paragraph of Item 5, the county highway department survey typescript, September 1962): *"The south pedestal was recovered from the creek bed forty feet below the abutment, where the 1954 gang threw it. The chisel mark on the roller seat is at two inches. Sheet 11 calls for three. There is no locomotive in this creek and there never was one. What the valley heard for forty-three winters was a bridge letting go of itself, one inch late."*
- Ruth's closing line (R04): *"My grandmother measured that bridge and was not believed. I measured it again sixty-five years later, with a better tape and worse handwriting, and she was right. That is all I have."* (1897 + 65 = 1962, consistent with her creek visit "last September" before the 1963 talk, and with R07's dating of the survey to September 1962.)

### CONFLICT — the expansion rule itself (40 vs 50 degrees per inch)
- **R02 (Pike):** "The rule the engineers worked to was one inch in three hundred feet for **fifty** degrees." Also: "Sheet 11 of the contract drawings called for **four inches** of travel on the south nest. Four inches was generous..."
- **R04/R07 (Adela's field book, quoted twice, now confirmed by the curator's direct transcription):** "one inch in three hundred feet for **forty** degrees" and "Sheet 11 calls for **three** [inches]."
- These are two direct, unreconciled numeric conflicts between R02 and R04/R07: the rule-of-thumb (40° vs 50° per inch) and what Sheet 11 actually specified (3" vs 4"). Ruth's entire mathematical proof depends on the 40°-rule and 3"-spec numbers from her grandmother's primary-source field book (now doubly confirmed by the curator's independent transcription in R07), which strengthens the case that R04/R07's figures are the more authoritative ones versus R02's secondhand company-memory numbers — though no source states this explicitly.

## JUDD RENNICK'S NIGHT BOOK (R03; independently catalogued/quoted in R07; discussed secondhand in R08)

- Judd went on nights in **1907** (R03's own early statement) — but see contradiction below; **R08 (Dorsey) instead says Judd "went on nights at that depot in 1909."** A third figure (1913) also appears in R03 itself, and R04 computes an evidence-based onset of 1912 (Wexler record). See the onset-date conflict list below — now four candidate dates: 1907, 1909, 1912, 1913.
- Five columns kept: date, day's high, night's low, whether she spoke, and the hour. (R07 confirms this exactly: "Five ruled columns: the date; the day's high; the night's low; whether the sound came; the hour it came.")
- **Sound description (Judd, R03):** one deep report; not a rattle, not a roll; didn't travel — "there and then it was done." Order of perception: the telegraph sounder on his table rattled in its bracket first (before the sound reached the room) → then the floor moved → then the noise itself. He timed the gap once with a watch and "could not get a reading on it, it is that short," but the order never varied across 43 winters. Always between midnight and 4 a.m., never evening, never daylight.
  - **R08 (Dorsey) gives an independent lay description, broadly consistent:** "One deep report, down low, the kind you take in through your feet before your ears catch up... Not a bang. A report. Like somebody dropped something heavy two rooms away in a house made all of one piece." Also confirms: never before midnight, never twice in a night, only in "hard sudden cold" (a day "decent at dinner time and murderous by dark"), never in a slow, steady cold spell. This matches Judd's/Ruth's quantitative 30-degree-fall/sub-6°F conditions in lay terms.
- **First occurrence, per Judd's spoken account:** "the winter of 1913" — he was alone on nights, thought a rail car had gotten loose in the quarry, went out with a lantern (cold enough that the lantern glass cracked), found nothing wrong, and "in the morning I ruled up a book." **This directly contradicts his own earlier statement that he started the book in 1907** (see CONFLICT section).
- **Two conditions, found empirically after reviewing the book, no exceptions in 43 winters:**
  1. Night's low must be **below 6°F** (never at 7°, never at 6.5° — strictly below 6).
  2. The day before must have been **at least 30°F warmer** than the following night's low (a fall of 30+ degrees).
- **Totals — THREE-WAY CONFLICT on the exact count:**
  - **R03 (Judd's spoken account) / R07 (direct quote of the night book's own back-page summary):** *"Forty-three winters. Sixty-one nights she spoke. Thirty-four winters she spoke at least once and nine she never spoke at all. Five nights answered both the conditions and gave nothing, which makes sixty-six nights that answered them in all."* (61 spoke + 5 silent-but-qualifying = 66 total qualifying nights; 34 + 9 = 43 winters.) R07's quotation is a direct transcription of the primary document itself — the strongest evidence for this version.
  - **R06 (Lettie Wexler, cross-checking the same book against her own weather record in 1962):** agrees on **61 nights she spoke**, and that every one matched her own book's sub-6°F/30°-fall criteria with no exceptions — but states **"eight nights answered both the conditions and gave nothing at all,"** for **"sixty-nine nights in all that answered them"** (61 + 8 = 69, not 66). This conflicts with R03/R07's "five" silent nights.
  - **R08 (Dorsey, recalling Judd's figures from memory in 1961):** gives yet a third, quite different tally: **"Fifty-seven nights, that book showed... Thirty winters she spoke in and thirteen she never opened her mouth at all — thirty and thirteen, and there is your forty-three."** (57 nights spoken, 30+13=43 winters — both the "nights spoken" figure (57 vs. 61) and the winters-split (30/13 vs. 34/9) disagree with R03/R07.)
  - Net: the core statistic of the whole story — how many nights the sound occurred — has three different values across sources: **61** (R03, R07 — both citing/quoting the book directly) vs. **57** (R08, secondhand recollection). The "silent but qualifying" count has two conflicting values: **5** (R03/R07, from the book itself) vs. **8** (R06, Lettie's independent recount). R07, quoting the primary artifact directly, is the most authoritative for the 61/5/66 figures; R06 and R08 are both independently produced but disagree with the primary source and with each other.
- **Judd's only guess (offered explicitly as speculation, not fact):** the sound wouldn't repeat twice within the same freeze — once it had spoken, it stayed silent until a "real thaw" (above 40°F daytime). Each of the 5 (or 8, per R06) silent-but-qualifying nights occurred within a cold spell where the sound had already spoken and no thaw above 40°F had intervened.
- **The gang note** (quoted in the front of his book, copied off the depot slate — see re-decking section above for full quote, now triple-confirmed by R03, R04, and R07's direct transcription).
- **Death-omen count:** "she rang for deaths" was the town belief. Judd counted (R03): 190 total deaths at Cadder Falls during his book-keeping years; 7 fell within a week after a sounding. He states the two numbers "and I have never said them at the store." **R08 (Dorsey) contradicts this with "better than a third" — see MAJOR CONFLICT flagged above in the 1898 Wreck section.**
- **The wager — now with THREE accounts and a new date conflict:**
  - **R03 (Judd's own account, per earlier notes), full detail:**
    - Setting: Dorsey Tice's store, mid-afternoon, **January 1937**.
    - Temps: 41°F at noon; 38°F at 4 p.m.; falling 4°F/hour steadily since 1 p.m.
    - Judd's calculation: 38 down to 6 = 32 degrees; at 4°F/hour = 8 hours → under 6°F around midnight. Day's high (41°F) to sub-6°F is a fall >30°F. No thaw since the second week of December, so the bridge was "rested" (eligible to sound again).
    - Judd predicted she would speak before morning; Dorsey bet him **$5** she would not.
    - Actual low that night: **3 below zero.** She spoke at **10 minutes past 1 a.m.** (1:10 a.m.).
    - Dorsey paid the $5 the next day, "in the store, in front of everybody" (per R03's telling), unprompted by Judd; told the story on himself for the rest of his life.
  - **R01's version (secondhand):** "Bet him she would speak on a certain night and she spoke. Five dollars, I always heard, and five dollars then was five dollars." No date or temperature detail.
  - **R08 (Dorsey's own firsthand account) — NEW DATE CONFLICT:** Dorsey dates the wager to **"the fourteenth of January, 1936"** (not 1937 as in R03) — "I say that without hesitation, I have told it a hundred times and it does not change." The circumstantial details otherwise match almost exactly: Judd came in "about four in the afternoon" (for **kerosene**, a new detail — a gallon can, not mentioned in R03), stated the same three temperatures — "forty-one at noon... thirty-eight at four o'clock... coming down four degrees an hour since one" — identical to R03's figures. Same outcome: "it went to three below that night, and she spoke at ten minutes past one." So the wager's temperatures, timing, and outcome are identical across R03 and R08, but **the year differs: 1937 (R03) vs. 1936 (R08).** R08's account is internally self-consistent: Dorsey says Judd "never once brought it up again in **twenty-five years**," and the recording is made in 1961; 1961−1936=25, exactly matching — giving R08's 1936 date a small internal corroboration that R03's 1937 date lacks. Also, per R08, Dorsey **paid Judd "the next morning before I opened"** (i.e., privately, before customers arrived) — a minor further conflict with R03's characterization that he paid "the next day, in the store, in front of everybody."
- **The rockers (bearing replacement):** "the rockers went in, in the **spring of 1953**" (R03) — new rocker bearings with **4 inches of travel each way** installed; old cast pedestals thrown over the abutment into the creek. Judd, the agent, was not told directly — found out from a section man.
  - **CONFLICT:** R02 (Pike) dates this rehabilitation to **March 1954** ("the road was rehabilitated for diesel power and the old bearings under the center span were taken out and new ones put in... And the noise... stopped, that year, and never came back"). R04 also refers to "**the 1954 gang**" that threw the pedestal into the creek. **R07 now independently confirms via the primary document itself: "The new bearings went into the bridge in March 1954"** and states "**the last night entered as heard is 9 February 1954**" — i.e., the sound occurred once more just weeks before the March 1954 rebuild, then never again. **R08 (Dorsey) also confirms 1954:** "in fifty-four the railroad went in there and rebuilt the whole business — new bearings... and she never spoke again." So R02, R04, R07, and R08 all agree on **1954**; only **R03's own spoken "spring of 1953"** is the outlier — now a 4-against-1 conflict, strongly suggesting 1954 is correct and Judd misspoke or misremembered by a year in his 1965 interview.
  - Judd's final observation: the **second winter after the rockers**, three nights occurred that were under 6°F with falls >30° — and there was no sound on any of them. He wrote his last note and stopped:
    > *"Second winter since the rockers went in. Three nights under six degrees, three falls of better than thirty, and not a sound out of her either winter. I have kept this book forty-three winters and I do not know now what it was I was listening to."*
    **R07 now dates this final entry precisely: 2 March 1956** ("Nothing after it but ruled blanks.") — new information not in R03/R04. This date is consistent with "second winter since the rockers went in" if the rockers went in March 1954 (winter 1954–55 = first winter after; winter 1955–56 = second winter, ending around March 1956) — this actually helps confirm 1954 (not 1953) as the correct rocker-installation year, since 1953 would make the "second winter" one year too early relative to the 2 March 1956 dateline.
    (Note: the phrase "either winter" is still slightly odd/ambiguous given he's describing only "the second winter" with three qualifying nights.)
  - Judd explicitly disclaims having any explanation: "I have not got one word of explanation, and I am not going to make one up at my age."
- Judd's own summary of the railroad geography (matches R02): quarry milepost 31, Falls milepost 23, bridge milepost 22, grade 2.2% down the nine miles off the quarry.
- **The Frayne girl (Ruth) came "a while back,"** sat at his table, read the whole book through without talking (which he appreciated), asked to take it away; he let her have it. **R07 now dates this precisely to 1962**, and states the book was returned to Judd afterward, remaining his own property until his 1969 death.
- **R07 adds one more statistic not in R03/R04:** "The fullest winter in the book is 1917–18, with five nights" — **independently corroborated by R06 (Lettie)**, who separately (and without having read the book in detail before 1962) notes: "the fullest winter in his book was 1917 and 1918, five nights, more than any other winter he kept — which surprised me not at all, since that was our own worst winter." Strong agreement between R06 and R07 on this specific point.

### CONFLICT — when Judd started the book / when the sound began (now FOUR candidate dates)
- R03 (early in his own account): "I went on nights in 1907 and I started the book that same winter" → implies book started **1907**.
- R03 (later in the same account): "The first time she spoke was the winter of **1913**... In the morning I ruled up a book" → implies the book started **1913**, prompted by the first occurrence. This is an internal contradiction within R03 itself.
- R04 (Ruth, using the independent Wexler weather record cross-checked against Judd's book): the sound's actual onset — the first night meeting both conditions after the August 1909 re-decking — was **17 January 1912**, quoting the Wexler log: *"Jan. 17. High 34. Low −2 at daylight. Clear, still."* Ruth states explicitly: "That is the night the sound began, and the night my father's uncle started his book."
- **R08 (Dorsey) adds a fourth figure: Judd "went on nights at that depot in 1909"** (distinct from all three of the above: 1907, 1912, 1913). Note this "1909" refers to when Judd began working nights, which is the same claim R03 makes for 1907 — so R03 and R08 disagree on Judd's night-shift start year as well as on the book's start year.
- **Net conflict: FOUR different start dates for the sound/book across sources — 1907, 1909, 1912, and 1913.** R04's version (1912) is the most evidentially grounded (tied to an independent, dated weather record and to the physics), but no source reconciles the discrepancy explicitly.
- Separately, R01 (Vesta, secondhand folk account) claims the sound began in **winter 1897–98**, immediately after the (fictionalized) wreck — a totally different era from the 1909–1913 window that R03/R04/R08 establish. This remains the biggest cross-source conflict: R01 ties onset to the 1898 wreck; R03/R04/R08 tie onset to the 1909 re-decking, roughly 11-15 years later.
- R01 also states the sound persisted "seventy-odd years" — if onset were 1897-98 and end 1953-54, that's about 56 years, not "seventy-odd" — a further internal-numeric looseness in R01 (unless Vesta is speaking loosely/rounding, or her elders' oral tradition simply exaggerated the span).

## THE WEXLER WEATHER RECORD (R04, independent corroboration; R06 firsthand; R07 archival)

- Kept at Kettle Bench, **three miles up the valley from Cadder Falls** (R06). Started **1 January 1894** by **Peter Wexler** (R04, R07) — **or 1884**, per Lettie's own first-person account in R06 — see CONFLICT above. Continued by **Lettie Wexler** (his daughter, per R04/R06 — or by "the younger of two brothers," per R07's conflicting curatorial note) since **1926**. Lent to Ruth in **1962**, in four cardboard boxes, no questions asked; all four boxes returned. Explicitly stated (R06): the Wexlers are unrelated to anyone else in the story and never recorded any sound themselves — pure weather data. Method (R06, new detail): thermometer on the north wall of the milk house, never moved; readings taken at daylight and at 4 p.m.; the daylight reading is the "low" unless the night was unusual, in which case it's noted in the margin "and you do not argue about it."
- Their record **agrees with Judd's book on all 61 nights** the sound occurred (independent cross-validation) — R04 and R06 agree on this headline finding, though R06's own count of *silent-but-qualifying* nights (8) differs from R03/R07's (5) — see conflict above.
- **Pre-re-decking window (Nov 1897 – Aug 1909):** 7 nights at Kettle Bench were below 6°F, 4 of those also had falls of 30°+ — i.e., met both of Judd's later-discovered conditions — yet **nothing was heard on any of them**, and "nobody at the Falls has ever claimed there was." This is presented as key proof: the original (over-generous, −54°F threshold) design would never fail in this valley, so nothing happened despite qualifying cold nights. **R06 confirms this exact figure independently** ("In those twelve years there were seven nights below six degrees. Four of the seven came after days at least thirty degrees warmer...").
  - Coldest reading in that 12-year window: **−9°F on 4 February 1899** — colder than typical but still short of −14°F (the threshold implied by the as-built 2-inch/66°F setting) and far short of what the corrected 6°F-threshold (post-1909) would require. **R06 confirms this figure and date exactly.**
- **Two winters immediately after the re-decking** (1909–1911ish) never got below **+9°F**; the coldest reading in that span was on **3 February 1911**. **R06 confirms this exactly** ("The winter of 1909 and 1910 and the winter of 1910 and 1911 never went below nine above zero... the coldest morning of the pair was the third of February, 1911").
- **First night under 6°F after the August 1909 re-decking: 17 January 1912** — quoted identically in R04, R06, and R07: *"Jan. 17. High 34. Low −2 at daylight. Clear, still."* (Fall from 34 to −2 = 36 degrees, also satisfying the 30-degree-fall condition.) Presented as the actual onset night of the sound.
- **Extremes across the whole Wexler series — CONFLICT on the record low:**
  - **R04 (prior notes):** "highest reading 101°F, July 1936; lowest reading −13°F, January 1918."
  - **R06 (Lettie, firsthand):** "Eleven below zero, in January of 1918, and that is the lowest figure in the whole series"; highest "one hundred and one degrees" in July 1936 (matches R04 on the high).
  - **R07 (curator, "from the depositor's own index sheet"):** "record low eleven below zero, January 1918; record high one hundred and one degrees, July 1936" — matches R06, not R04.
  - **Net: two independent sources (R06, R07) agree on −11°F as the record low for January 1918; only the earlier-recorded figure (R04, −13°F) disagrees.** This suggests −11°F is more likely correct and −13°F may be an error in the earlier retelling/notes, but it is presented here as an unresolved conflict per the task's rules (not silently corrected).
- R06 adds: winter **1917–1918** was "the deepest cold anybody up here ever wrote down" — matching its status (per R06 and R07 both) as the fullest winter for the sound in Judd's book (5 nights) — a nice cross-source thematic consistency (worst winter on record ⇒ most soundings in one winter).

## THE 1911 NINESTONE FIRE (R02 only — not mentioned in any other retelling)

- **4 April 1911:** the roundhouse and office at Ninestone burned. Pike's father watched it from across the yard and never again cared for the smell of a coal fire. Destroyed: all construction records of the road — contract files, erection time books, the entire account of how the viaduct was built and by whom. Pike: "I do not think anybody at the time understood that a fire could take away a thing that had already happened." (Note: this means the *company's own* copy of the records was lost — but Adela's *private duplicate* field book survived independently in her family, which is precisely why Ruth's grandmother's book in R04/R07 is the only surviving primary documentation of the original construction/setting details — a detail not stated outright by any narrator but inferable from combining R02 and R04/R07.)

## MISCELLANEOUS DETAILS AND ODDITIES

- R01: Someone at the store proposed "ice in the gorge" as a rival theory; Vesta rebuts it: "why not every cold night, why the killing nights only" — the ice theory can't explain the sound's selectivity, which (unknowingly, from Vesta's perspective) foreshadows Judd's/Ruth's precise threshold-based explanation.
- R01's qualitative description of a "killing night" (a soft/warm day, then the sky "went hard and clear" and cold at dark) matches, in lay terms, Judd's/Ruth's quantitative 30-degree-fall condition — thematically consistent even though R01 gives no numbers. **R08 (Dorsey) gives an almost identical lay description independently:** "the kind of day that is decent at dinner time and murderous by dark."
- R01 specifically frames the sound as one report described by Vesta's mother as: **"Like a door shut in a cellar three towns off."** (Direct quote, attributed to Vesta's mother via Vesta.)
- R01: the town's blamed cause for the sound stopping: "the railroad had spoiled it... Somebody had come from off with money and taken it away from us." **R08 (Dorsey) gives a closely related but distinct framing:** "This town was disappointed. A thing that was ours for as long as anybody in this town could remember, that Ninestone did not have and the state road did not have, and some men come out with a crane and take it away in a month and do not even know they have taken it." Both sources agree emotionally (a sense of dispossession/loss at the 1954 rebuild) though the exact phrasing/blame differs.
- R03: Judd's description of the felt/heard order is more granular than R01's — telegraph sounder rattle, then floor, then sound — a detail no other source mentions (R01 only mentions floor-then-sound, no telegraph detail; R08 mentions only "floor comes up to meet you," no telegraph detail either).
- Age check: R04 (1963) describes Judd as "seventy-four." R03 (1965 interview) has Judd say "I was born in eighty-eight" (1888). 1963 − 1888 = 75, so "seventy-four" in 1963 is off by about one year (plausible if his birthday fell later in the year) — minor, not a hard conflict.
- Genealogy oddity: see dedicated GENEALOGY CONFLICT section above (now three-way: R01 "grand-niece," R04 "father's uncle," R07 archival "first cousins" [Judd/Wendell] making Judd Ruth's first cousin once removed).
- Warren Tice appears as: resident engineer on the original construction, later CVR chief engineer at Ninestone (R02); as "W.T." in Adela's field-book quote who overrules her pedestal-setting objection (R04/R07); as uncle of storekeeper Dorsey Tice per secondhand R01, but as Dorsey's own **father** per firsthand R08 (MAJOR CONFLICT, see above); died **1937** (R08); personally closed the span in 1897 and took young Dorsey out on the deck once (R08, new anecdote); subject of an unspecified, denied rumor about something said at his 1931 retirement dinner (R08, new unresolved thread).
- The name "Coyle" (foreman, 1909 gang) appears in R03's direct quote and is now independently confirmed via R07's direct transcription of the same night-book entry.
- Steel upgrade specifics (60-lb → 85-lb rail/decking steel) are consistent between R02 (describing 1908) and R03/R04/R07 (describing 1909) — the *steel spec* details match even though the *year* is disputed.
- "Forty tons heavier" (post-re-decking deck weight) is consistent across R02, R03 (Coyle's quote), R04, and R07.
- R02's own explanatory theory (added weight overloading the roller nest) is superseded/contradicted by R04's more precise thermal-binding explanation — both blame the same re-decking event, but for different physical reasons (weight vs. miscalibrated thermal travel threshold). Neither source acknowledges the other's theory (R02 predates R04's investigation).
- Pike's (R02) closing line, asserting the sound was purely local to Cadder Falls: **"We never heard it at Ninestone. My father would have written it down."**
- **New (R08): "Anders Berg set that nest."** An entirely new, otherwise-uncorroborated name credited by town common knowledge (per Dorsey) with physically setting the south roller nest — not mentioned in Adela's field book (which names only "W.T." as the overruling engineer) or in any other source. Left as an unverified/uncorroborated claim.
- **New (R05): the far side of the story.** Alma Nace's memoir independently and unknowingly corroborates every fact about Locomotive No. 9's sale and scrapping that R02/R04/R07 established from the Cadder Valley side — the sale (with a price/date discrepancy, see PRICE CONFLICT above), the renumbering to "No. 4," the 27 years of ordinary, unhaunted service in Bly County, and the exact May 1929 scrapping (verbatim shop-book quote matches R07's photostat exactly, including the $470 Ravel Brothers figure) — while explicitly professing total ignorance of the ghost legend growing up around the engine's old name and home. This is the strongest possible independent confirmation that the "ghost of Number Nine" legend (R01) is false: the real engine's own family's own records show her living an entirely ordinary, undramatic second life 200 miles away, oblivious to the story built around her supposed death.
- **New (R07): the curator's own open questions**, useful as an explicit list of what remains unresolved as of 1970: (1) whether the Item 1/Item 2 wrapper attributions are swapped (J. Rennick vs. A. Rennick); (2) whether Item 6 (the Bly County photostats) should be described in the Pell County catalogue at all, or simply cross-referenced to Bly County; (3) accession numbers still needed for Item 4 (the pedestal); (4) the date and circumstances of the *Ninestone Sentinel* newspaper file's own deposit into the Society, which the curator cannot find on any slip.

## MASTER CONFLICT/ODDITY CHECKLIST (updated, for quick lookup)

1. **Bearing-replacement year:** 1953 (R03) vs. 1954 (R02, R04, R07, R08) — now 4-against-1.
2. **Expansion rule:** 1 inch per 50°F (R02) vs. 1 inch per 40°F (R04/R07, primary field-book quote, doubly confirmed).
3. **Sheet 11 spec:** 4 inches of travel (R02) vs. 3 inches (R04/R07, quoted multiple times).
4. **Re-decking year:** 1908 (R02) vs. 1909 (R03's own quoted note, R04, AND R07's direct transcription — now triple-confirmed) vs. 1911 (R03's own spoken narration — self-contradictory).
5. **Sound onset year/cause:** 1897–98, caused by the (fictional) wreck (R01, folk legend) vs. 1912, caused by the 1909 re-decking's miscalibration (R04, evidence-based) vs. 1913 (R03's spoken account) vs. 1907 (R03's own earlier statement) vs. 1909 (R08's claim about when Judd went on nights) — now a four/five-way conflict.
6. **What the sound actually was:** ghost of Number Nine and her drowned crew (R01, folk belief) — refuted by R02 (no deaths, engine sold and later scrapped) — independently confirmed refuted by R05 (the actual engine's ordinary 27-year second life and 1929 scrapping, from the buyer's side) — refuted/explained mechanistically by R04/R07 (bridge shoe binding and releasing under thermal stress, precisely calculated).
7. **Duration claimed:** "seventy-odd years" (R01) vs. actual span implied by other sources (~1909/1912 to 1953/54, i.e., roughly 41-47 years, matching Judd's stated "forty-three winters").
