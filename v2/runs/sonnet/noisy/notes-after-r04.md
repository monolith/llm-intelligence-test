# Notes on Retellings 01–04 (the "Sallow Creek / Cadder Falls ghost sound" set)

All four documents concern the same real-world puzzle: a mysterious deep booming sound heard at night near Cadder Falls, on the Cadder Valley Railroad (CVR), for roughly four decades, and four different narrators' accounts/explanations of it, recorded at different times. Below: full cast, full timeline, all quotes verbatim, all numbers, and every conflict/oddity found — within and between the four retellings.

---

## THE FOUR SOURCES (narrator, format, date recorded/written)

1. **R01 — "What the Valley Heard."** Narrator: **Vesta Cobb**, a mill hand's daughter, born **1920** in Cadder Falls (second house up from the mill gate). Format: oral-history tape recorded at her kitchen table for the county's oral-history collection, **1974**. She heard the sound herself as a child; presents the town's ghost-story belief as fact.

2. **R02 — "A Clerk's Son Remembers" (chapter "The Sallow Creek Viaduct").** Narrator: **Harlan Pike**, son of the CVR's chief clerk. From his unpublished memoir *"The Company at Ninestone,"* written in his seventies, **1958** (earliest-written of the four, predates R03/R04). Debunking/company-records account.

3. **R03 — "The Night Book."** Narrator: **Judd Rennick**, retired station agent, interviewed at his kitchen table for the Pell County Historical Society, **1965**. He is the one who logged the sound for 43 winters. Empirical/statistical account, admits he has no explanation.

4. **R04 — "What the Creek Gave Back."** Narrator: **Ruth Frayne**, civil engineer (Pell County highway department, 3 years' experience). A talk delivered to the **Pell County Historical Society**, read at Ninestone, **1963** (so chronologically this talk predates Judd's 1965 interview and Vesta's 1974 tape, though it comes fourth in our reading order). Her creek inspection was "last September" relative to 1963, i.e. ~Sept 1962. Presents a full engineering explanation, corroborated by an independent weather record.

Chronological order of composition: R02 (1958) → R04 (1963, talk) → R03 (1965, interview) → R01 (1974, tape). Note R02 (1958) predates Ruth's investigation, so Pike never knew of / responds to her findings.

## FULL CAST AND RELATIONSHIPS

- **Vesta Cobb** — narrator R01; born 1920, Cadder Falls; father worked the mill his whole life; mother's people at the Falls since the rail line was built (grandmother boarded the construction workers). Husband said she "could talk the paint off a porch."
- **Harlan Pike** — narrator R02; his (unnamed) father was **chief clerk of the Cadder Valley Railroad from 1893 to 1919** (26 years). Pike wrote the memoir in his 70s (1958).
- **Judd Rennick** — narrator R03; born **1888**, at Cadder Falls. Station agent (39 years total at the depot). Father: **Emil Rennick**, a carpenter who built the falsework (temporary timber supporting the iron until it could stand alone) for the Sallow Creek Viaduct — proud of it, never credited. Emil's younger sister (Judd's aunt): **Adela Rennick** — worked for the same railroad before Judd was born, in the engineering party; never discussed it with Judd. Judd went on nights in 1907 (per his own early statement, he "started the book that same winter" — but see CONFLICT list, this contradicts his own later narrative that the book began after the sound first spoke in 1913). Judd is described in R01 as living into his 80s, still in town when "the young Frayne woman" (Ruth) came round; R01 says she was his **"grand-niece."** In R04, Ruth calls him **"my father's uncle"** (i.e., her father's uncle) — see oddity below re: exact genealogical relationship.
- **Ruth Frayne** — narrator R04; civil engineer, Pell County highway department. Father: **Wendell Frayne**. Grandmother: **Adela Rennick** (born 1874, died 1959, age 85) — Adela's field book passed to Wendell Frayne on her death, then to Ruth. So Ruth is Adela's granddaughter, and Judd Rennick (Adela's nephew, per R03) is called by Ruth "my father's uncle" — genealogically Judd and Wendell would actually be first cousins (both one generation below Adela/Emil), not uncle/nephew, so the exact family relationship between the Rennick and Frayne lines is a little inconsistent/loosely stated across sources (R01 says "grand-niece," R04 says "father's uncle"). Not fully resolved — flag as ambiguity, not a hard contradiction.
- **Adela Rennick** — engineering-party "computer" (and rodman, per R04) for the CVR while the viaduct was being built. Per R02: "did the arithmetic, and she did it better than the party did" (Pike's father's own phrase). Kept her own duplicate field book in black oilcloth, separate from the official party book — thought eccentric. Her employment ended when the engineering party was reduced; two others from the party were rehired in the following August for the Ashby extension work, she was not. Per R02, Pike believes (without documentary proof, just "the temperature of a room") that an entry she wrote criticizing the bridge got back to resident engineer Warren Tice, who "never forgave" it, and she was gone within four years. Per R04, one such entry is preserved (see Quotes section) — she reported the pedestal was set to the wrong (2", not 3") mark and was overruled by "W.T." (Warren Tice). Born 1874, died 1959 (age 85).
- **Warren Tice** — resident engineer on the viaduct construction, later the CVR's "chief engineer at Ninestone," described as "a considerable man in this county" (R02). Referred to as "W.T." in Adela's field-book quote (R04).
- **Dorsey Tice** — storekeeper at "Dorsey Tice's" store by the tracks at Cadder Falls; nephew of "old Warren Tice" (R01). The Tices were "railroad people out of Ninestone, engineering people, big in the company"; Dorsey's storekeeping was seen by his family as "a comedown," which he didn't mind. Known for the best hard candy in the county, weighed heavy for children (R01 only). Lost a $5 bet to Judd over whether the sound would occur on a specific night (see Wager below); told the story on himself for the rest of his life.
- **Mr. Coyle** — foreman of the August 1909 bridge-repair (re-decking) gang; quoted in Judd's night-book note (R03) saying "the deck is forty ton heavier than it was."
- **Peter Wexler** — began a volunteer daily weather record (high/low/precipitation) at Kettle Bench, starting **1 January 1894**.
- **Lettie Wexler** — Peter's daughter; continued the weather record from **1926** onward; lent all four cardboard boxes of records to Ruth Frayne without asking why. Explicitly stated as unrelated to anyone else in the story and never recorded any sound.
- **Locomotive No. 9** — a 4-4-0 built by the **Rowan Locomotive Works in 1889**, works number **1142**, cost the CVR **$8,600** new. Central to the ghost legend (R01) and to its factual refutation (R02, R04).

## THE PHYSICAL SETTING (consistent across sources)

- **Cadder Valley Railroad (CVR)** — 31 miles total (R02).
  - **Ninestone** — milepost 0; county seat; company office, the elder Pike's desk.
  - **Sallow Creek Viaduct** — milepost 22.
  - **Cadder Falls** — milepost 23 (mill town).
  - **Ashby quarry** — milepost 31 (the reason the railroad was built at all).
- Grade from the quarry down to the viaduct: **2.2%** ("two and two-tenths percent"), held constant the whole way (R02). R03 adds this is "down the nine miles off the quarry" — consistent, since milepost 31 (quarry) minus milepost 22 (viaduct) = 9 miles.
- **Sallow Creek Viaduct structure** (R02): begun April 1896; opened to Cadder Falls **14 November 1897**. Three spans: 120 ft + 300 ft (center, pin-connected truss, over the gorge proper) + 120 ft = **540 feet total**, **97 feet above the water**.
- Thermal mechanics: center span pinned solid at the **north** end; the **south** end free to travel, resting on a nest of rollers in a cast pedestal (the "south shoe"). Steel lengthens in heat, shortens in cold.
- **The span was closed 12 September 1897** at thermometer **66°F** (R02; R04's grandmother's book gives the precise time, **3:40 p.m.**, matching the date/temp exactly).

## THE 1898 WRECK — AND THE GHOST LEGEND IT SPAWNED

- **R01's legend (folk belief, stated as fact by Vesta):** Locomotive **Number Nine** went into the gorge "in the bad winter when the bridge was new," her whole crew went in with her, all were killed, and none were ever recovered — "they are down there yet, under the gravel and water." The sound is supposedly Number Nine's ghost still running the grade and unable to stop. Onset: "that same winter, the first winter after they opened that bridge — that would be ninety-seven going into ninety-eight" (winter 1897–98), immediately after the wreck. Duration given: "seventy-odd years."
- **R02's factual account (Pike, using company/newspaper records) directly refutes this:**
  - Date: **6 March 1898**, 7:20 a.m. ("twenty minutes past seven") — in daylight, not night.
  - A **work train** (not a passenger/regular run) came down off the quarry grade without enough brakes and **went off the iron at the north approach of the new viaduct** — i.e., never went into the gorge at all.
  - **Nobody was killed.** Two of the gang were hurt. Took 9 days to rerail.
  - Verbatim newspaper quote, *Ninestone Sentinel*, 8 March 1898: *"No lives were lost. Two of the gang are hurt, and the engine is on her side at the north end of the new viaduct, where she will be got up within the fortnight."*
  - Locomotive No. 9 specs: 4-4-0, Rowan Locomotive Works, built 1889, works no. 1142, cost $8,600 new.
  - No. 9 was **sold 19 June 1901** to the **Tolliver Lumber Company**, Bly County (200 miles north), for **$2,150**, "delivered on her own wheels" (i.e., she steamed away under her own power). Verbatim ledger quote (Pike's father's handwriting): *"Locomotive No. 9, 4-4-0, Rowan Works 1889, works no. 1142, sold this day to the Tolliver Lumber Company, Bly County, for two thousand one hundred and fifty dollars ($2,150.00), delivered on her own wheels."*
  - Pike: "three years after she is supposed to have been lying at the bottom of Sallow Creek with her dead."
- **R04 adds the locomotive's final fate:** Ruth wrote to the Bly County Historical Rooms (200 miles north), which hold the Tolliver Lumber Company's books (deposited 1938 by the company's bookkeeper, apparently unconsulted since). Photostats confirmed: stock-book entry of 19 June 1901 (matches R02 exactly), plus a **shop-book entry of May 1929 recording No. 9 cut up (scrapped) at Sixmile**. "She left this valley alive and she died two hundred miles from it."
- **R03's separate refutation angle:** Judd counted deaths at Cadder Falls to test the folk theory that "she rang for deaths" (i.e., someone would die within a week of the sound). Over the 43 winters he kept the book: **190 total deaths** at Cadder Falls; only **7** fell within a week after a night the sound occurred. He notes this ratio (7/190) without further comment, but it's presented to undercut the omen legend.

## THE 1908/1909 RE-DECKING — CONFLICTING DATES, CONVERGING PHYSICS

- **R02 (Pike) says the re-decking was "in 1908":** bridge gang on structure "some weeks that summer"; 85-lb steel replacing 60-lb; new ties throughout; both shoes jacked and re-set; finished deck **40 tons heavier**. Pike's own theory (his only stated opinion in the chapter): the added weight overloaded the roller nest, and "the noise the Falls made so much of began somewhere about that time and not before it."
- **R03 (Judd's night-book quote, copied off the depot slate) says "August 1909":** *"Bridge gang here three weeks, August 1909. New 85-lb. steel, new ties, both shoes jacked and set again to the mark. Thermometer on the north pier read 86 at four o'clock when they seated the south nest. Mr. Coyle says the deck is forty ton heavier than it was."* — **Immediately after quoting this, Judd's own narration says "That was the summer of 1911,"** an internal contradiction within R03 itself (the note says 1909, his spoken recollection says 1911).
- **R04 (Ruth, citing Judd's book) also says August 1909**, matching the note's date, not Judd's spoken "1911": "a bridge gang that was on the structure for three weeks in August 1909... thermometer on the north pier reading eighty-six degrees at four o'clock when they seated the south nest." (Ruth's paraphrase omits Coyle's name, but is clearly the same note R03 quotes directly.)
- **CONFLICT:** three different years for the same re-decking event: **1908** (R02), **1909** (R03's own written quote AND R04), **1911** (R03's spoken recollection, contradicting its own quoted note).
- Both R02 and R03/R04 agree: 85-lb steel replacing 60-lb, new ties, both shoes jacked/re-set, **40 tons heavier**.

## THE ENGINEERING EXPLANATION (Ruth Frayne's arithmetic, R04)

- Ruth's dry-creek inspection (creek diverted 11 weeks by a flood-control project) had **four findings**:
  1. **No locomotive in Sallow Creek.** Walked the bed 800+ feet above/below the viaduct — no frame, boiler shell, driving wheel, "nothing of iron larger than a spike." Photographs "on the table at the back."
  2. **The pedestal.** Found 40 feet below the abutment on the south side, in the gravel "where the 1954 gang threw it over" — the old south shoe/roller-nest pedestal, "the size of a kitchen stove." A chisel mark (the original erection "setting mark") on the roller seat measures **2 inches** (checked three times with a steel tape, same result each time). The 1954 replacement bearings are **rockers** (not rollers) measuring **4 inches** of travel each way (also measured by Ruth).
  3. **Grandmother's field book** (Adela Rennick's, in black oilcloth) — passed Adela → Wendell Frayne (1959, on Adela's death) → Ruth.
  4. **The night book** — lent to Ruth directly by Judd Rennick himself (74 years old at the time, living three streets from the depot).
- **Ruth's math, using her grandmother's stated rule** (see CONFLICT below re: which rule):
  - Rule: 1 inch of travel per 300 ft of span per **40 degrees** F of temperature change.
  - Sheet 11 (the design spec) called for **3 inches** of cold-weather travel = 120°F of allowable cooling.
  - Span closed at 66°F → as *designed*, the bridge would only run out of travel at **66 − 120 = −54°F** (never reached in the valley) — so the design, if built correctly, would never have failed.
  - But it was actually built to the **2-inch** chisel mark (not 3) = 80°F of allowable cooling → **66 − 80 = −14°F** — still colder than the valley reaches, which is why nothing happened for the first 12 years (1897–1909).
  - **August 1909:** the gang re-jacked the shoes back to the *same* 2-inch mark, but did so with the steel at **86°F** (not the original 66°F). New threshold: **86 − 80 = 6°F above zero.**
  - This **6°F threshold matches Judd's night-book finding exactly** (sound only occurs when night's low falls below 6°F). Mechanism: when the span cools past its now-miscalibrated binding point, the south shoe "hangs up" (can't slide further), stress builds in the truss, then the shoe "lets go all at once" and the span drops into position — producing one sharp report that travels along the rails. Ruth's line: **"It was not a train. It was a bridge, arriving where it should have been."**
- **Quoted entries from Adela Rennick's field book (R04):**
  - Front matter: *"Rule for the long span: one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees."*
  - Page 62: *"Sept. 12, 1897. Span closed 3:40 p.m., thermometer 66°. Rollers set at the two-inch mark. Sheet 11 calls for three. Reported to W.T., who answers that the pedestal cannot be recast before the fall haul and that two inches is more than the valley will ever ask for. Entered here so that it is somewhere. — A.R."*
- **Closing quote of Ruth's report** (written on the tailgate of her truck): *"The south pedestal was recovered from the creek bed forty feet below the abutment, where the 1954 gang threw it. The chisel mark on the roller seat is at two inches. Sheet 11 calls for three. There is no locomotive in this creek and there never was one. What the valley heard for forty-three winters was a bridge letting go of itself, one inch late."*
- Ruth's closing line: *"My grandmother measured that bridge and was not believed. I measured it again sixty-five years later, with a better tape and worse handwriting, and she was right. That is all I have."* (1897 + 65 = 1962, consistent with her creek visit "last September" before the 1963 talk.)

### CONFLICT — the expansion rule itself (40 vs 50 degrees per inch)
- **R02 (Pike):** "The rule the engineers worked to was one inch in three hundred feet for **fifty** degrees." Also: "Sheet 11 of the contract drawings called for **four inches** of travel on the south nest. Four inches was generous..."
- **R04 (Adela's field book, quoted twice):** "one inch in three hundred feet for **forty** degrees" and "Sheet 11 calls for **three** [inches]" (both in the Sept 12, 1897 entry and in Ruth's closing report quote).
- These are two direct, unreconciled numeric conflicts between R02 and R04: the rule-of-thumb (40° vs 50° per inch) and what Sheet 11 actually specified (3" vs 4"). Note Ruth's entire mathematical proof (the -54°F / -14°F / 6°F chain) depends on the 40°-rule and 3"-spec numbers from her grandmother's primary-source field book, not Pike's secondhand company-memory numbers — so if a reader must pick which is more authoritative, R04's is a primary document (an actual period field-book quote) vs. R02's secondhand recollection, but this is not stated explicitly by either narrator; it's left as a discrepancy for the reader to notice.

## JUDD RENNICK'S NIGHT BOOK (R03) — FULL DETAIL

- Judd went on nights in 1907; per his early statement "started the book that same winter" (1907) — but see contradiction below.
- Five columns kept: date, day's high, night's low, whether she spoke, and the hour.
- **Sound description (Judd, R03):** one deep report; not a rattle, not a roll; didn't travel — "there and then it was done." Order of perception: the telegraph sounder on his table rattled in its bracket first (before the sound reached the room) → then the floor moved → then the noise itself. He timed the gap once with a watch and "could not get a reading on it, it is that short," but the order never varied across 43 winters. Always between midnight and 4 a.m., never evening, never daylight.
- **First occurrence, per Judd's spoken account:** "the winter of 1913" — he was alone on nights, thought a rail car had gotten loose in the quarry, went out with a lantern (cold enough that the lantern glass cracked), found nothing wrong, and "in the morning I ruled up a book." **This directly contradicts his own earlier statement that he started the book in 1907** (see CONFLICT section).
- **Two conditions, found empirically after reviewing the book, no exceptions in 43 winters:**
  1. Night's low must be **below 6°F** (never at 7°, never at 6.5° — strictly below 6).
  2. The day before must have been **at least 30°F warmer** than the following night's low (a fall of 30+ degrees).
- **Totals:** 61 nights she spoke; 34 winters with at least one occurrence; 9 winters with none (34+9 = 43, matches the "forty-three winters" total). 5 additional nights met both conditions but produced nothing — so 66 nights total met the conditions (61 spoke + 5 silent).
- **Judd's only guess (offered explicitly as speculation, not fact):** the sound wouldn't repeat twice within the same freeze — once it had spoken, it stayed silent until a "real thaw" (above 40°F daytime). Each of the 5 silent-but-qualifying nights occurred within a cold spell where the sound had already spoken and no thaw above 40°F had intervened.
- **The gang note** (quoted in the front of his book, copied off the depot slate — see re-decking section above for full quote and the 1909/1911 internal contradiction).
- **Death-omen count:** "she rang for deaths" was the town belief. Judd counted: 190 total deaths at Cadder Falls during his book-keeping years; 7 fell within a week after a sounding. He states the two numbers "and I have never said them at the store."
- **The wager (January 1937), full detail:**
  - Setting: Dorsey Tice's store, mid-afternoon.
  - Temps: 41°F at noon; 38°F at 4 p.m.; falling 4°F/hour steadily since 1 p.m.
  - Judd's calculation: 38 down to 6 = 32 degrees; at 4°F/hour = 8 hours → under 6°F around midnight. Day's high (41°F) to sub-6°F is a fall >30°F. No thaw since the second week of December, so the bridge was "rested" (eligible to sound again).
  - Judd predicted she would speak before morning; Dorsey bet him **$5** she would not.
  - Actual low that night: **3 below zero.** She spoke at **10 minutes past 1 a.m.** (1:10 a.m.).
  - Dorsey paid the $5 the next day, in the store, in front of everybody (unprompted by Judd); told the story on himself for the rest of his life.
  - **(R01's version of the same event, secondhand):** "Bet him she would speak on a certain night and she spoke. Five dollars, I always heard, and five dollars then was five dollars." No date or temperature detail given in R01 — consistent with but far less detailed than R03's firsthand account.
- **The rockers (bearing replacement):** "the rockers went in, in the **spring of 1953**" (R03) — new rocker bearings with **4 inches of travel each way** installed; old cast pedestals thrown over the abutment into the creek. Judd, the agent, was not told directly — found out from a section man.
  - **CONFLICT:** R02 (Pike) dates this rehabilitation to **March 1954** ("the road was rehabilitated for diesel power and the old bearings under the center span were taken out and new ones put in... And the noise... stopped, that year, and never came back"). R04 also refers to "**the 1954 gang**" that threw the pedestal into the creek. So R03 says 1953; R02 and R04 both say 1954 — a direct one-year conflict, with R03 as the outlier.
  - Judd's final observation: the **second winter after the rockers**, three nights occurred that were under 6°F with falls >30° — and there was no sound on any of them. He wrote his last note and stopped:
    > *"Second winter since the rockers went in. Three nights under six degrees, three falls of better than thirty, and not a sound out of her either winter. I have kept this book forty-three winters and I do not know now what it was I was listening to."*
    (Note: the phrase "either winter" is slightly odd/ambiguous given he's describing only "the second winter" with three qualifying nights — possibly implying he's summarizing across both the first and second post-rocker winters, but the first winter's data isn't given explicitly.)
  - Judd explicitly disclaims having any explanation: "I have not got one word of explanation, and I am not going to make one up at my age."
- Judd's own summary of the railroad geography (matches R02): quarry milepost 31, Falls milepost 23, bridge milepost 22, grade 2.2% down the nine miles off the quarry.
- **The Frayne girl (Ruth) came "a while back,"** sat at his table, read the whole book through without talking (which he appreciated), asked to take it away; he let her have it.

### CONFLICT — when Judd started the book / when the sound began
- R03 (early in his own account): "I went on nights in 1907 and I started the book that same winter" → implies book started **1907**.
- R03 (later in the same account): "The first time she spoke was the winter of **1913**... In the morning I ruled up a book" → implies the book started **1913**, prompted by the first occurrence. This is an internal contradiction within R03 itself.
- R04 (Ruth, using the independent Wexler weather record cross-checked against Judd's book): the sound's actual onset — the first night meeting both conditions after the August 1909 re-decking — was **17 January 1912**, quoting the Wexler log: *"Jan. 17. High 34. Low −2 at daylight. Clear, still."* Ruth states explicitly: "That is the night the sound began, and the night my father's uncle started his book."
- **Net conflict: three different start dates for the sound/book — 1907, 1912, and 1913** — across and within these two sources. R04's version is the most evidentially grounded (tied to an independent, dated weather record and to the physics), but no source reconciles the discrepancy explicitly.
- Separately, R01 (Vesta, secondhand folk account) claims the sound began in **winter 1897–98**, immediately after the (fictionalized) wreck — a totally different era from the 1909–1913 window that R03/R04 establish. This is the biggest cross-source conflict: R01 ties onset to the 1898 wreck; R03/R04 tie onset to the 1909 re-decking, roughly 11-14 years later.
- R01 also states the sound persisted "seventy-odd years" — if onset were 1897-98 and end 1953-54, that's about 56 years, not "seventy-odd" — a further internal-numeric looseness in R01 (unless Vesta is speaking loosely/rounding, or her elders' oral tradition simply exaggerated the span).

## THE WEXLER WEATHER RECORD (R04, independent corroboration)

- Kept at Kettle Bench. Started **1 January 1894** by **Peter Wexler**; continued by his daughter **Lettie Wexler** since **1926**. Lent to Ruth in four cardboard boxes, no questions asked. Explicitly stated: the Wexlers are unrelated to anyone else in the story and never recorded any sound themselves — pure weather data.
- Their record **agrees with Judd's book on all 61 nights** the sound occurred (independent cross-validation).
- **Pre-re-decking window (Nov 1897 – Aug 1909):** 7 nights at Kettle Bench were below 6°F, 4 of those also had falls of 30°+ — i.e., met both of Judd's later-discovered conditions — yet **nothing was heard on any of them**, and "nobody at the Falls has ever claimed there was." This is presented as key proof: the original (over-generous, −54°F threshold) design would never fail in this valley, so nothing happened despite qualifying cold nights.
  - Coldest reading in that 12-year window: **−9°F on 4 February 1899** — colder than typical but still short of −14°F (the threshold implied by the as-built 2-inch/66°F setting) and far short of what the corrected 6°F-threshold (post-1909) would require.
- **Two winters immediately after the re-decking** (1909–1911ish) never got below **+9°F**; the coldest reading in that span was on **3 February 1911**.
- **First night under 6°F after the August 1909 re-decking: 17 January 1912** — quoted: *"Jan. 17. High 34. Low −2 at daylight. Clear, still."* (Fall from 34 to −2 = 36 degrees, also satisfying the 30-degree-fall condition.) Presented as the actual onset night of the sound.
- Extremes across the whole Wexler series: highest reading **101°F, July 1936**; lowest reading **−13°F, January 1918**.

## THE 1911 NINESTONE FIRE (R02 only — not mentioned in any other retelling)

- **4 April 1911:** the roundhouse and office at Ninestone burned. Pike's father watched it from across the yard and never again cared for the smell of a coal fire. Destroyed: all construction records of the road — contract files, erection time books, the entire account of how the viaduct was built and by whom. Pike: "I do not think anybody at the time understood that a fire could take away a thing that had already happened." (Note: this means the *company's own* copy of the records was lost — but Adela's *private duplicate* field book survived independently in her family, which is precisely why Ruth's grandmother's book in R04 is the only surviving primary documentation of the original construction/setting details — a detail not stated outright by any narrator but inferable from combining R02 and R04.)

## MISCELLANEOUS DETAILS AND ODDITIES

- R01: Someone at the store proposed "ice in the gorge" as a rival theory; Vesta rebuts it: "why not every cold night, why the killing nights only" — the ice theory can't explain the sound's selectivity, which (unknowingly, from Vesta's perspective) foreshadows Judd's/Ruth's precise threshold-based explanation.
- R01's qualitative description of a "killing night" (a soft/warm day, then the sky "went hard and clear" and cold at dark) matches, in lay terms, Judd's/Ruth's quantitative 30-degree-fall condition — thematically consistent even though R01 gives no numbers.
- R01 specifically frames the sound as one report described by Vesta's mother as: **"Like a door shut in a cellar three towns off."** (Direct quote, attributed to Vesta's mother via Vesta.)
- R01: the town's blamed cause for the sound stopping: "the railroad had spoiled it... Somebody had come from off with money and taken it away from us."
- R03: Judd's description of the felt/heard order is more granular than R01's — telegraph sounder rattle, then floor, then sound — a detail no other source mentions (R01 only mentions floor-then-sound, no telegraph detail).
- Age check: R04 (1963) describes Judd as "seventy-four." R03 (1965 interview) has Judd say "I was born in eighty-eight" (1888). 1963 − 1888 = 75, so "seventy-four" in 1963 is off by about one year (plausible if his birthday fell later in the year) — minor, not a hard conflict.
- Genealogy oddity (restated): R01 calls Ruth Frayne "his [Judd's] grand-niece"; R04 has Ruth call Judd "my father's [Wendell Frayne's] uncle." Given R03 establishes Judd's aunt is Adela, and R04 establishes Adela is Ruth's grandmother (via Wendell), the precise generational math doesn't cleanly produce "grand-niece" — flagged as an unresolved ambiguity in the family tree across sources, not explicitly reconciled by any narrator.
- Warren Tice appears as: resident engineer on the original construction, later CVR chief engineer at Ninestone (R02); as "W.T." in Adela's field-book quote who overrules her pedestal-setting objection (R04); and as uncle of storekeeper Dorsey Tice (R01) — consistent thread tying the engineering family to the store/wager anecdote.
- The name "Coyle" (foreman, 1909 gang) appears only in R03's direct quote, not named in R02's or R04's paraphrases of the same event.
- Steel upgrade specifics (60-lb → 85-lb rail/decking steel) are consistent between R02 (describing 1908) and R03/R04 (describing 1909) — the *steel spec* details match even though the *year* is disputed.
- "Forty tons heavier" (post-re-decking deck weight) is consistent across R02, R03 (Coyle's quote), and R04.
- R02's own explanatory theory (added weight overloading the roller nest) is superseded/contradicted by R04's more precise thermal-binding explanation — both blame the same re-decking event, but for different physical reasons (weight vs. miscalibrated thermal travel threshold). Neither source acknowledges the other's theory (R02 predates R04's investigation).
- Pike's (R02) closing line, asserting the sound was purely local to Cadder Falls: **"We never heard it at Ninestone. My father would have written it down."**

## MASTER CONFLICT/ODDITY CHECKLIST (for quick lookup)

1. **Bearing-replacement year:** 1953 (R03) vs. 1954 (R02, R04).
2. **Expansion rule:** 1 inch per 50°F (R02) vs. 1 inch per 40°F (R04, primary field-book quote).
3. **Sheet 11 spec:** 4 inches of travel (R02) vs. 3 inches (R04, quoted twice).
4. **Re-decking year:** 1908 (R02) vs. 1909 (R03's own quoted note AND R04) vs. 1911 (R03's own spoken narration — self-contradictory).
5. **Sound onset year/cause:** 1897–98, caused by the (fictional) wreck (R01, folk legend) vs. 1912, caused by the 1909 re-decking's miscalibration (R04, evidence-based) vs. 1913 (R03's spoken account) vs. 1907 (R03's own earlier statement about when he started the book) — three-to-four-way conflict.
6. **What the sound actually was:** ghost of Number Nine and her drowned crew (R01, folk belief) — refuted by R02 (no deaths, engine sold and later scrapped) — refuted/explained mechanistically by R04 (bridge shoe binding and releasing under thermal stress, precisely calculated).
7. **Duration claimed:** "seventy-odd years" (R01) vs. actual span implied by other sources (~1909/1912 to 1953/54, i.e., roughly 41-45 years, matching Judd's stated "forty-three winters").
8. **Judd's age at time of Ruth's visit:** "seventy-four" (R04, 1963) vs. birth year 1888 stated by Judd himself (R03), which would make him ~75 in 1963.
9. **Genealogical relationship** between Judd Rennick and Ruth Frayne: "grand-niece" (R01) vs. "my father's uncle" (R04) — doesn't cleanly reconcile with Adela being Judd's aunt and Wendell (Ruth's father) presumably being Adela's son.
10. **The wager:** consistent core event (Judd vs. Dorsey, $5, Judd right) between R01 (vague, secondhand) and R03 (detailed, firsthand, dated January 1937) — no real conflict, just difference in detail level.
11. Internal R03 self-contradiction on when he started the book (1907 vs. 1913 — see #5).
12. Internal R03 self-contradiction on the re-decking year (August 1909 per his own quoted note vs. "summer of 1911" in his own narration — see #4).
13. Minor phrasing oddity in Judd's final quoted note: "not a sound out of her **either winter**" while only describing "the second winter" in detail — ambiguous whether he means both post-rocker winters or is misspeaking.
