## Section A — Reconstruction

### A1. The building of the Sallow Creek Viaduct

The Cadder Valley Railroad (CVR) ran 31 miles from Ninestone (milepost 0, the county seat and company office) to the Ashby stone quarry (milepost 31), the reason the line existed. Cadder Falls, a mill town, sat at milepost 23; one mile short of it, at milepost 22, the line crossed Sallow Creek on a three-span steel viaduct — 120 ft, a 300-ft pin-connected center span over the gorge, and 120 ft, 540 feet in all, carried 97 feet above the water. Construction began April 1896. Emil Rennick, a Cadder Falls carpenter (b. 1866), built the falsework — the temporary timber staging that held the iron truss until it could stand on its own. Warren Tice was resident engineer.

The center span's south end rode on a roller nest in a cast pedestal, free to slide as the steel expanded and contracted (the north end was fixed). The governing rule was one inch of travel per 300 ft of span per 40°F of temperature change, and the contract drawings (Sheet 11) specified 3 inches of cold-weather travel for the south nest. Emil's younger sister, Adela Rennick (b. 1874), worked the job as the party's "computer" — she did the arithmetic and also ran a rod in the field, unusual for the crew — and kept a private field book alongside the company's official one.

The span was closed on 12 September 1897 at 3:40 p.m., thermometer 66°F. Adela's book records the rollers were actually set to only a 2-inch mark. She reported this to Tice, who overruled her: the pedestal could not be recast before the fall lumber haul, and two inches was, in his judgment, more travel than the valley would ever ask for. The first train reached Cadder Falls 14 November 1897. Nothing went wrong for twelve years — even the undersized 2-inch setting left the bridge a theoretical margin down to −14°F, colder than the valley's winters reached.

### A2. The night book and what was heard

On the morning of 6 March 1898, a runaway work train came down off the Ashby quarry grade and derailed at the viaduct's north approach — in daylight, on dry rails, nowhere near the gorge. Nobody was killed; two gang members were hurt (one's arm broken, one scalded), confirmed by both a contemporary newspaper account and Adela's own field-book entry; the engine was rerailed within nine days. Over the following years the town's telling inflated this into a wreck that threw Locomotive No. 9 and her whole crew into the gorge, none ever recovered — and when an unexplained deep boom began to be heard on the coldest nights, the town took it for that engine's ghost.

In August 1909 a bridge gang re-decked the span with heavier rail (85 lb replacing 60 lb) and new ties — adding roughly 40 tons of dead load — and re-jacked both shoes back to the *same* 2-inch mark, but this time with the steel at 86°F rather than the original 66°F. That silently moved the bridge's failure threshold from a theoretical −14°F up to a practical +6°F, a temperature the valley's winters actually reached. The first night meeting the new threshold was 17 January 1912 (high 34°F, low −2°F at daylight) — the true onset of the sound, fourteen years after the wreck, not the winter immediately after it.

Judd Rennick (Emil's son, b. 1888), night operator from 1909 and station agent from 1915, began a ruled log that winter and kept it 43 winters: date, day's high, night's low, whether the bridge spoke, and the hour. Two conditions held without exception: the night's low below 6°F, and the day's high at least 30°F warmer. The bridge spoke 61 nights across 34 winters; 5 further nights met both conditions and gave nothing. Judd also tested and demolished the folk belief that the sound "rang for deaths": of 190 deaths in Cadder Falls during his years of keeping the book, only 7 fell within a week of a sounding — far too few to support the omen. The last sounding was 9 February 1954; the bridge was rebuilt with new bearings the next month and was never heard again.

### A3. The locomotive's second life

Locomotive No. 9 — a 4-4-0 built by the Rowan Locomotive Works in 1889, works no. 1142, costing the CVR $8,600 new — survived the 1898 derailment well enough to keep working. The CVR's own stock book records her sold intact on 19 June 1901 to the Tolliver Lumber Company of Bly County, 200 miles north, for $2,150, delivered under her own steam over eleven days and four railroads. (Tolliver's own ledger instead records paying $2,510; the two company records disagree and neither is independently confirmed against the other.)

Silas Tolliver (founder, b. 1849, d. 1922) put her to work on the Sixmile tram, a 19-mile logging line; the shop repainted her "9" as "4." She struggled one season on the tram's steep grades, then thrived 27 years on the straight main stem: boiler retube 1909, new tires 1913, a cracked frame welded 1919 and again 1922, a full rebuild 1924. In September 1928 a newer secondhand engine arrived and No. 4 was set out under a tarpaulin. In May 1929 she was cut up at Sixmile and sold for scrap to Ravel Brothers for $470; her boiler shell became a stationary wash-water tank at the mill.

The Tolliver company wound up in 1936. In 1938 Pearl Tolliver Nace — Silas's daughter, the company's bookkeeper for 39 years, and the one who had originally spotted the CVR's sale advertisement — personally drove the company's stock book, shop book, timber ledgers, and eleven boxes of correspondence to the Bly County Historical Rooms, where they sat uninspected for 24 years until Ruth Frayne's 1962 inquiry connected them back to the Sallow Creek story: the engine the valley believed dead in its creek had lived on, unaware of its own legend, for three more decades of logging work two hundred miles away.

### A4. The 1962 survey and what it established

In September 1962 a state flood-control project diverted Sallow Creek for eleven weeks, and Ruth Frayne — Pell County highway department civil engineer, Adela Rennick's granddaughter through her father Wendell — used the dry bed to settle the story. Walking 800-plus feet of creek bed above and below the viaduct, she found no locomotive: no frame, boiler, wheels, or iron larger than a spike. Forty feet below the south abutment, in the gravel where the 1954 repair gang had thrown it, she recovered the original cast pedestal and found on its seat a chisel mark measuring exactly 2 inches, checked three times.

That physical fact confirmed what her grandmother's field book (borrowed from her father Wendell, who had kept it since Adela's 1959 death) had recorded 65 years earlier: the bridge was built to a 2-inch mark, not the 3 inches the drawings specified. Cross-referencing that figure and the expansion rule against Judd Rennick's 43-winter night book (which Judd lent her personally that year) and an entirely independent record — the Wexler family weather log at Kettle Bench, lent by Lettie Wexler — she reconstructed the mechanism: the 1897 setting gave a margin down to −14°F, never reached in the valley, but the August 1909 re-decking re-seated the same undersized bearing at a warmer temperature, shifting the margin to +6°F, a threshold the valley's winters crossed repeatedly over the next 43 years. She wrote it up as a September 1962 county survey report, recovered the pedestal as physical exhibit, and later told the Historical Society: "It was not a train. It was a bridge, arriving where it should have been."

**B4.** Warren Tice — the resident engineer who built the bridge and personally overruled Adela's objection about the roller setting. It matters to January 1936 because the man betting Judd $5 the bridge would stay silent was, unknowingly, the son of the engineer whose own uncorrected bearing setting — and its 1909 re-seating — was the actual physical cause of the sound he was betting against.

**B5.** All four record strands passed through Ruth Frayne's hands in 1962, but by four different routes, and none of them became her permanent property that year:
- **Field book** — Adela → her son Wendell (on Adela's 1959 death, kept on a shelf) → put directly into Ruth's hands by Wendell when she began her survey; formally donated to the Historical Society only in 1963.

So the "one person's hands" in 1962 was Ruth's — as a temporary convergence for her investigation, not a custodial one; each original went back to (or toward) a different final resting place, years apart.

## Section C — Math

**C1.** Rule used: 1 inch per 300 ft per 40°F (Adela's field book, quoted independently by R04 and R07 — see E1 for why this rule, not the 50°F rule in R02/R09, is used: it's the one that correctly predicts the observed threshold below). Span closed at 66°F.
- (a) As drawn (3 in.): 3 × 40 = 120°F of allowable cooling → 66 − 120 = **−54°F**.
- (b) As actually built (2 in.): 2 × 40 = 80°F allowance → 66 − 80 = **−14°F**.
- (c) After August 1909, re-seated at 86°F to the same 2-in. mark: 86 − 80 = **+6°F**.
- (d) Margin cost: threshold moved from −14°F to +6°F → **20 degrees** of margin lost (6 − (−14) = 20).

**C2.** 
- (a) 38°F at 4 p.m., threshold 6°F, falling 4°F/hr: (38 − 6) / 4 = 8 hours → 4 p.m. + 8 h = **about midnight (12 a.m.)**.
- (b) Day's high 41°F, night's low 3 below zero (−3°F): fall = 41 − (−3) = **44 degrees**. That exceeds the 30-degree minimum, so **yes, the second condition was satisfied**.

**C3.** The book runs 43 winters, from winter **1911–12** (first night heard, 17 Jan 1912 — the night the Wexler record independently confirms as the first to meet both conditions after the 1909 re-decking) through winter **1953–54** (last night heard, 9 Feb 1954; Judd retired that June). I use 61 nights heard / 5 silent-but-qualifying, per the night book's own back-page summary as directly quoted by the curator (R07) and matching Judd's own 1965 account (R03) — see E1 for why this figure is used over the 57-night figure shared by two other narrators.
- (a) 61 / 43 = **1.4** nights per logged winter.
- (b) 61 + 5 = **66** nights that met both conditions, heard or not.
- (c) 61 / 34 (winters with at least one sounding) = **1.8**.
- Excluded: three further qualifying-but-silent nights reported in "the second winter since the rockers went in" (winter 1955–56, per the book's final dated entry, 2 March 1956). These fall after the 43-winter span and after the March 1954 fix that ended the phenomenon; they're recorded as a confirmatory epilogue, not part of the 43-winter count.

**C4.**
- (a) Built 1889, cut up May 1929 → **40 years old**.
- (b) Original cost $8,600. Sale price is unresolved between two primary-side ledgers (seller vs. buyer — see E1): $2,150 (CVR ledger) → 2150/8600 = **25.0%**; $2,510 (Tolliver ledger) → 2510/8600 = **29.2%**. I cannot pick one over the other from the sources; both computations are shown.
- (c) **27 years**, per R05's own explicitly stated total, consistent with its stated 1902 purchase date (1929 − 1902 = 27). Note this rests on the 1902 date; using the CVR's dated 1901 ledger entry instead gives 28 years — see E1.

**C5.**
- (a) 120 + 300 + 120 = **540 feet**.
- (b) Quarry (milepost 31) to viaduct (milepost 22) = 9 miles (arithmetic from the mileposts, matching R02/R03 directly; R09's "eight miles" is an outlier — see E1). 9 mi × 5,280 ft/mi = 47,520 ft. 47,520 × 0.022 = **1,045.4 feet**.
- (c) Record high 101°F (July 1936, agreed everywhere) minus record low −11°F (January 1918 — resolved in favor of R06/R07 over R04/R11's −13°F; see E1) = 112°F range. Using the 40°F-per-inch rule for the 300-ft span: 112 / 40 = **2.8 inches**.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** **Necessary but not sufficient, both jointly.** Across all 43 winters and 61 soundings, there is no exception on record where the bridge spoke without the night's low being below 6°F *and* the prior day's high being at least 30°F warmer — so both conditions are necessary. But they are not sufficient: 5 nights (or 8, per the conflicting count in R06) met both conditions and produced nothing. R09's memo (para. 14) supplies the reason this isn't a contradiction: once the bearing releases, it sits at the far end of its travel with nothing left to give, and can only be reset by a daytime thaw above roughly 40°F. A qualifying night inside an unbroken cold spell, with no such thaw beforehand, is therefore silent even though it meets both logged conditions — the two conditions are necessary triggers for readiness, not a guarantee of another release.

**D2.** Three independent reasons the sound cannot have been Locomotive No. 9, each from a separate documentary chain:
1. **The CVR's own 1901 sale ledger** (R02, echoed in R07's photostat): the engine was sold whole and steamed away under her own power on 19 June 1901 — alive and mobile three years after the "wreck" that supposedly killed her.
2. **The contemporary 1898 newspaper account** (*Ninestone Sentinel*, 8 March 1898, quoted in R02/R07): nobody died; the engine derailed at the north approach, not in the gorge, and was rerailed within a fortnight — this directly contradicts the legend at its source, before any later investigation.
3. **The Tolliver Lumber Company's independent buyer-side records**, in a different county entirely (R05): the engine, renumbered No. 4, worked 27 documented years hauling logs 200 miles north and was scrapped in 1929, with maintenance records (retube, new tires, two frame welds, a rebuild) that only make sense for a working machine, not one sitting in a creek bed.
These are independent because they come from three separate archives kept by three unrelated parties (the CVR itself, a public newspaper, and a private lumber company in another county) with no shared chain of custody. Reasons 1 and 3 do not depend on searching the creek at all — either would still hold **even if the 1962 survey had never been made**.

**D3.** **No — the objection did not cause her departure.** Pike's theory (R02) that Tice "never forgave" the field-book entry is explicitly offered as unproven speculation. The primary document — the CVR's own 30 April 1901 letter, quoted in full in both R07 and R10 — shows the *entire* engineering party, not Adela alone, was let go effective 15 May 1901 as a routine reduction in force, and that Tice himself asked that a positive recommendation be entered on her record. Her son Wendell (R10), who holds the letter, directly rebuts the grudge theory and also explains the one fact that seemed to support it (two other party members were rehired months later for the Ashby extension, she was not): the rehires were a rodman and chainman for field work, and she was the party's only computer — a role the smaller extension crew didn't carry. A document plus the family's own considered account outrank an unproven secondhand suspicion.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The sound was the sudden release of a thermally over-stressed bridge bearing, not a ghost and not (primarily) a static overload. The center span's south end rode on a roller nest meant to travel 3 inches with temperature, per the rule of 1 inch per 300 ft per 40°F; it was actually built in 1897 to a 2-inch mark, which — closed at 66°F — still left an unreachable margin down to −14°F, so nothing happened for twelve years. In August 1909, a re-decking crew re-seated the same undersized bearing to the same 2-inch mark, but did the work with the steel at 86°F instead of 66°F, which moved the practical failure point up to +6°F — a temperature the valley's winters actually reached. Below that threshold the bearing had no travel left; the span kept contracting anyway, storing strain in the truss, until the bearing let go all at once, discharging that stored strain as a single sharp release — hence one deep, non-repeating report, transmitted efficiently through the continuous rail (registering in the depot's telegraph sounder and the floor before it was heard as sound), never resembling a bell or an exhaust. The second condition — a fall of 30°F or more in a day — is required because a slow decline lets the bearing creep in small increments, dissipating strain continuously; only a rapid fall outruns the bearing's ability to relieve itself, so strain accumulates instead. Some qualifying nights were silent because once released, the bearing sits at the end of its travel with nothing left to give; it can only be reset by a daytime thaw of some hours above roughly 40°F, so consecutive cold nights within one unbroken cold spell stay silent. The sound began in 1912, not 1897, because the *original* 1897 setting (even though undersized) still had enough margin never to bind; only the 1909 re-seating, at a warmer ambient temperature, narrowed that margin into the valley's actual climate. It stopped permanently in March 1954 because the rehabilitation for diesel power replaced the roller nest with rocker bearings offering 4 inches of travel each way — a margin no valley temperature on record can exhaust.

**The competing explanation the sources themselves offer for the onset** is the folk legend: that Locomotive No. 9 and her crew died in the gorge in the 1898 wreck, and the sound was the dead engine or her ghost, present from that first winter. It fails on several independent grounds: the contemporary 1898 newspaper account and Adela's own field-book entry agree nobody died and the engine derailed at the north approach, not the gorge; the CVR's own 1901 sale ledger shows the engine sold whole and steamed away under her own power; the Tolliver company's independent records trace her working 27 more years, 200 miles away, until scrapped in 1929; and, decisively, the empirical onset date (17 January 1912, fixed by the Wexler weather record) falls fourteen years after the wreck and lines up precisely with the 1909 re-decking instead — a date the ghost story cannot explain at all. (A secondary, non-ghost alternative appears in R09: that added 1909 dead load, not bearing travel, caused the binding. This is offered only as unproven "opinion," and it does not explain the specific empirical signature — the sharp 6°F cutoff, the 30-degree-fall requirement, or the need for an intervening thaw — that the travel-exhaustion mechanism accounts for exactly.)

**Physical evidence recovered in 1962 confirming the theory:** the cast pedestal, found 40 feet below the south abutment, bearing a chisel mark on the roller seat measuring exactly 2 inches — direct physical proof of the as-built (not as-designed) setting that the mechanism depends on — together with the complete absence of any locomotive wreckage in 800-plus feet of exposed creek bed, ruling out the competing theory.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** A 1897 railroad bridge over Sallow Creek was built with an undersized bearing; an engineer overruled the one worker who documented it. For twelve years nothing happened. A 1909 repair re-set the same flawed bearing at a warmer temperature, and from 1912 the bridge began releasing built-up strain on sharp cold nights with one deep boom — which the valley, remembering a real but harmless 1898 derailment, blamed on a "ghost" locomotive that had actually been sold, renamed, and was working two hundred miles away. It took four separate records — a carpenter's-sister's field book, a station agent's 43-winter log, a neighboring farm's weather diary, and a 1962 engineering survey — kept by people who mostly never spoke to one another, to reassemble the true, unhaunted mechanism.
