*Compiled from r01–r12 read in sequence, from memory, no re-reading.*

## Section A — Reconstruction

### A1. The building of the Sallow Creek Viaduct

The Cadder Valley Railroad (CVR) ran 31 miles from Ninestone (milepost 0, the county seat) to the Ashby quarry (milepost 31). Its principal structure, the Sallow Creek Viaduct at milepost 22, was begun in April 1896: three spans — 120 feet, a 300-foot pin-connected center truss, and 120 feet — totaling 540 feet, 97 feet above the creek. The line opened to Cadder Falls (milepost 23) on 14 November 1897. The resident engineer was Warren Tice, later the CVR's chief engineer at Ninestone; Emil Rennick built the timber falsework; the party's "computer" (rodman and calculator) was Emil's younger sister, Adela Rennick.

Steel expands and contracts with temperature, so the center span was pinned solid at its north end and left free at the south, riding on a nest of rollers in a cast pedestal. The governing rule, recorded in Adela's own field book, was one inch of travel per 300 feet for every 40°F of change. Sheet 11 of the contract drawings called for 3 inches of travel in the south nest. The span was closed on 12 September 1897 at 3:40 p.m. with the steel at 66°F — but the rollers were set to only a 2-inch mark, because, per Adela's book, the pedestal could not be recast before the fall haul. She reported the shortfall to Tice ("W.T."), was overruled, and wrote it down anyway "so that it is somewhere." A family story passed down at two removes (Emil to Adela to her son) has Tice privately admitting at his 1931 retirement dinner, "we set that nest a mark shy and I have listened to it ever since"; his son Dorsey Tice denies this was ever said.

In August 1909 a bridge gang spent three weeks re-decking the structure: new 85-lb rail (replacing 60-lb), new ties, and both shoes jacked and reset — to the same 2-inch mark, but with the steel now at 86°F. The new deck was 40 tons heavier. This reseating at a different reference temperature is what set up the "boom" heard for the next 43 winters (see A2). The bridge's original roller nest served, without anyone recognizing the defect for what it was, until March 1954, when the railroad, rebuilding for diesel power, replaced it with rocker bearings good for 4 inches of travel each way.

### A2. The night book and what was heard from the bridge

Valley folklore held that Cadder Falls could hear its dead: a single deep report, felt through the floor before it was heard, on the coldest nights — the ghost of Locomotive No. 9 and her crew, supposedly lost in the gorge when the bridge was new. That belief traced to the runaway of 6 March 1898, in which No. 9 left the rails at the north approach — but killed no one, and was rerailed within nine days.

The real sound was real and sharply patterned. Station agent Judd Rennick (Adela's nephew, son of Emil) kept a ruled ledger for 43 winters — date, day's high, night's low, whether the bridge spoke, and the hour — from the first occurrence on 17 January 1912 (independently confirmed by the unrelated Wexler family's weather record at Kettle Bench: high 34°F, low −2°F) through the last, 9 February 1954. Two conditions held without exception: the night's low had to fall below 6°F, and the day-to-night drop had to be at least 30 degrees. Both were necessary; together they still weren't sufficient — 5 nights met both and nothing happened, because the bearing could only release once per cold spell and needed an intervening thaw (roughly 40°F or above) to reset. Over the 43 winters: 61 nights heard, 34 winters with at least one occurrence, 9 with none; 66 nights in all met both conditions. Of 190 deaths recorded in Cadder Falls over that span, only 7 fell within a week of a sounding, enough to disprove the older belief that the bridge "rang for a death." A well-known wager (a storekeeper betting $5 against the agent that the bridge would not speak, and losing) rested entirely on this same arithmetic.

The true mechanism, established in 1962, was the undersized 1897 roller nest, reset in 1909 at a warmer reference temperature — which raised its effective binding point from a temperature the valley never reached to one it reached most winters. The sound stopped for good when the bearings were replaced in March 1954.

### A3. The locomotive's second life

Locomotive No. 9, a 4-4-0 built in 1889 by the Rowan Locomotive Works (works no. 1142), cost the CVR $8,600 new. She survived the 1898 runaway well enough to be rerailed within nine days and worked another three years on the CVR. On 19 June 1901 the company sold her — "delivered on her own wheels" — to the Tolliver Lumber Company of Bly County, 200 miles north, for $2,150, an entry preserved in the CVR's own stock book and independently corroborated by a photostat obtained in 1962.

Tolliver wanted a road engine fast enough to outrun his geared, timber-climbing locomotives on the mill's straight main stem; his bookkeeper daughter Pearl found the CVR's advertisement. The engine steamed north under her own power — eleven days, four connecting railroads. Tolliver repainted her number: the 9 became a 4, and she was known thereafter as "the Four." (A Tolliver family memoir recalls the purchase as 1902 for $2,510; this is very likely later embellishment — see Section E.)

She struggled for a season on grades built for geared engines, then found her place on the main stem and became, in her granddaughter-memoirist's words, "an institution": boiler retube 1909, new tires 1913, a cracked frame welded twice (1919, 1922), a full overhaul in 1924. In September 1928 a second-hand eight-coupled engine replaced her; she sat out that winter under a tarpaulin. In May 1929, at 40 years old, she was cut up at Sixmile — Tolliver's own shop book records "Scrap to Ravel Brothers, $470." Her boiler shell became a stationary water tank at the mill. She was never within 200 miles of the gorge she was rumored to haunt.

### A4. The 1962 survey and what it established

In September 1962, an eleven-week flood-control drawdown emptied Sallow Creek to the gravel, and Ruth Frayne — a Pell County highway engineer, and Adela Rennick's granddaughter — used the dry bed to inspect the viaduct's piers. She walked roughly 800 feet of the creek bed and found no locomotive: no frame, boiler, wheels, or cab iron, nothing larger than a rail spike, ending decades of belief that No. 9 lay wrecked in the gorge.

Forty feet below the south abutment she recovered the original cast-iron pedestal — the south shoe — discarded there by the 1954 bridge gang. On its roller seat she measured, three times, a chisel mark at 2 inches: physical confirmation of what her grandmother's field book had recorded in writing 65 years earlier — the nest had been set 1 inch short of the 3 inches Sheet 11 specified.

Combining that physical evidence with three documentary sources — Adela's field book, Judd's 43-winter night book, and the Wexler family's independent weather record — Frayne worked the arithmetic: as built in 1897 the nest would not have bound until −14°F; after the 1909 re-decking, reset to the same mark but at a warmer reference temperature, it would bind at +6°F — exactly the threshold in Judd's book. The Wexler record confirmed no night between 1897 and 1909 had reached −14°F, explaining the silence until 17 January 1912, and confirmed the two conditions matched night-for-night. She reported to the Pell County Historical Society in 1963: "What the valley heard for forty-three winters was a bridge letting go of itself, one inch late."

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Siblings — Emil (b. 1866) was the older brother, Adela (b. 1874 or 1876, see E1) the younger sister. Stated directly by both Judd ("my father had a younger sister, Adela") and Wendell ("My mother, Adela Rennick, was his younger sister").

**B2.** Aunt and nephew — Judd was Emil's son, and Emil was Adela's brother, so Judd was Adela's nephew. Established independently by Judd himself and by Wendell's genealogy. The one thing that "points elsewhere" is cosmetic, not genealogical: the Historical Society's wrapper on Adela's field book is initialed for "J. Rennick" and the wrapper on Judd's night book is initialed "A. Rennick" — the curator flags this as an unresolved swap. But the actual contents settle it: the field book is signed "A.R." throughout and the night book is in Judd's own hand and voice (confirmed in his own 1965 interview). The mislabeled wrappers are an archival mixup, not a genealogical claim, and don't affect the aunt/nephew relationship.

**B3.** First cousin once removed. Derivation: Ruth's father Wendell and Judd were first cousins (Wendell's mother Adela and Judd's father Emil were siblings). One generation below a first-cousin pair makes Ruth and Judd first cousins once removed. Town folklore (r01) called her his "grand-niece" — wrong; Wendell's own note explicitly corrects this and explains the error's likely source: Ruth called Judd "uncle" as a child and he never corrected her.

**B4.** Warren Tice. This matters to January 1936/37 only indirectly: the wager itself (Judd vs. Dorsey, $5, resolved by temperature arithmetic) had nothing mechanically to do with Warren Tice. What it does explain is the personal stake behind everything else Dorsey says in the same interview: Dorsey is the son of the engineer whose 1897 decision (overruling Adela, setting the nest short) is, on the theory this test settles on, the actual cause of the sound Dorsey spent his life betting on and telling stories about — and Dorsey is also the one flatly denying that his father ever confessed to it. Note also an internal contradiction in Dorsey's own account: he opens by insisting his father "had no hand in that bridge" and "was in the Ninestone office the whole time it was building," then later in the same interview recalls his father telling him personally, "he had closed the span himself in ninety-seven." Every other source (r02, r04, r07, r09, r10, r12) calls Warren Tice "the resident engineer" on the job — a role that by definition means on-site. Dorsey's opening denial looks like a defensive overstatement, contradicted by his own later memory and by everyone else.

**B5.** All four converged on Ruth Frayne in 1962 by four separate, independent routes:
- **Field book** (Adela's) — family inheritance: sat on a shelf at Adela's house from 1901 (when she stopped writing in it) until her death in 1959, passed to her son Wendell, who handed it directly to his daughter Ruth in September 1962 when she began the survey.
- **Night book** (Judd's) — personal loan: Judd Rennick lent it to Ruth himself in 1962 and she returned it to him afterward (it entered the Historical Society only after his death in 1969).
- **Weather record** (Wexler's) — personal loan from an unrelated family: Lettie Wexler gave Ruth all four boxes of it in 1962, unasked for a receipt.
- **Bly County ledgers** — written request: Ruth wrote to the Bly County Historical Rooms, where the Tolliver Lumber Company's books had been deposited in 1938, and they returned photostats of the relevant stock-book and shop-book entries.

None of the four required any of the others — the field book came through blood, the other three through Ruth's professional survey work.

## Section C — Math

**C1.** Rule: 1 inch of travel per 300 ft of span per 40°F (Adela's field book — the primary source; see E1 for the conflicting "50°F" figure I rejected). Span closed at 66°F.

(a) As drawn (Sheet 11: 3 inches): 3 × 40 = 120°F of cooling capacity. 66 − 120 = **−54°F**.

(b) As actually set in 1897 (2 inches): 2 × 40 = 80°F. 66 − 80 = **−14°F**.

(c) After the August 1909 re-seating (still 2 inches, but steel at 86°F): 2 × 40 = 80°F. 86 − 80 = **+6°F**. (This matches Judd's empirical threshold exactly — no exceptions in 43 winters.)

(d) Margin lost: from −14°F to +6°F is a shift of 6 − (−14) = **20 degrees**. The 1909 work made the bridge bind at a temperature 20 degrees warmer than before — moving the trigger from a cold this valley essentially never reaches to one it reaches most winters.

**C2.** Station thermometer: 38°F at 4:00 p.m., falling 4°F/hour. Threshold: 6°F.

(a) 38 − 6 = 32 degrees to fall; at 4°F/hour that's 32 ÷ 4 = 8 hours. 4:00 p.m. + 8 hours = **about midnight**. (This is Judd's own arithmetic, done at the counter, and the sound actually came at 1:10 a.m. — close enough, given the rate could ease off overnight.)

(b) Day's high 41°F, night's low −3°F. Fall = 41 − (−3) = **44 degrees**. Yes — 44 ≥ 30, so the second condition was satisfied, comfortably.

**C3.** Using the primary/directly-sourced figures (Judd's own 1965 count and the curator's 1970 direct transcription of the physical book both agree: 61 nights heard, 34 winters with ≥1 occurrence, 9 winters with none, 5 further nights met both conditions and were silent, 66 total qualifying nights).

(a) 61 nights ÷ 43 winters = 1.4186… → **1.4**

(b) **66** nights (61 heard + 5 silent-but-qualifying)

(c) 61 nights ÷ 34 winters-with-≥1 = 1.7941… → **1.8**

The count runs over the winter of **1911–12** (first occurrence 17 January 1912, fixed by cross-reference to the independent Wexler weather record) through the winter of **1953–54** (last occurrence 9 February 1954, fixed by the curator's direct transcription of the book's own summary page). That span is 43 winters inclusive, matching every account of "forty-three winters."

Excluded: the 3 further nights, in the two winters *after* the March 1954 bearing replacement, that met both conditions and produced nothing — these post-date the 43-winter record and are a separate confirming note appended afterward, not part of the tally the book's own summary covers. Also excluded (as unreliable secondhand figures, not genuine alternative data): the "57 nights / 30 winters spoke / 13 silent" figures given by the storekeeper and the school pamphlet, and the "8 silent nights / 69 total" figures given by the weather-keeper's recollection and the county engineer's memo — both pairs are explicitly flagged in their own sources as secondhand or half-remembered, against two mutually-consistent primary accounts.

**C4.**

(a) Built 1889, cut up May 1929: 1929 − 1889 = **40 years old**.

(b) Original cost $8,600 (agreed by two sources). Sale price to Tolliver: $2,150 (company stock-book entry, corroborated by an independently obtained photostat — see E1 for the conflicting $2,510 figure, which I reject). 2,150 ÷ 8,600 = **25%**. (The clean round number is itself evidence the $2,150 figure is the correct one — $2,510 gives an untidy 29.2%.)

(c) Delivered 19 June 1901; set aside from active main-stem service in September 1928 (replaced by a second-hand eight-coupled engine; she wasn't physically scrapped until May 1929, but that's shop time, not working time). 1928 − 1901 = **27 years**. This matches the buyer-side memoir's own claim of "twenty-seven years" — which only works arithmetically from an 1901 delivery, not the 1902 purchase date that same memoir states elsewhere, reinforcing that 1901 is correct.

**C5.**

(a) 120 + 300 + 120 = **540 feet**. (Two sources round this to "six hundred feet" — see E1; 540 is the exact sum of the stated spans, given identically by the two most technical sources.)

(b) Grade 2.2% over the distance from the quarry (milepost 31) to the viaduct (milepost 22) = 9 miles (31 − 22), matching Judd's own direct statement ("two and two-tenths percent down the nine miles off the quarry"). One source (a memo by someone who never worked the line) says "eight miles"; I reject that single, unsupported figure in favor of the milepost arithmetic plus a firsthand local witness. 9 miles × 5,280 ft/mile = 47,520 ft. Fall = 0.022 × 47,520 = **1,045.4 feet** (≈1,045 ft).

(c) Highest recorded: 101°F (July 1936 — agreed by three sources). Lowest recorded: −11°F (January 1918 — the weather-keeper's own account and the curator's citation of the actual index sheet agree on this; one source says −13°F, which I reject — see E1). Range = 101 − (−11) = 112°F. At 1 inch per 40°F: 112 ÷ 40 = **2.8 inches**.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** Both conditions were **necessary but not jointly sufficient**. Necessary: in every logged instance where the bridge was heard, the night's low was below 6°F *and* the day-to-night fall was at least 30 degrees — no exceptions, cross-checked night-for-night against an independent weather record. Not sufficient: 5 further nights met both conditions and nothing was heard. The missing piece was state, not temperature — once the bearing released, it stayed released until an intervening thaw (roughly 40°F+) returned it to its "cocked" position; a second qualifying night within the same unbroken cold spell had nothing left to release. So the two logged conditions are necessary triggers for a *possible* release, but whether a given qualifying night actually produced a sound depended on a third, unlogged factor: whether a thaw had reset the mechanism since the last release.

**D2.** Three independent reasons, with independence noted:

1. **The 1898 wreck killed no one, and the engine did not stay in the gorge.** Established by a contemporary newspaper notice (8 March 1898), Adela's own field-book entry from the same week, and an eyewitness (Emil Rennick, on the ground that morning) — all predating and independent of the 1962 survey. This alone rules out "a dead crew" as the source.
2. **The engine was sold intact and working in 1901, and worked another 27 years two hundred miles away**, per the CVR's own sale ledger and the Tolliver company's own shop book — again fully independent of, and predating, the 1962 survey.
3. **The timing doesn't fit.** If the sound were the wreck settling, it should have started immediately in 1898. Instead, cross-referencing Judd's book against the unrelated Wexler weather record shows twelve full years (1897–1909) with several nights cold enough to plausibly qualify and total silence, with the sound only starting in January 1912 — tracking the August 1909 re-decking, not the 1898 wreck. This is a documentary/statistical argument, independent of the physical dig.
4. *(Confirming, but not independent of the survey itself)* The 1962 physical inspection of ~800 feet of creek bed found no locomotive at all.

Reasons 1–3 would still hold even if the 1962 survey had never happened, since none of them depend on digging in the creek bed — they rest on documents and records that predate 1962.

**D3.** Most likely no — or at least, not provably. The dated, documented cause of Adela's 1901 departure is a letter stating the *entire* engineering party was reduced, with her time paid through 15 May 1901. Two colleagues (a rodman and a chainman — field-labor roles) were rehired in August 1901 for separate extension work, but the "computer" position she held was never filled again by anyone — consistent with the whole party, including a position that had no successor, simply being eliminated, rather than her being singled out. The theory that she was pushed out specifically over the 1897 objection comes from a single source (Harlan Pike's memoir), who explicitly labels it his own inference from "the temperature of a room," not documented fact, and it is directly and specifically rebutted by her own son, citing the letter itself. The four-year gap between the objection (1897) and the departure (1901) also weakens a direct retaliation reading. I don't think the sources let you rule the suspicion out entirely — Pike clearly believed it, based on something he witnessed as a boy — but the only document in evidence describes a general staff reduction, and the family's own record disputes the alternative reading. On balance: no clear causal link is established, and the documented explanation is sufficient on its own.

## Section E — Contradictions

Below, "primary" means a document or a direct participant's own account; "secondary/secondhand" means a narrator explicitly relying on others' memory, town talk, or a source they admit not consulting directly.

1. **Viaduct total length:** 540 ft (r02, r09 — exact sum of the three stated spans) vs. 600 ft (r11, r12 — rounded). *Resolved by arithmetic* (120+300+120=540); the two more technical sources agree on the exact figure. **Two narrators (r11, r12) share the same wrong rounded value.**

2. **Movement coefficient:** "1 inch per 40°F" (r04 and r07, both directly quoting/transcribing Adela's own field book) vs. "1 inch per 50°F" (r02's memoir; r09's memo, which admits relying on secondhand reports). *Resolved by document outranking memory*: the primary field book, examined independently by two different people, says 40°F; and only 40°F reproduces the observed 6°F threshold exactly. **Two narrators (r02, r09) share the same wrong value** — though they may not be fully independent, since r09 explicitly says it worked from secondhand reports of the kind r02 represents.

3. **Sheet 11's specified travel:** 3 inches (r04, r07 — both quoting the field book/survey report) vs. 4 inches (r09 alone). *Resolved by the two-source primary quote*; r09's figure looks like a mix-up with the 1954 rocker bearings' unrelated 4-inch spec.

4. **Date of the re-decking work:** August 1909 (Judd's own quoted slate note in r03, and matching quotes in r04, r07, r09 — the same primary note cited four times) vs. "1908" (r02) vs. "the summer of 1911" (r03's own narrative aside, moments after quoting "August 1909" — an internal self-contradiction). *Resolved in favor of 1909* by the repeated, word-for-word primary quotation; r02 and r03's own aside are both memory slips.

## Section F — Theory

The sound was a bridge bearing releasing under stored strain, not a train, a corpse, or a bell. The center span's south end rode on a nest of rollers meant to let the steel slide 3 inches as it cooled; at construction in September 1897 (steel at 66°F) it was set to only 2 inches, because the pedestal couldn't be recast in time — an error Adela Rennick reported and was overruled on. As built, that shortfall alone would only have bound the bearing at −14°F, a cold this valley barely brushed (−9°F in 1899) in the twelve years before 1909, so nothing happened. In August 1909 a bridge gang, doing ordinary maintenance, jacked both shoes and reset them to that *same* 2-inch mark — but this time with the steel at 86°F, not 66°F. That single change in reference temperature moved the effective binding point from −14°F to +6°F, a threshold the valley crosses most winters. That is why the sound began in January 1912, not in 1898: the mechanism wasn't in place until 1909, and the first sufficiently cold, sufficiently fast night after that came in 1912.

The character of the sound — one deep report, felt in the rails and the depot's telegraph sounder before it was heard through the air, never repeating within a night — follows directly from the mechanism: as the span cooled past its binding point, the shoe stuck; contraction continued anyway, so strain built up and stored in the truss and its pins until the bearing let go all at once, discharging that stored energy in a single sharp release, transmitted far more efficiently through continuous rail than through open air. The second condition (a fall of at least 30 degrees in a day, not just a low reading) follows too: a slow decline lets the bearing creep in small increments, bleeding off strain continuously with no big release; only a fast drop outruns that creep and lets strain accumulate. Qualifying-but-silent nights are explained by the bearing's one-shot nature: once released, it sits at the cold end of its travel with nothing left to give, and only a real daytime thaw (roughly 40°F or more) walks it back to a position where it can bind and release again. Permanent cessation in March 1954 follows from simple arithmetic: the new rocker bearings allow 4 inches of travel each way, which under the correct 40°F-per-inch rule cannot be exhausted at any temperature this valley has ever recorded.

The competing explanation offered in the sources is added dead load: the 1909 deck was 40 tons heavier, and both Harlan Pike (r02) and county engineer H. L. Quarles (r09) suggest the extra weight, not travel, bound the bearing. This fails on two counts. First, it has no mechanism for a sharp, repeatable temperature threshold — friction from a heavier deck would just make binding somewhat likelier across the board, not gate it precisely at 6°F, whereas the travel-exhaustion account, using Adela's own correct 40°F rule and the 1909 re-seating temperature, predicts +6°F exactly, matching 43 winters of data with no exceptions. Second, it has no way to explain why some qualifying nights were silent — a constant extra load can't "run out" and reset itself the way a bearing at the end of its travel does. Quarles's own memo, notably, only reaches for the dead-load theory *because* he used the wrong coefficient (50°F, copied secondhand) and the wrong Sheet-11 figure (4 inches), got a mismatched −14°F, and admitted he couldn't reconcile it with the record — a problem that disappears entirely once the correct primary-source numbers are used.

The physical evidence recovered in 1962 that confirms this theory is the cast-iron south pedestal itself, pulled from the creek bed 40 feet below the abutment, with a chisel mark on its roller seat measured three times at exactly 2 inches — physically proving the undersized 1897 setting that the mechanism, and the whole 43-winter record, depended on.

## Section G — Summary

Cadder Falls believed a ghost haunted Sallow Creek: Locomotive No. 9, supposedly wrecked with her crew in the 1898 gorge accident, "spoke" on the coldest nights. In truth nobody died in that wreck; the engine was sold running in 1901 and scrapped 200 miles away in 1929. The real cause was mechanical: an 1897 bridge bearing set an inch short of spec, re-seated in 1909 at a warmer reference temperature, so that after 1912 it bound and released with one deep report whenever a night fell below 6°F after a 30-degree drop. New bearings in 1954 ended it for good. Four separate records — an engineer's field book, a station agent's log, a farm's weather diary, and a 1962 survey — were each needed because no single one held the whole mechanism.
