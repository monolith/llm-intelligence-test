# Notes after retellings r01–r08 (Sallow Creek Viaduct / "the sound" at Cadder Falls)

Handoff notes for a fresh reader. Covers the eight retellings only (r01 Vesta Cobb, r02 Harlan Pike, r03 Judd Rennick, r04 Ruth Frayne, r05 Alma Nace, r06 Lettie Wexler, r07 Merrit Sable, r08 Dorsey Tice). The eight interleaved noise documents (Darwin, Twain, Mill, Babbage, Madison, Beeton, Austen, Melville) are deliberately excluded. Everything below is from the texts; where a reader adds an inference or arithmetic check it is marked **[my inference]** (the r01–r04 reader's inferences are carried forward unchanged and marked the same way; new ones from the r05–r08 reader are marked **[my inference, r05–r08 reader]** where it matters who made them).

## 1. People and relationships (all sources merged)

### The Rennick / Frayne family
- **Emil Rennick** — Judd's father. Carpenter. **Built the falsework for the bridge** ("the timber they stand the iron on until the iron will stand by itself. Then you knock the timber down"). Proud of it; "never got a line of credit for it anywhere." (r03) Sable (r07): "Judd Rennick's father, Emil, and Adela, who wrote the field book, were brother and sister."
- **Adela Rennick** — Emil's **younger sister** (r03). **Born 1874, died 1959 at eighty-five** (r04). Sable (r07): "**born 1876 and died 1959**" — **CONFLICT on birth year** (1874 vs 1876). "Miss Adela Rennick" (r02). Worked for the Cadder Valley Railroad in the **engineering party**, "out in the field with the men" (r03). r02: the party's **computer** ("She did the arithmetic, and she did it better than the party did" — Pike's father's phrase). r04: "**computer and rodman** for the railroad's engineering party while the viaduct was building." Kept **her own duplicate of the party's book, in her own hand** (r02: "thought eccentric and was not"); r04: "in black oilcloth, all her life." Never spoke about her railroad time to Judd — he asked twice as a boy and got nothing (r03). Entered "whatever she thought" in that book, including what she thought about the bridge; one such entry got back to the resident engineer (Tice), who "never forgave that entry, and she was gone inside four years" (r02). Her time with the road ended when the engineering party was reduced; two of the party were taken back on "in the August following, for the Ashby extension work, and she was not" (r02). **r07 supplies the documents:** letter of **30 April 1901** — party "reduced entire on the 15th May next," her time paid to that day, "**Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card**"; and a printed slip of **20 July 1901** re-engaging **J. Corliss, rodman; F. Wain, chainman** from 1 August 1901 for the Ashby extension (full text in §13). Signs her entry "**A.R.**" (r04, r07). Sable: her field-book pages "of the years after 1898 much less full than those before"; by Wendell's account "she had it in her own keeping from **1901** until her death in 1959, after which it was his" (r07). Book passed at her death to her son **Wendell Frayne**, who gave it to Ruth (r04); Sable records it as **given to the Society in 1963 by Wendell Frayne** on an unsigned slip in his hand (r07).
- **Judd Rennick** — son of Emil; **born 1888, at Cadder Falls** ("I was born in eighty-eight, here") (r03). Ruth (1963) says he **is seventy-four** and lives **three streets from the depot** he kept (r04). Vesta (1974): he "lived on and on… into his eighties, up on the hill there" (r01). Went on **nights in 1907**; "then they made me agent"; "**Thirty-nine years at that depot, all told**"; retired "a good while now" and "still wake[s] at four" (r03). **Dorsey (r08): "He went on nights at that depot in 1909, a young fellow then, and they made him agent in 1915, and he sat in that office until he retired in June of 1954."** **Sable (r07): "station agent at Cadder Falls 1915–1954, who died that year [1969]."** **[my inference, r05–r08 reader]** 1915–1954 = 39 years, which matches Judd's "thirty-nine years… all told" only if the night years are excluded; nights-from-1907 (Judd) vs 1909 (Dorsey) is a separate conflict. Died **1969** (r07; night book received from his estate that year) — at 81, consistent with Vesta's "into his eighties." Station agent who kept the **night book** (all sources). Described by Vesta as "the most ordinary man you ever saw in your life, and he had this one thing"; wrote "in that little hand of his" (r01). Dorsey: "Judd was the writing one. Judd wrote down what happened and I sold kerosene to the people it happened to"; "He was a fixture. You do not ask a fixture questions"; "in all those years I do not believe five people ever asked" (r08). Lettie (r06): "A man who keeps the same column that long, in ink, without anybody asking him to, is a man I would have got along with"; she never met him. Sable describes his hand: "A heavier, blunter pencil than Item 1 and a hand that slopes" (r07). **[oddity]** Lettie says "in ink"; Sable says pencil.
- **Wendell Frayne** — Adela's son; Ruth's father; received Adela's field book at her death (1959) and gave it to Ruth (r04). Sable (r07): "Mr. Wendell Frayne of Cadder Falls" deposited the field book in 1963; he and Judd Rennick "were first cousins"; "living" in 1970.
- **Ruth Frayne** — Adela's granddaughter; Wendell's daughter; engineer with the Pell County highway department, three years in it (1963). Calls Judd "**my father's uncle**" (r04). Vesta calls her "the young Frayne woman… his grand-niece, they told me," who went "house to house with her notebook, very polite, very quick" and was let have Judd's book (r01). Judd: "The Frayne girl came a while back and sat where you are sitting and read the whole book through at this table, and she did not talk while she read, which I appreciated. She asked to take it away with her. I let her have it. She will do something with it. I never did." (r03) Lettie (r06): "the Frayne girl came up the hill. Ruth Frayne, the engineer — she does the county's road surveys, and she was working down at the creek that fall [1962]"; "she brought every box back, which I will say for her, because not everybody does"; "it took a stranger with a survey to teach me that"; "She did not tell me [the question] and I did not ask." Sable (r07): "The author of Item 5 is the granddaughter of the writer of the field book, Adela Rennick"; "a county highway engineer."
- **[my inference — genealogy oddity]** By Judd's own account Adela is his father's younger sister, i.e., Judd's **aunt**. Adela's son Wendell is therefore Judd's **first cousin**, and Ruth is Judd's first cousin once removed — NOT his grand-niece, and Judd is NOT Wendell's uncle. Ruth's "my father's uncle" and Vesta's "grand-niece, they told me" contradict Judd's stated relationship. **Sable (r07) sides with Judd: Wendell and Judd "were first cousins."** Also see §17 on Adela's dates of service.

### The Tice family
- **Warren Tice** — **resident engineer** on the viaduct work, "afterward the company's chief engineer at Ninestone and a considerable man in this county" (r02). Almost certainly the "**W.T.**" of Adela's 12 Sept 1897 entry (r04, r07) **[my inference; neither r04 nor r07 expands the initials]**; the 30 April 1901 letter names "Mr. Tice" as asking that his recommendation be entered on Adela's card (r07). Vesta: "old Warren Tice"; the Tices "were railroad people out of Ninestone, engineering people, big in the company" (r01). **Dorsey (r08) — Warren is his FATHER:** "My father had no hand in that bridge. He was in the Ninestone office the whole time it was building… Warren Tice, his name was. He was a railroad engineer, a good one, and he finished as chief engineer of the Cadder Valley up at Ninestone, and he **died in 1937**, and the bridge is the last thing he would have wanted his name hung on." Retirement dinner **spring 1931**; a story circulates about something he supposedly said there — Dorsey was present, "He said nothing of the kind, and he never in his life spoke to me about that bridge." Yet later in the same tape: "He took me out on the deck when I was six [≈1899] and told me **he had closed the span himself in ninety-seven**… He held the back of my collar the whole time… He was proud of it. He was not a proud man about much." "A big quiet man who came home once a week when I was small and stood in the kitchen with his hat still on until my mother told him to sit. He built things all over this county and he never talked about one of them at his own table." (r08)
- **Dorsey Tice** — Warren Tice's **nephew** (r01) / Warren Tice's **son** (r08, his own account) — **CONFLICT**. **Born 1893** "right here, in the house that stood where the feed shed is now"; "Sixty-eight in March" (1961) (r08). Kept the **store "right there by the tracks"** at Cadder Falls (r01); lives "upstairs" in "this building" (r08); his family thought store-keeping a comedown, he did not; "best hard candy in the county and he weighed it heavy for children" (r01). "My father had the county in him and I never got farther than this counter, and I have not been sorry about it more than twice" (r08). Sparred with Judd over the book "for years, in a friendly way." Lost the **$5 wager** (r01, r03, r08) and "told that story on himself for the rest of his life" (r03) / "would tell it on himself, which is why people liked him" (r01) / "I have told it a hundred times and it does not change… I have made more than five dollars' worth out of telling about losing it" (r08). Never wrote anything down: "There is nothing in this store on paper but the day book, and the day book says what people owed me and nothing about bridges" (r08). Has not stood on the viaduct deck since age six "and I am not going to."
- **Dorsey's mother** (unnamed) — told Warren to sit (r08).

### The Pike family
- **Harlan Pike** — narrator of r02; son of the CVR's chief clerk; grew up "with a stock book"; sat in the office as a boy "while men talked over my head"; stood on the viaduct deck as a boy "with my father's hand in my collar." Memoir written 1958 in his seventies. **[oddity]** Dorsey's father also held him by "the back of my collar" on the deck (r08) — the same image in two sources.
- **Pike's father** (unnamed) — **chief clerk of the Cadder Valley Railroad from 1893 to 1919** ("twenty-six years"); desk "with the window on the yard" at Ninestone; wrote the No. 9 sale entry himself; regarded her selling as "a small personal wound"; watched the 1911 fire "from the far side of the yard and never afterward cared for the smell of a coal fire"; "not a man who handed out compliments to save postage." "We never heard it at Ninestone. My father would have written it down."

### The Cobb family
- **Vesta Cobb** — narrator of r01; **born 1920**, "second house up from the mill gate," Cadder Falls. Father in the mill "his whole working life." Mother's people at the Falls "since the line was built" — her **grandmother boarded the men who came in to build it** and "was here for" the wreck; told Vesta the ghost story. Husband used to say "I could talk the paint off a porch." A **brother** (up before her the night she heard it). Mother said "that is only the train, go to sleep"; mother's phrase for the sound: "Like a door shut in a cellar three towns off." Father would say at the mill gate at four in the afternoon "well, she may talk tonight," and mother told him not to say so in front of the children.

### The Tolliver / Nace family (r05, Bly County)
- **Silas Tolliver** — founder; **born 1849**; "came into this county with a mill, a horse, and a conviction that the horse was temporary." Built the mill twice, "the second time out of the first one's lumber." Kept the horse for years and complained about it. **Died 1922** "in the chair by the office stove"; the paper's obituary "led with the horse." Wanted a road engine in **1901**: "something with big wheels and a long wheelbase that would bring a made-up train of loads down the main stem **faster than a man could walk beside it**… He had watched his own timber walk for twenty years." Said of No. 4's first season: "he had bought a racehorse to pull a stump." Repeated the $8,600-vs-purchase-price comparison "for the rest of his life to anybody who would hold still." "Stopped counting, which for him was a form of praise."
- **Pearl Tolliver Nace** — Silas's daughter, Alma's mother; **born 1881**; kept the company's books **from the age of eighteen** (≈1899) "for thirty-nine years" (→ 1938) "because she was better at it than anybody her father could hire" (Silas's phrase, said in front of company). Found the CVR advertisement and "put it on the desk without a word." Wrote the $8,600 figure in the margin. Fed the Pell County delivery crew. Made the May 1929 shop-book entry. In **1938** drove the stock book, shop book, timber ledgers and **eleven boxes of correspondence** herself to the **Bly County Historical Rooms**. "She said she did not care to have a thing end twice." = the "company's bookkeeper" of r04 **[my inference]**.
- **Alma Nace** — narrator of r05; Pearl's daughter; **sixty-three in 1970** (born ≈1907); **thirty-one in 1938**. Grew up at Sixmile; "Every child raised at Sixmile could tell you where she was on the hill with their eyes shut."
- **The Pell County delivery crew** (unnamed) — brought No. 9 north "eleven days and four railroads," handed her over at the interchange, drank coffee in the Tolliver kitchen, went home "on the cars like passengers." The **fireman** — "the politest man she ever met and had a **burn on his wrist** he would not talk about" (Pearl, via r05). **[my inference, r05–r08 reader]** Possibly the "Sherrod scalded at the deck" of the 1898 wreck (r07) — pure speculation.
- **The shop foreman** (unnamed) — regarded the second frame weld (1922) "as a personal insult" (r05).
- **Ravel Brothers** — scrap dealers; took No. 4's tires and brass out separately, "being men who knew what they were about"; paid **$470** (r05, r07).

## 2. Places and the railroads

- **Cadder Valley Railroad (CVR)** — "thirty-one miles of it, and no more" (r02). Existed for the **Ashby quarry**. "Everything else the company owned was sidings." Alma: "a railroad down in Pell County, a little branch line" (r05).
- **Ninestone** — **milepost 0**; county seat; shops, office, roundhouse; Pike's father's desk; the *Ninestone Sentinel*; Historical Society talk (r04) read there; Society's premises include a **north store room** where the pedestal lies (r07). Pike: "We never heard it at Ninestone." Dorsey: a thing "that Ninestone did not have and the state road did not have" (r08).
- **Sallow Creek Viaduct** — **milepost 22** (r02, r03). Spans **Sallow Creek** gorge.
- **Cadder Falls** ("the Falls") — mill town, **milepost 23** (r02, r03). Has a mill with a "big saw," a mill gate, a depot with a platform, Dorsey Tice's store by the tracks (with a **porch thermometer** and a **sugar barrel**, r08; a **feed shed** now standing where Dorsey's birth-house stood), "the row"/"the street" of houses. Vesta's home; Judd's depot. Wendell Frayne "of Cadder Falls" (r07).
- **Ashby quarry** — **milepost 31**; "the reason the whole thing existed" (r02). Judd: "the quarry is milepost 31, the Falls is 23, the bridge is 22, and it is two and two-tenths percent down the nine miles off the quarry." **Ashby extension** work: re-engagement from 1 August 1901 (r07).
- **Grade** — **2.2 percent** from the quarry down to the viaduct, "held at that the whole way" (r02); "nine miles" (r03). Pike: "a great deal of falling for a small railroad with second-hand brakes… the company's answer was to hire careful men and pray for cold weather on the drums."
- **Pell County** — the county (Historical Society; highway department). Alma: "a railroad down in Pell County."
- **Kettle Bench** — home of the Wexler weather record (r04); "**three miles up the valley from Cadder Falls**, on the shoulder above the creek where the frost sits longer than it does in town" (r06); "three miles above Cadder Falls" (r07). The milk house with the thermometer on its north wall (r06). "The schoolhouse" is at Sixmile, not here (r05).
- **Bly County** — **two hundred miles north**; Tolliver Lumber Company; **Bly County Historical Rooms** (r02, r04, r05, r07). Alma's home county.
- **Sixmile** — Tolliver's base; where No. 9 was cut up, May 1929 (r04, r05, r07). **Sixmile Creek** gives the tram its name (r05). Has a shop, a mill, a schoolhouse, the Tolliver house and kitchen, a "main stem" (r05).
- **The Sixmile tram** (r05) — "named for the creek and not for its length"; began at about six miles; "By the end it was **nineteen miles of thirty-pound rail** running up **three drainages**, with switchbacks where the ground would not take a curve, and grades that the men described in language my mother would not enter in a book. The rail was light and the ties were whatever came off the hill nearest, and it hauled more timber than anything in this county before or since." The cut ran out in **1936**.
- **The interchange** — where the Pell County crew handed No. 9 over (r05); "eleven days and four railroads" north.
- **Rowan Locomotive Works** — builder of No. 9 (r02, r05, r07).

## 3. The viaduct: construction facts

- Begun **April 1896** (r02). Line **opened to Cadder Falls 14 November 1897** (r02); r04: "the opening of the line in November 1897"; Lettie: "the twelve years after the line opened — from the winter of 1897 and 1898 through to the summer of 1909" (r06).
- **Three spans: 120 ft + 300 ft pin-connected truss across the gorge proper + 120 ft = 540 ft** total; **97 ft above the water** (r02). Dorsey at six: "I can still feel the ties through my shoes and the creek making that noise a long way down… All that air under a boy is a thing you remember" (r08).
- Center (300-ft) span **pinned solid at its north end**; **south end free**, riding on a **nest of rollers set in a cast pedestal — "the south shoe"** (r02, r04). Ruth: "pinned at the north end and free at the south."
- Thermal rule of thumb — **CONFLICT**:
  - r02 (Pike): "**one inch in three hundred feet for fifty degrees**… For our span, being three hundred feet exactly, it came out at an inch for every fifty degrees." "You will find it in any office of that period."
  - r04 (Adela's field book, front matter, quoted) and r07 (inside cover, "a single ruled line evidently kept there for reference and transcribed here exactly"): > *Rule for the long span: one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees.* → an inch per **forty** degrees. Ruth's whole arithmetic rests on 40.
- Span **closed 12 September 1897**, thermometer **66°** (r02, r04, r07 agree); r04/r07 add **3:40 p.m.** Dorsey: his father "had closed the span himself in ninety-seven" (r08).
- **Sheet 11 of the contract drawings** — **CONFLICT**:
  - r02: called for **four inches** of travel on the south nest ("generous… showed a proper caution in whoever drew it").
  - r04 (Adela) and r07 (same entry transcribed): "**Sheet 11 calls for three.**" Ruth's report: "Sheet 11 calls for three."
  - **[my inference]** Pike's "four inches" matches the travel of the **1954 rocker bearings** ("four inches of travel each way," r03/r04) — possible conflation.
- **As built:** rollers set at the **two-inch mark** (Adela's entry; chisel mark measured by Ruth; card on the pedestal, r07).
- **Who set the nest:** Adela's entry has her reporting to "W.T.," who answers "that the pedestal cannot be recast before the fall haul and that two inches is more than the valley will ever ask for" (r04, r07). Dorsey (r08): "**Anders Berg set that nest.**" Dorsey also: his father "had no hand in that bridge… in the Ninestone office the whole time it was building," and, later, "closed the span himself in ninety-seven."
- Falsework built by Emil Rennick (r03).
- Steel "breathed on that nest, out in summer and back in winter, for fifty-odd years" (r02). Pike's odd phrase: "a valley that runs from the middle of July to the middle of January."

### Adela's field-book entry, page 62 (r04 and r07, quoted verbatim, identical in both)
> *Sept. 12, 1897. Span closed 3:40 p.m., thermometer 66°. Rollers set at the two-inch mark. Sheet 11 calls for three. Reported to W.T., who answers that the pedestal cannot be recast before the fall haul and that two inches is more than the valley will ever ask for. Entered here so that it is somewhere. — A.R.*

Ruth: "She reported it. She was overruled. She wrote it down so that it would be somewhere, and there it sat for sixty-five years while the valley told itself a ghost story." Sable: "the most substantial entry in the book, and the one the survey of 1962 (Item 5) turns on."

### Adela's field-book entry, page 71 (r07 only, quoted verbatim)
> *March 6, 1898. No. 9 off the iron at the north approach, 7:20 a.m., running away from the quarry. Nobody killed. Lidell's arm broke, Sherrod scalded at the deck. Nine days to rerail her.*

## 4. The wreck of No. 9 and the engine's later life

- r02: **Morning of 6 March 1898**, a **work train** came down off the quarry grade "without brakes enough to hold her." Locomotive **No. 9** "went off the iron at the **north approach** of the new viaduct at **twenty minutes past seven**." **Nobody was killed. Two of the gang were hurt. It took nine days to rerail her.** r07 (Adela p. 71) names the two hurt: **Lidell** (arm broke) and **Sherrod** (scalded at the deck) and adds "running away from the quarry."
- *Ninestone Sentinel* of **8 March 1898** (r02, quoted):
  > *No lives were lost. Two of the gang are hurt, and the engine is on her side at the north end of the new viaduct, where she will be got up within the fortnight.*
  Sable has seen the same account in the Society's own *Sentinel* file; the date and circumstances of that file's deposit are unknown to him (r07 query).
- Pike: "There was no crew in the gorge. The engine was not in the gorge. The engine was on her side at the north end, in daylight, with half the county looking at her."
- **No. 9** — **4-4-0** (an "American-type," r05), **Rowan Locomotive Works, built 1889, works number 1142**, cost **$8,600 new** ("a sum to be spoken of in a lowered voice," r02; "eight thousand six hundred dollars new — my mother wrote that figure in the margin of the page," r05); "the best machine we owned" (r02).
- **Sale — CONFLICT on price and year:**
  - r02: **Sold 19 June 1901** to a lumber concern in **Bly County, two hundred miles north**, for **$2,150**. CVR stock-book entry written by Pike's father (r02, quoted; r07 transcribes the same text from the photostat):
    > *Locomotive No. 9, 4-4-0, Rowan Works 1889, works no. 1142, sold this day to the Tolliver Lumber Company, Bly County, for two thousand one hundred and fifty dollars ($2,150.00), delivered on her own wheels.*
    Pike: "she steamed away north under her own power, three years after she is supposed to have been lying at the bottom of Sallow Creek with her dead."
  - r05 (Alma): "In 1901 my grandfather decided he wanted a road engine"; the advertisement was for "a **twelve-year-old** American-type engine" (1889 + 12 = 1901 **[my inference]**); "**We paid two thousand five hundred and ten dollars for her. That was in 1902.**" Repeated: "Two thousand five hundred and ten dollars she cost us, and four hundred and seventy she fetched." → **$2,510 (r05) vs $2,150 (r02, r07 photostat)** — the digits transposed; **1902 (r05) vs 19 June 1901 (stock book)**.
- **Delivery** (r05): "Delivered on her own wheels, was the phrase… It meant she came north under her own steam — **eleven days and four railroads**, with a Pell County crew who handed her over at the interchange, drank coffee in our kitchen, and turned around and went home on the cars like passengers."
- **Renumbering** (r05): "The shop painted out the 9 and painted on a 4, because the company numbered its engines in the order it bought them and never once in the order of their merit. So she was No. 4 from that season onward, and everybody called her the Four, and nobody under thirty knew she had ever been anything else."
- **Tolliver service** (r05): "a disappointment for exactly one season" — put on grades up where the geared engines belonged, "slipped and sulked and boiled her water away"; then kept on the main stem "where the ground was straight," and "she was not an engine, she was an institution." "**Twenty-seven years she worked for us**, near enough all of them on that main stem." **Boiler retube 1909. New tires 1913. Cracked frame welded 1919 and welded again 1922** ("which the shop foreman regarded as a personal insult"). **A shopping in 1924** "that took her down to her bones and put her back together, and she came out of it sounding, my mother said, exactly like herself." Her whistle "you could tell from any other whistle in the county"; "when the loads came down the main stem in the evening **you could feel her in the floor of the house before you heard her**"; "a whistle you could hear at the schoolhouse."
- **Retirement and scrapping** (r05, r07): **September 1928** the company took delivery of a second-hand **eight-coupled** engine; No. 4 "set out behind the shop… through that winter with a tarpaulin over her stack and snow banked on her running board" (men walked out to look at her; so did Alma). **May 1929** cut up. Pearl's shop-book entry (r05, "two lines long and I have it in front of me"; r07 photostat, identical):
  > *No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile this month. Scrap to Ravel Brothers, $470.*
  Ravel took the tires and brass separately; "the boiler shell went into the mill and served out the rest of its life as a stationary tank for the wash water. It was still there when the mill came down." Sable: "the engine having been set aside the previous year."
- r04 corroboration: Ruth wrote to the **Bly County Historical Rooms** (200 miles north), which hold the Tolliver Lumber Company's books, **deposited 1938 by the company's bookkeeper**, "consulted by nobody since." Photostats by return: a **stock book entry of 19 June 1901** records Cadder Valley No. 9 sold to Tolliver, delivered on her own wheels; a **shop book entry of May 1929** records her **cut up at Sixmile**. "She left this valley alive and she died two hundred miles from it." Alma (r05): the books "did not draw one single inquiry for **twenty-four years**" (1938 + 24 = 1962 **[my inference]**). Sable (r07, Item 6): the two photostats are "copies only… supplied to the 1962 survey by another institution and left with the report"; the Tolliver stock book and shop book "are **not** in this collection and have never been; they are at the Bly County Historical Rooms… deposited there in 1938, and the correspondence file shows they were catalogued on arrival. We hold the two pages and nothing else of that company."
- r01 (Vesta's version): "**Number Nine went into that gorge in the bad winter when the bridge was new, and her crew went in with her, and they were killed, and they were never brought up.** They are down there yet, under the gravel and the water… She comes down off the quarry the way she came down that morning and she cannot stop, and when she hits she makes that one sound." Told her by her grandmother, "and she was here for it."
- r04 Finding one: Ruth walked the dry bed "something over eight hundred feet, above and below the viaduct" — **no locomotive**, "no frame, no boiler shell, no driving wheel, nothing of iron larger than a spike." Photographs on the table at the back.
- r05 (Alma) on the bridge: "The county the Four came from had some noise on a bridge down there — a sound in the winters that they made stories about, and I gather there was a good deal of talk about it at one time. I never knew the rights of it and I never inquired. It was two hundred miles away and it had nothing to do with our engine, who spent those years hauling logs off three drainages in Bly County with a whistle you could hear at the schoolhouse." "Somebody else can have the bridge."
- r08 (Dorsey): "Some called it the train. I let them."

## 7. The sound itself — descriptions

- r01 (Vesta): "**It was one noise.**" One deep report, then nothing, "and the nothing after it was worse than the noise was." Not "a rattling or a rolling, like thunder walking off down the valley." "Not sharp like a shot. Deep, the way a big thing sounds when it is a long way down." Mother: "Like a door shut in a cellar three towns off." **Felt before heard** — "came up through the floorboards into your feet, the way the mill did when they started the big saw." Always the small hours: "**Between midnight and four**, and I would say **closer to two** than either end, though I would not swear to it." Never at supper or noon. Only on "**killing nights**": day soft, "shirtsleeve weather almost, mud in the road," then sky hard and clear about dark, "the whole night came to pieces on you." Could be felt coming at the mill gate at four in the afternoon.
- r03 (Judd): "It was one report. Deep. It was not a rattle and it was not a roll and **it did not travel** — it was there and then it was done. And **it came up the rails first**": the telegraph **sounder** on his table "would rattle in its bracket before the sound got to the room. A little dry rattle, like a bug in a box. Then the floor. Then the noise." Timed the gap with a watch once, couldn't get a reading; "the order never changed in forty-three winters." "Always between midnight and four. Never in the evening. Never at daylight."
- r04 (Ruth): "the sound the valley called **the boom**" (only r04 uses that word; r01/r03/r08 say "she spoke"/"she may talk"/"she will speak"); "one report that ran along the rails."
- r08 (Dorsey): "**It was one sound.** That is the first thing people get wrong — they will tell you it went on, that it rolled, that it repeated. It did not. One deep report, down low, the kind you take in through your feet before your ears catch up. **Small hours, always. I never heard it before midnight in my life** and I have been awake for a good many midnights in this building. And **never twice in the one night**. Whatever she was going to say, she said it once and was done." "You would be asleep upstairs and the floor would come up and meet you… Not a bang. A report. **Like somebody dropped something heavy two rooms away in a house made all of one piece.**" "It only ever came in the hard sudden cold — the kind of day that is **decent at dinner time and murderous by dark**. Never in a slow cold. **We could go three weeks at zero and never hear a thing**, and then one afternoon the bottom would drop out of the thermometer and every old woman on this street would say, we will hear her tonight. And **half the time we did**."
- r06 (Lettie): "He was writing down a sound in a station at **two in the morning**." Of 17 Jan 1912: "That is the kind of night the cold comes down out of the sky and just sits." Cold "not a gradual cold, a dropping cold."
- r05 (Alma, of the engine at Sixmile, not the bridge): "you could feel her in the floor of the house before you heard her" — **[oddity]** the same floor-first phrasing the Falls uses for the bridge.
- Vesta's own experience (the one she vouches for): "a winter in the thirties, and I could not give you the night." Rain that day; the wash froze on the line; mother cross. The whole street came awake at once; "the floor went first," then the sound; brother up before her; father at the window with no lamp lit; cold on the glass; **no wind at all**; dogs going off up and down the row one after another, then quitting, "as still as the inside of a cup"; doors opening; "grown men out on their porches in their union suits in that cold, holding their arms, looking up the valley toward the bridge, and not a one of them saying anything." "It was not a surprise to them. It was a visit." Mother: "that is only the train, go to sleep." Vesta: "I notice that the ones who are surest are the ones who were never in the house."
- Dorsey's own experience (wager night): "I heard her, lying up there in the dark, knowing exactly what it was costing me" (r08).
- Judd's first time: he "thought a car had got away in the quarry and come down on us," went out on the platform with a lantern, nothing wrong, "so cold the lantern glass cracked." Ruled up the book the next morning.
- Town folklore (r03): "**she rang for deaths**" — somebody at the Falls would be dead inside the week. Judd's count: **190 deaths at Cadder Falls** during the years of the book; **7** fell inside a week after a night she spoke. Numbers put in the back of the book; never said at the store. **Dorsey (r08): "he counted the dying too, against what the old women held. Better than a third of the burying in this town came inside the week after a night she spoke, he told me once, and he did not appear to think much of it either way."** → **CONFLICT**: 7 of 190 (≈3.7%, r03) vs "better than a third" (r08). Judd said he never said the numbers at the store; Dorsey says Judd "told me once."
- Store theory (r01): **ice in the gorge**; Vesta's rebuttal: "then why not every cold night, why the killing nights only." Dorsey (r08): "There was talk up here at the time about why" (Lettie, r06: "there is always talk up here about why").

## 8. Judd Rennick's night book

- **Five columns**: the date; the day's high; the night's low; whether she spoke; the hour. No opinions ("a man who writes down his opinion once will write down his opinion instead of the reading the next time"). Vesta's description: "the night, and the cold, and the hour of it." Dorsey: "Ruled it himself, columns and all… Every night she spoke he wrote it down, and the temperature with it, and what time" (r08). Lettie: "a ruled ledger where he set down every night the bridge made its noise… in ink" (r06). Sable (r07): "**Station ledger, half-bound, 'the night book'** (so called on the wrapper and by everyone who has asked after it)… Five ruled columns: the date; the day's high; the night's low; whether the sound came; the hour it came. Forty-three winters, unbroken. A heavier, blunter pencil than Item 1 and a hand that slopes." Wrapper attribution "**A. Rennick**" (swapped — see §16).
- Conditions (derived after "a good many winters"):
  1. Night's **low below six degrees** — "Never once. Not at seven, not at six and a half. Below six."
  2. Day before **at least thirty degrees warmer** than the night that followed — "It had to fall thirty… a soft day and then the bottom out of it."
  - "Both, every time. There is not an exception in the book."
  - Lettie's independent check (r06): "Every night his book says the bridge spoke, my book says the night went under six degrees. Not one exception in what I looked at. And every one of those nights came after a day at least thirty degrees warmer than the night that followed it… I took a dozen of his dates at random and turned to them in my own pages, and they agreed, every one, to the degree."
- **Totals — CONFLICT (three versions):**
  - r03 (Judd), r04 (Ruth), r07 (Sable's transcription of the back page): **61 nights** she spoke; **34 winters** with at least one; **9 winters** with none; 34 + 9 = **43 winters**. **5 nights** met both conditions and gave nothing; so **66 nights** met the conditions in all. Sable's transcription (r07, "on a page ruled off from the rest and headed only with a date"):
    > *Forty-three winters. Sixty-one nights she spoke. Thirty-four winters she spoke at least once and nine she never spoke at all. Five nights answered both the conditions and gave nothing, which makes sixty-six nights that answered them in all.*
  - r06 (Lettie, who read the book for an hour on her kitchen table in 1962): "His summing was at the back. **Sixty-one nights** she spoke, he had, over the winters he kept it. And **eight nights** answered both the conditions and gave nothing at all — cold enough, and the fall steep enough, and no sound out of her. So **sixty-nine nights** in all that answered them, by his own count, of which sixty-one spoke and eight did not." "I asked him nothing about the eight because I never met him."
  - r08 (Dorsey): "**Fifty-seven nights**, that book showed. Fifty-seven in forty-three winters. **Thirty winters** she spoke in and **thirteen** she never opened her mouth at all — thirty and thirteen, and there is your forty-three."
- **Fullest winter:** **1917–18, five nights**, "more than any other winter he kept" (r06; r07 agrees: "The fullest winter in the book is 1917–18, with five nights"). Lettie: "which surprised me not at all, since that was our own worst winter."
- **Last night entered as heard: 9 February 1954** (r07). "The new bearings went into the bridge in March 1954" (r07).
- Judd's one guess: "She would not speak twice inside one freeze." After speaking she was silent until a **real thaw — above forty in the daytime**. All five silent nights sit in a cold spell where she had already spoken with nothing above forty between. "That is not a theory about what she was. It is a rule about when." Dorsey's "three weeks at zero and never hear a thing" (r08) and "never twice in the one night" are consistent with this **[my inference, r05–r08 reader]**.
- Ruth's summary (r04): 61 nights; every low below six above zero at the bottom of a fall of 30+; "Further nights answered both conditions and produced nothing at all, and his own summing at the back of the book gives the count of them. He kept no theory. He kept columns." (**Minor oddity**: Judd says the back of the book holds the death numbers 7/190; Ruth says the back holds the count of silent nights; Sable's transcription of the back page has the silent-night count and no death figures; Lettie likewise.)
- **When the book began — CONFLICT (now four versions in four sources):**
  - r03: "I went on **nights in 1907 and I started the book that same winter**."
  - r03, later: "**The first time she spoke was the winter of 1913**… In the morning I ruled up a book."
  - r04 (via Wexler record): first night under six after August 1909 was **17 January 1912** — "That is the night the sound began, and the night my father's uncle started his book."
  - r08 (Dorsey): Judd "went on nights at that depot in **1909**… they made him agent in 1915."
  - r07 (Sable): "Forty-three winters, unbroken"; last entry dated **2 March 1956**; agent 1915–1954.
  - **[my inference]** (r01–r04 reader) 43 winters counted from winter 1912–13 through 1954–55 (the "second winter since the rockers" if rockers = spring 1953) = exactly 43, which fits "winter of 1913" read as 1912–13 and Judd's 1953 date; it fits neither 1907 nor a 1954 rocker date cleanly.
  - **[my inference, r05–r08 reader]** With Sable's date of **2 March 1956** for the last entry and rockers in **March 1954**, "second winter since the rockers" = 1955–56, and 43 winters ending 1955–56 begin with **1913–14** — fitting Judd's "winter of 1913" read as 1913–14, but NOT Ruth's 17 January 1912 (winter 1911–12, which would make 45 winters to 1955–56). No reading reconciles all four.
- Judd's **last entry** (quoted in r03 and r07, identical; r07 dates it **2 March 1956**, "Nothing after it but ruled blanks"; "two leaves further on" from the summary page):
  > *Second winter since the rockers went in. Three nights under six degrees, three falls of better than thirty, and not a sound out of her either winter. I have kept this book forty-three winters and I do not know now what it was I was listening to.*

## 9. The $5 wager (r01, r03, r08)

- r03 (Judd's detailed account): **January 1937**, in Dorsey Tice's store, mid-afternoon. "It had made **forty-one at noon**. It was **thirty-eight at four o'clock** and it had been **falling four degrees an hour since one**, steady." Judd's sum at the counter: 38 down to 6 = 32 degrees; at 4°/hr = 8 hours → under six "about midnight"; day had been 41, so a fall of "better than thirty"; **no thaw since the second week of December**, "so she was rested." Judd said she would speak before morning; **Dorsey bet five dollars she would not**. Low that night **three below**; **she spoke at ten minutes past one**. Dorsey paid in the store the next day "in front of everybody there."
- r08 (Dorsey's account): "**The fourteenth of January, 1936.** I say that without hesitation, I have told it a hundred times and it does not change." Judd came in "about four in the afternoon for kerosene — a gallon can, he carried it himself — and he sets it on this counter and he says, she will speak tonight." Dorsey: "Judd, she will not." The figures — Judd "said them twice, once to me and once to a fellow who came in behind him": **forty-one at noon; thirty-eight at four o'clock; coming down four degrees an hour since one, steady, "you could watch it on the porch thermometer."** "Judd said all three of those numbers like a man reading a bill of lading." Dorsey: "five dollars it does not." Judd: "He did not even take his hand off the can. He said, five dollars." "It went to **three below** that night, and she spoke at **ten minutes past one**, and I heard her." "**I paid him the next morning before I opened.** He put the bill in his vest and never once brought it up again in **twenty-five years**, which is worse than if he had." "Five dollars was five dollars in 1936."
- **[my inference — arithmetic oddity]** 41 at noon falling 4°/hr since 1 p.m. would give ~29 at 4 p.m., not 38; and 38 at 4 p.m. is only 3° below the noon reading. The stated figures don't cohere — and **both Judd and Dorsey give the identical incoherent figures**.
- **CONFLICTS between r03 and r08:** year **1937 (Judd) vs 14 January 1936 (Dorsey)**; payment **"in the store the next day in front of everybody there" (Judd) vs "the next morning before I opened" (Dorsey)**. Dorsey's "twenty-five years" (1936 → 1961) is internally consistent with his own date.
- r01 (Vesta): "Dorsey lost money to him over it once. Bet him she would speak on a certain night and she spoke. Five dollars, I always heard, and five dollars then was five dollars." (Grammatically ambiguous about who bet which way; r03 and r08 settle it: Judd said she would, Dorsey bet she would not.) "They were still telling it when I was grown and married."

## 10. The end of the sound — the diesel rebuild (year CONTESTED)

- r01 (Vesta): "It stopped when the railroad put new iron under that bridge — they were down there **all that spring** with their cranes and their trucks, doing it over for the diesels — and after that she never spoke again." The Falls said "the railroad had spoiled it"; "Somebody had come from off with money and taken it away from us." No year given.
- r02 (Pike): "**1954, in March**, when the road was rehabilitated for diesel power and the old bearings under the center span were taken out and new ones put in… stopped, that year, and never came back. The valley took this hard, as though something had been stolen from it."
- r03 (Judd): "the rockers went in, in the **spring of 1953**. They rebuilt the bridge for the diesels. They put in **rocker bearings with four inches of travel each way**, and they threw the old **cast pedestals** [plural] over the abutment into the creek, and nobody told me a thing about it. I was the agent. I found out from a section man."
- r04 (Ruth): "the **1954 gang** threw it over"; "the bearings that were put in in **1954**, which are rockers, not rollers, measure four inches of travel each way, and I measured those too."
- r06 (Lettie): "The noise stopped some years back. I could not tell you the year within two and I will not guess at it in print."
- r07 (Sable): "The new bearings went into the bridge in **March 1954**"; last night heard 9 February 1954; Judd retired 1954.
- r08 (Dorsey): "Then in **fifty-four** the railroad went in there and rebuilt the whole business — new bearings, a gang here for weeks eating up my stock, and they went away, and she never spoke again. ## 11. Ruth Frayne's investigation (r04) — full detail, with r06/r07 corroboration

- Occasion: the **state's flood-control cut** took Sallow Creek out of its bed for **eleven weeks**; gorge "dry to the gravel." She went out "**last September**" (= September 1962, talk given 1963; "sixty-five years" after 1897). Inspection "routine, and stopped being routine on the second afternoon." "I did not go out to Sallow Creek last September to settle anything." Sable (r07): survey report dated **September 1962**, "typescript with a linen tracing folded in."
- **Finding one (nothing)** — no locomotive; see §4.
- **Finding two (the pedestal)** — **forty feet below the abutment on the south side**, in the gravel; "a casting the size of a kitchen stove"; the **old south shoe of the center span**, which carried the roller nest. Cut into the roller seat is a **chisel mark** = the **setting mark**. Measured **three times with a steel tape**, same answer: **the chisel mark is at two inches**. The 1954 rockers: four inches each way. Sable (r07, Item 4): "**Pedestal, cast iron**, broken at one corner, two men to move it, on the floor of the north store room where it will have to stay." Card with the object, in Ruth's hand: > *Recovered from the creek bed forty feet below the south abutment. Chisel mark on the roller seat at two inches.* Recovered September 1962; given to the Society in 1963 with the report; "too heavy to bring up to the desk and which nobody has yet measured"; needs an accession number.
- **Finding three (Adela's field book)** — see §3 for both quotations.
- **Finding four (the night book)** — see §8; plus the August 1909 gang note.
- **The arithmetic (blackboard):**
  - Inch per 40°. Sheet 11's **3 inches = 120° of cooling**; 66 − 120 = **−54°**: "As drawn, that bridge would have run out of travel at fifty-four below, which this valley has never come anywhere near."
  - As built, **2 inches = 80°**; 66 − 80 = **−14°**: "Also a temperature this valley does not reach. And that is the reason nobody noticed anything for twelve years: a wrong bridge behaved exactly like a right one, because the difference between them lay in weather that never came."
  - August 1909, re-set to the same 2-inch mark at **86°**: 86 − 80 = **6° above zero** — "Which is where Judd Rennick's night book says the bridge spoke, sixty-one times in forty-three winters, and never once above."
  - Mechanism: "When the span cooled past its binding point the nest ran out of travel, the south shoe hung up, and the truss carried the load in stress until the shoe let go all at once and the span dropped into position with one report that ran along the rails. It was not a train. It was a bridge, arriving where it should have been."
  - **[my inference]** Her arithmetic explains the "below six" threshold but not Judd's second condition (30° fall) or his "once per freeze" observation; she does not address these.
- **Second witness — the Wexler weather record** (Kettle Bench): high, low, precipitation every day. Beginning put by Miss Wexler at **1 January 1894** ("and I have used her date"). Begun by **Peter Wexler**; daughter **Lettie** has kept it since **1926**; lent in **four cardboard boxes**. "Their weather agrees with the station agent's book on every one of the sixty-one nights. Two records kept by people who had no occasion to compare them, neither knowing what the other was for." Lettie's side (r06): "the two books lie down together like they were kept by one person."
- **Onset per Wexler record:** between opening (Nov 1897) and re-decking (Aug 1909) there were **seven nights at Kettle Bench below six degrees, four of them at the bottom of falls of thirty degrees or more**. "Nothing was heard on any of them, and nobody at the Falls has ever claimed there was." Coldest of those twelve years: **nine below, 4 February 1899** — "well short of the fourteen below that the bridge as built would have required." The two winters after the re-decking never got below **nine above zero**, coldest **3 February 1911**. First night under six after August 1909: **17 January 1912** (quoted; r06 quotes it identically "in my father's hand"):
  > *Jan. 17. High 34. Low −2 at daylight. Clear, still.*
  "That is the night the sound began, and the night my father's uncle started his book." "The silence of those seven nights is not an absence of data. It is the proof. If the error of 1897 had been enough by itself, the valley would have heard the bridge in 1899." Lettie confirms every figure (r06): "In those twelve years there were seven nights below six degrees. Four of the seven came after days at least thirty degrees warmer than the night that followed. The coldest reading of the whole twelve years was nine below, on the fourth of February, 1899. I remember writing that out for her twice because she wanted the date as well as the figure." "The winter of 1909 and 1910 and the winter of 1910 and 1911 never went below nine above zero, either one of them, and the coldest morning of the pair was the third of February, 1911." Of 17 Jan 1912: "Thirty-six degrees of fall in a day, and clear and still after it." She went through the twelve years "page by page over two evenings."
- **Series extremes:** highest **101°, July 1936** (r04; r06 "which I took myself and did not believe, and took again an hour later"; r07 index sheet). Lowest — **CONFLICT**: **thirteen below, January 1918** (r04) vs **eleven below zero, January 1918** (r06: "the lowest figure in the whole series from my father's first page to this morning's"; r07 index sheet: "record low eleven below zero, January 1918").

Miss Wexler keeps her weather book while she lives, then the Society — Sable: received **1967** "from the farm," the year Lettie died (r07). ## 14. The Wexler weather record — consolidated (r04, r06, r07)

- **What it is:** "three figures a day and nothing else. The day's high. The night's low. The rain, or the snow measured melted" (r06). "Daily high, daily low, precipitation" (r07). Thermometer on the **north wall of the milk house**; readings **at daylight and again at four**; "the low is the low at daylight unless the night was strange, in which case you say so in the margin and do not argue about it" (r06). Form: "farm daybooks and later ruled sheets… Two hands, the second taking over partway through" (r07).
- **Start:** **1884** (r06: "He began the weather record in 1884 and kept it until 1926, and I have kept it since, which makes eighty-one years of it, now" — 1884 + 81 = 1965 ✓; also "eighty-odd years of pages") vs **1 January 1894** (r04, Ruth quoting Miss Wexler; r07, "The depositor's own index sheet gives the beginning of the record as 1 January 1894, and I have entered that date on the authority of the sheet, having no other"). **CONFLICT** — and the 1894 date is attributed to Lettie herself in both r04 and r07.
- **Keepers:** Peter (father) then Lettie (daughter) from 1926 (r04, r06) vs "two brothers named Wexler, the younger of whom carried it on after the elder gave it up" (r07). **CONFLICT.**
- **Lettie's discipline:** "I have not missed a morning since 1926. I have taken it with a broken wrist and I have taken it the morning after my father's funeral… It is only that a record with a hole in it is not a record. It is a story about the weather, and there are enough of those." "In eighty-odd years of pages there is not one word about it [the bridge], because we never wrote down a noise. We wrote down weather. That turns out to have been worth something, though it was not why we did it."
- **Her advice** (r06): "Take the reading at the same hour. Write it in ink. Do not correct yesterday because today makes it look wrong. Do not leave a day out because nothing happened — the days when nothing happened are half of what the record is for, and it took a stranger with a survey to teach me that. Seven nights below six degrees in twelve years, four of them after a warm day: my father wrote those down for no reason at all, and sixty years later they turned out to be the answer to somebody's question. I do not know what the question was."
- **Figures:** see §11 (seven nights / four / −9 on 4 Feb 1899 / 1909–11 min +9, coldest 3 Feb 1911 / 17 Jan 1912 high 34 low −2 / 101° July 1936 / lowest −11 or −13 Jan 1918).
- **Custody:** four cardboard boxes lent to Ruth 1962, all returned (r06); deposited **1967** "from the farm," "in the same condition in which they went down to the 1962 survey and came back" (r07). Lettie died 1967 (r07).
