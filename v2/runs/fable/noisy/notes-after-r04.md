# Notes after retellings r01–r04 (Sallow Creek Viaduct / "the sound" at Cadder Falls)

Handoff notes for a fresh reader. Covers ONLY the four retellings (r01 Vesta Cobb, r02 Harlan Pike, r03 Judd Rennick, r04 Ruth Frayne). The four interleaved noise documents (Darwin, Twain, Mill, Babbage) are deliberately excluded. Everything below is from the texts; where I add an inference or arithmetic check of my own it is marked **[my inference]**.

## 1. People and relationships (all sources merged)

### The Rennick / Frayne family
- **Emil Rennick** — Judd's father. Carpenter. **Built the falsework for the bridge** ("the timber they stand the iron on until the iron will stand by itself. Then you knock the timber down"). Proud of it; "never got a line of credit for it anywhere." (r03)
- **Adela Rennick** — Emil's **younger sister** (r03). **Born 1874, died 1959 at eighty-five** (r04). "Miss Adela Rennick" (r02). Worked for the Cadder Valley Railroad in the **engineering party**, "out in the field with the men" (r03). r02: the party's **computer** ("She did the arithmetic, and she did it better than the party did" — Pike's father's phrase). r04: "**computer and rodman** for the railroad's engineering party while the viaduct was building." Kept **her own duplicate of the party's book, in her own hand** (r02: "thought eccentric and was not"); r04: "in black oilcloth, all her life." Never spoke about her railroad time to Judd — he asked twice as a boy and got nothing (r03). Entered "whatever she thought" in that book, including what she thought about the bridge; one such entry got back to the resident engineer (Tice), who "never forgave that entry, and she was gone inside four years" (r02). Her time with the road ended when the engineering party was reduced; two of the party were taken back on "in the August following, for the Ashby extension work, and she was not" (r02). Signs her entry "**A.R.**" (r04). Book passed at her death to her son **Wendell Frayne**, who gave it to Ruth (r04).
- **Judd Rennick** — son of Emil; **born 1888, at Cadder Falls** ("I was born in eighty-eight, here") (r03). Ruth (1963) says he **is seventy-four** and lives **three streets from the depot** he kept (r04). Vesta (1974): he "lived on and on… into his eighties, up on the hill there" (r01). Went on **nights in 1907**; "then they made me agent"; "**Thirty-nine years at that depot, all told**"; retired "a good while now" and "still wake[s] at four" (r03). Station agent who kept the **night book** (all four sources). Described by Vesta as "the most ordinary man you ever saw in your life, and he had this one thing"; wrote "in that little hand of his" (r01).
- **Wendell Frayne** — Adela's son; Ruth's father; received Adela's field book at her death (1959) and gave it to Ruth (r04).
- **Ruth Frayne** — Adela's granddaughter; Wendell's daughter; engineer with the Pell County highway department, three years in it (1963). Calls Judd "**my father's uncle**" (r04). Vesta calls her "the young Frayne woman… his grand-niece, they told me," who went "house to house with her notebook, very polite, very quick" and was let have Judd's book (r01). Judd: "The Frayne girl came a while back and sat where you are sitting and read the whole book through at this table, and she did not talk while she read, which I appreciated. She asked to take it away with her. I let her have it. She will do something with it. I never did." (r03)
- **[my inference — genealogy oddity]** By Judd's own account Adela is his father's younger sister, i.e. Judd's **aunt**. Adela's son Wendell is therefore Judd's **first cousin**, and Ruth is Judd's first cousin once removed — NOT his grand-niece, and Judd is NOT Wendell's uncle. Ruth's "my father's uncle" and Vesta's "grand-niece, they told me" contradict Judd's stated relationship. Also see §7 on Adela's dates of service.

### The Tice family
- **Warren Tice** — **resident engineer** on the viaduct work, "afterward the company's chief engineer at Ninestone and a considerable man in this county" (r02). Almost certainly the "**W.T.**" of Adela's 12 Sept 1897 entry (r04) **[my inference; r04 never expands the initials]**. Vesta: "old Warren Tice"; the Tices "were railroad people out of Ninestone, engineering people, big in the company" (r01).
- **Dorsey Tice** — Warren Tice's **nephew** (r01). Kept the **store "right there by the tracks"** at Cadder Falls; his family thought store-keeping a comedown, he did not; "best hard candy in the county and he weighed it heavy for children" (r01). Sparred with Judd over the book "for years, in a friendly way." Lost the **$5 wager** (r01, r03) and "told that story on himself for the rest of his life" (r03) / "would tell it on himself, which is why people liked him" (r01).

### The Pike family
- **Harlan Pike** — narrator of r02; son of the CVR's chief clerk; grew up "with a stock book"; sat in the office as a boy "while men talked over my head"; stood on the viaduct deck as a boy "with my father's hand in my collar." Memoir written 1958 in his seventies.
- **Pike's father** (unnamed) — **chief clerk of the Cadder Valley Railroad from 1893 to 1919** ("twenty-six years"); desk "with the window on the yard" at Ninestone; wrote the No. 9 sale entry himself; regarded her selling as "a small personal wound"; watched the 1911 fire "from the far side of the yard and never afterward cared for the smell of a coal fire"; "not a man who handed out compliments to save postage." "We never heard it at Ninestone. My father would have written it down."

### The Cobb family
- **Vesta Cobb** — narrator of r01; **born 1920**, "second house up from the mill gate," Cadder Falls. Father in the mill "his whole working life." Husband used to say "I could talk the paint off a porch." A **brother** (up before her the night she heard it). Mother said "that is only the train, go to sleep"; mother's phrase for the sound: "Like a door shut in a cellar three towns off." Father would say at the mill gate at four in the afternoon "well, she may talk tonight," and mother told him not to say so in front of the children.

"The Wexlers never wrote down a sound in their lives, and are related to nobody in this story." (r04)

## 2. Places and the railroad

- **Cadder Valley Railroad (CVR)** — "thirty-one miles of it, and no more" (r02). Existed for the **Ashby quarry**. "Everything else the company owned was sidings."
- **Ninestone** — **milepost 0**; county seat; shops, office, roundhouse; Pike's father's desk; the *Ninestone Sentinel*; Historical Society talk (r04) read there. Pike: "We never heard it at Ninestone."
- **Sallow Creek Viaduct** — **milepost 22** (r02, r03). Spans **Sallow Creek** gorge.
- **Cadder Falls** ("the Falls") — mill town, **milepost 23** (r02, r03). Has a mill with a "big saw," a mill gate, a depot with a platform, Dorsey Tice's store by the tracks, "the row"/"the street" of houses. Vesta's home; Judd's depot.
- **Ashby quarry** — **milepost 31**; "the reason the whole thing existed" (r02). Judd: "the quarry is milepost 31, the Falls is 23, the bridge is 22, and it is two and two-tenths percent down the nine miles off the quarry."
- **Grade** — **2.2 percent** from the quarry down to the viaduct, "held at that the whole way" (r02); "nine miles" (r03). Pike: "a great deal of falling for a small railroad with second-hand brakes… the company's answer was to hire careful men and pray for cold weather on the drums."
- **Pell County** — the county (Historical Society; highway department).
- **Kettle Bench** — home of the Wexler weather record (r04).
- **Bly County** — **two hundred miles north**; Tolliver Lumber Company; Bly County Historical Rooms (r02, r04).
- **Sixmile** — where No. 9 was cut up, May 1929 (r04).
- **Rowan Locomotive Works** — builder of No. 9 (r02).

## 3. The viaduct: construction facts

- Begun **April 1896** (r02). Line **opened to Cadder Falls 14 November 1897** (r02); r04: "the opening of the line in November 1897."
- **Three spans: 120 ft + 300 ft pin-connected truss across the gorge proper + 120 ft = 540 ft** total; **97 ft above the water** (r02).
- Center (300-ft) span **pinned solid at its north end**; **south end free**, riding on a **nest of rollers set in a cast pedestal — "the south shoe"** (r02, r04). Ruth: "pinned at the north end and free at the south."
- Thermal rule of thumb — **CONFLICT**:
  - r02 (Pike): "**one inch in three hundred feet for fifty degrees**… For our span, being three hundred feet exactly, it came out at an inch for every fifty degrees." "You will find it in any office of that period."
  - r04 (Adela's field book, front matter, quoted): > *Rule for the long span: one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees.* → an inch per **forty** degrees. Ruth's whole arithmetic rests on 40.
- Span **closed 12 September 1897**, thermometer **66°** (r02 and r04 agree); r04 adds **3:40 p.m.**
- **Sheet 11 of the contract drawings** — **CONFLICT**:
  - r02: called for **four inches** of travel on the south nest ("generous… showed a proper caution in whoever drew it").
  - r04 (Adela): "**Sheet 11 calls for three.**" Ruth's report: "Sheet 11 calls for three."
  - **[my inference]** Pike's "four inches" matches the travel of the **1954 rocker bearings** ("four inches of travel each way," r03/r04) — possible conflation.
- **As built:** rollers set at the **two-inch mark** (Adela's entry; chisel mark measured by Ruth).
- Falsework built by Emil Rennick (r03).
- Steel "breathed on that nest, out in summer and back in winter, for fifty-odd years" (r02). Pike's odd phrase: "a valley that runs from the middle of July to the middle of January."

### Adela's field-book entry, page 62 (r04, quoted verbatim)
> *Sept. 12, 1897. Span closed 3:40 p.m., thermometer 66°. Rollers set at the two-inch mark. Sheet 11 calls for three. Reported to W.T., who answers that the pedestal cannot be recast before the fall haul and that two inches is more than the valley will ever ask for. Entered here so that it is somewhere. — A.R.*

Ruth: "She reported it. She was overruled. She wrote it down so that it would be somewhere, and there it sat for sixty-five years while the valley told itself a ghost story."

## 4. The wreck of No. 9 and the engine's later life

- r02: **Morning of 6 March 1898**, a **work train** came down off the quarry grade "without brakes enough to hold her." Locomotive **No. 9** "went off the iron at the **north approach** of the new viaduct at **twenty minutes past seven**." **Nobody was killed. Two of the gang were hurt. It took nine days to rerail her.**
- *Ninestone Sentinel* of **8 March 1898** (r02, quoted):
  > *No lives were lost. Two of the gang are hurt, and the engine is on her side at the north end of the new viaduct, where she will be got up within the fortnight.*
- Pike: "There was no crew in the gorge. The engine was not in the gorge. The engine was on her side at the north end, in daylight, with half the county looking at her."
- **No. 9** — **4-4-0**, **Rowan Locomotive Works, built 1889, works number 1142**, cost **$8,600 new** ("a sum to be spoken of in a lowered voice"); "the best machine we owned."
- **Sold 19 June 1901** to a lumber concern in **Bly County, two hundred miles north**, for **$2,150**. CVR stock-book entry written by Pike's father (r02, quoted):
  > *Locomotive No. 9, 4-4-0, Rowan Works 1889, works no. 1142, sold this day to the Tolliver Lumber Company, Bly County, for two thousand one hundred and fifty dollars ($2,150.00), delivered on her own wheels.*
  Pike: "she steamed away north under her own power, three years after she is supposed to have been lying at the bottom of Sallow Creek with her dead."
- r04 corroboration: Ruth wrote to the **Bly County Historical Rooms** (200 miles north), which hold the Tolliver Lumber Company's books, **deposited 1938 by the company's bookkeeper**, "consulted by nobody since." Photostats by return: a **stock book entry of 19 June 1901** records Cadder Valley No. 9 sold to Tolliver, delivered on her own wheels; a **shop book entry of May 1929** records her **cut up at Sixmile**. "She left this valley alive and she died two hundred miles from it."
- r01 (Vesta's version): "**Number Nine went into that gorge in the bad winter when the bridge was new, and her crew went in with her, and they were killed, and they were never brought up.** They are down there yet, under the gravel and the water… She comes down off the quarry the way she came down that morning and she cannot stop, and when she hits she makes that one sound." Told her by her grandmother, "and she was here for it."
- r04 Finding one: Ruth walked the dry bed "something over eight hundred feet, above and below the viaduct" — **no locomotive**, "no frame, no boiler shell, no driving wheel, nothing of iron larger than a spike." Photographs on the table at the back.

## 5. The 1911 fire (r02)

- **4 April 1911**: the **roundhouse and office at Ninestone burned**. Lost: "the construction record of the road — the contract files, the erection time books, the whole account of how the viaduct was built and by whom. After that morning the company possessed nothing in writing about the building of its own bridge."
- **[my inference]** This makes Adela's duplicate field book the only surviving construction record, and raises the question of where Pike gets his 66°/Sheet-11/four-inch figures from (he says of the Tice–Adela affair "I have nothing in writing on the point").

## 6. The re-decking (year is CONTESTED)

- r02 (Pike): **1908**, "some weeks that summer": **85-lb steel in place of the 60**, new ties throughout, **both shoes jacked and re-set**; finished deck **forty tons heavier**; "I have that from the office."
- r03 (Judd), note in the **front of the night book**, copied "off the depot slate a good while after" (quoted):
  > *Bridge gang here three weeks, August 1909. New 85-lb. steel, new ties, both shoes jacked and set again to the mark. Thermometer on the north pier read 86 at four o'clock when they seated the south nest. Mr. Coyle says the deck is forty ton heavier than it was.*
  Then Judd **says aloud: "That was the summer of 1911."** — **internal conflict** with his own written note (August 1909). Also: "three weeks of them, and dust in the office"; "I did not think it meant anything."
- r04 (Ruth): "**August 1909**," three weeks, same details (85-lb steel, ties, both shoes jacked and set again to the mark, north-pier thermometer **86° at four o'clock** when they seated the south nest). She does **not** repeat the 40-ton figure.
- **Summary of conflict:** re-decking year = **1908 (Pike) / 1909 (Judd's written note, Ruth) / 1911 (Judd spoken)**.
- Pike's opinion (the one opinion in his chapter): "Forty tons is forty tons. A nest of rollers under a bridge was never meant to carry what that new deck put upon it, and the noise the Falls made so much of began somewhere about that time and not before it. I say the weight did it."
- Ruth's reading: the gang "set them again to the same two-inch mark — with the steel at eighty-six degrees"; "a gang doing careful work in good faith at eighty-six degrees, to finish what was begun at sixty-six."

## 7. The sound itself — descriptions

- r01 (Vesta): "**It was one noise.**" One deep report, then nothing, "and the nothing after it was worse than the noise was." Not "a rattling or a rolling, like thunder walking off down the valley." "Not sharp like a shot. Deep, the way a big thing sounds when it is a long way down." Mother: "Like a door shut in a cellar three towns off." **Felt before heard** — "came up through the floorboards into your feet, the way the mill did when they started the big saw." Always the small hours: "**Between midnight and four**, and I would say **closer to two** than either end, though I would not swear to it." Never at supper or noon. Only on "**killing nights**": day soft, "shirtsleeve weather almost, mud in the road," then sky hard and clear about dark, "the whole night came to pieces on you." Could be felt coming at the mill gate at four in the afternoon.
- r03 (Judd): "It was one report. Deep. It was not a rattle and it was not a roll and **it did not travel** — it was there and then it was done. And **it came up the rails first**": the telegraph **sounder** on his table "would rattle in its bracket before the sound got to the room. A little dry rattle, like a bug in a box. Then the floor. Then the noise." Timed the gap with a watch once, couldn't get a reading; "the order never changed in forty-three winters." "Always between midnight and four. Never in the evening. Never at daylight."
- r04 (Ruth): "the sound the valley called **the boom**" (only r04 uses that word; r01/r03 say "she spoke"/"she may talk"); "one report that ran along the rails."
- Vesta's own experience (the one she vouches for): "a winter in the thirties, and I could not give you the night." Rain that day; the wash froze on the line; mother cross. The whole street came awake at once; "the floor went first," then the sound; brother up before her; father at the window with no lamp lit; cold on the glass; **no wind at all**; dogs going off up and down the row one after another, then quitting, "as still as the inside of a cup"; doors opening; "grown men out on their porches in their union suits in that cold, holding their arms, looking up the valley toward the bridge, and not a one of them saying anything." "It was not a surprise to them. It was a visit." Mother: "that is only the train, go to sleep." Vesta: "I notice that the ones who are surest are the ones who were never in the house."
- Judd's first time: he "thought a car had got away in the quarry and come down on us," went out on the platform with a lantern, nothing wrong, "so cold the lantern glass cracked." Ruled up the book the next morning.
- Town folklore (r03): "**she rang for deaths**" — somebody at the Falls would be dead inside the week. Judd's count: **190 deaths at Cadder Falls** during the years of the book; **7** fell inside a week after a night she spoke. Numbers put in the back of the book; never said at the store.
- Store theory (r01): **ice in the gorge**; Vesta's rebuttal: "then why not every cold night, why the killing nights only."

## 8. Judd Rennick's night book

- **Five columns**: the date; the day's high; the night's low; whether she spoke; the hour. No opinions ("a man who writes down his opinion once will write down his opinion instead of the reading the next time"). Vesta's description: "the night, and the cold, and the hour of it."
- Conditions (derived after "a good many winters"):
  1. Night's **low below six degrees** — "Never once. Not at seven, not at six and a half. Below six."
  2. Day before **at least thirty degrees warmer** than the night that followed — "It had to fall thirty… a soft day and then the bottom out of it."
  - "Both, every time. There is not an exception in the book."
- **Totals**: **61 nights** she spoke; **34 winters** with at least one; **9 winters** with none; 34 + 9 = **43 winters**. **5 nights** met both conditions and gave nothing; so **66 nights** met the conditions in all.
- Judd's one guess: "She would not speak twice inside one freeze." After speaking she was silent until a **real thaw — above forty in the daytime**. All five silent nights sit in a cold spell where she had already spoken with nothing above forty between. "That is not a theory about what she was. It is a rule about when."
- Ruth's summary (r04): 61 nights; every low below six above zero at the bottom of a fall of 30+; "Further nights answered both conditions and produced nothing at all, and his own summing at the back of the book gives the count of them. He kept no theory. He kept columns." (**Minor oddity**: Judd says the back of the book holds the death numbers 7/190; Ruth says the back holds the count of silent nights.)
- **When the book began — CONFLICT (three versions in two sources):**
  - r03: "I went on **nights in 1907 and I started the book that same winter**."
  - r03, later: "**The first time she spoke was the winter of 1913**… In the morning I ruled up a book."
  - r04 (via Wexler record): first night under six after August 1909 was **17 January 1912** — "That is the night the sound began, and the night my father's uncle started his book."
  - **[my inference]** 43 winters counted from winter 1912–13 through 1954–55 (the "second winter since the rockers" if rockers = spring 1953) = exactly 43, which fits "winter of 1913" read as 1912–13 and Judd's 1953 date; it fits neither 1907 nor a 1954 rocker date cleanly.
- Judd's **last entry** (quoted):
  > *Second winter since the rockers went in. Three nights under six degrees, three falls of better than thirty, and not a sound out of her either winter. I have kept this book forty-three winters and I do not know now what it was I was listening to.*
- Judd's summary: "I have got sixty-one nights and a hundred and ninety deaths and two conditions that never failed, and I have not got one word of explanation."
- Custody: Judd lent/gave it to Ruth (r01, r03, r04); Ruth (1963): "The night book comes here [to the Society] when Judd Rennick has done with it, and I hope that is a long time yet."

## 9. The $5 wager (r01 and r03)

- r03 (Judd's detailed account): **January 1937**, in Dorsey Tice's store, mid-afternoon. "It had made **forty-one at noon**. It was **thirty-eight at four o'clock** and it had been **falling four degrees an hour since one**, steady." Judd's sum at the counter: 38 down to 6 = 32 degrees; at 4°/hr = 8 hours → under six "about midnight"; day had been 41, so a fall of "better than thirty"; **no thaw since the second week of December**, "so she was rested." Judd said she would speak before morning; **Dorsey bet five dollars she would not**. Low that night **three below**; **she spoke at ten minutes past one**. Dorsey paid in the store the next day "in front of everybody there."
- **[my inference — arithmetic oddity]** 41 at noon falling 4°/hr since 1 p.m. would give ~29 at 4 p.m., not 38; and 38 at 4 p.m. is only 3° below the noon reading. The stated figures don't cohere.
- r01 (Vesta): "Dorsey lost money to him over it once. Bet him she would speak on a certain night and she spoke. Five dollars, I always heard, and five dollars then was five dollars." (Grammatically ambiguous about who bet which way; r03 settles it: Judd said she would, Dorsey bet she would not.) "They were still telling it when I was grown and married."

## 10. The end of the sound — the diesel rebuild (year CONTESTED)

- r01 (Vesta): "It stopped when the railroad put new iron under that bridge — they were down there **all that spring** with their cranes and their trucks, doing it over for the diesels — and after that she never spoke again." The Falls said "the railroad had spoiled it"; "Somebody had come from off with money and taken it away from us." No year given.
- r02 (Pike): "**1954, in March**, when the road was rehabilitated for diesel power and the old bearings under the center span were taken out and new ones put in… stopped, that year, and never came back. The valley took this hard, as though something had been stolen from it."
- r03 (Judd): "the rockers went in, in the **spring of 1953**. They rebuilt the bridge for the diesels. They put in **rocker bearings with four inches of travel each way**, and they threw the old **cast pedestals** [plural] over the abutment into the creek, and nobody told me a thing about it. I was the agent. I found out from a section man."
- r04 (Ruth): "the **1954 gang** threw it over"; "the bearings that were put in in **1954**, which are rockers, not rollers, measure four inches of travel each way, and I measured those too."
- **Conflict: 1953 (Judd) vs 1954 (Pike, Ruth).** Also: Judd, having gone on nights in 1907 and served "thirty-nine years… all told," says he was still agent when the rockers went in (1953 = 46 years after 1907) — **[my inference]** only coherent if the 39 years are counted from when he was made agent.

## 11. Ruth Frayne's investigation (r04) — full detail

- Occasion: the **state's flood-control cut** took Sallow Creek out of its bed for **eleven weeks**; gorge "dry to the gravel." She went out "**last September**" (= September 1962, talk given 1963; "sixty-five years" after 1897). Inspection "routine, and stopped being routine on the second afternoon." "I did not go out to Sallow Creek last September to settle anything."
- **Finding one (nothing)** — no locomotive; see §4.
- **Finding two (the pedestal)** — **forty feet below the abutment on the south side**, in the gravel; "a casting the size of a kitchen stove"; the **old south shoe of the center span**, which carried the roller nest. Cut into the roller seat is a **chisel mark** = the **setting mark**. Measured **three times with a steel tape**, same answer: **the chisel mark is at two inches**. The 1954 rockers: four inches each way.
- **Finding three (Adela's field book)** — see §3 for both quotations.
- **Finding four (the night book)** — see §8; plus the August 1909 gang note.
- **The arithmetic (blackboard):**
  - Inch per 40°. Sheet 11's **3 inches = 120° of cooling**; 66 − 120 = **−54°**: "As drawn, that bridge would have run out of travel at fifty-four below, which this valley has never come anywhere near."
  - As built, **2 inches = 80°**; 66 − 80 = **−14°**: "Also a temperature this valley does not reach. And that is the reason nobody noticed anything for twelve years: a wrong bridge behaved exactly like a right one, because the difference between them lay in weather that never came."
  - August 1909, re-set to the same 2-inch mark at **86°**: 86 − 80 = **6° above zero** — "Which is where Judd Rennick's night book says the bridge spoke, sixty-one times in forty-three winters, and never once above."
  - Mechanism: "When the span cooled past its binding point the nest ran out of travel, the south shoe hung up, and the truss carried the load in stress until the shoe let go all at once and the span dropped into position with one report that ran along the rails. It was not a train. It was a bridge, arriving where it should have been."
  - **[my inference]** Her arithmetic explains the "below six" threshold but not Judd's second condition (30° fall) or his "once per freeze" observation; she does not address these.
- **Second witness — the Wexler weather record** (Kettle Bench): high, low, precipitation every day. Beginning put by Miss Wexler at **1 January 1894** ("and I have used her date"). Begun by **Peter Wexler**; daughter **Lettie** has kept it since **1926**; lent in **four cardboard boxes**. "Their weather agrees with the station agent's book on every one of the sixty-one nights. Two records kept by people who had no occasion to compare them, neither knowing what the other was for."
- **Onset per Wexler record:** between opening (Nov 1897) and re-decking (Aug 1909) there were **seven nights at Kettle Bench below six degrees, four of them at the bottom of falls of thirty degrees or more**. "Nothing was heard on any of them, and nobody at the Falls has ever claimed there was." Coldest of those twelve years: **nine below, 4 February 1899** — "well short of the fourteen below that the bridge as built would have required." The two winters after the re-decking never got below **nine above zero**, coldest **3 February 1911**. First night under six after August 1909: **17 January 1912** (quoted):
  > *Jan. 17. High 34. Low −2 at daylight. Clear, still.*
  "That is the night the sound began, and the night my father's uncle started his book." "The silence of those seven nights is not an absence of data. It is the proof. If the error of 1897 had been enough by itself, the valley would have heard the bridge in 1899."
- **Series extremes:** highest **101°, July 1936**; lowest **thirteen below, January 1918**.
- **Disposition:** Adela's field book → the Society "this year" (1963). Miss Wexler keeps her weather book while she lives, then the Society. Night book → the Society when Judd is done with it. The pedestal: "I would like in the entrance hall, where people can put a hand on it."
- **Last paragraph of her report** ("written on the tailgate of the truck and not improved since," quoted):
  > *The south pedestal was recovered from the creek bed forty feet below the abutment, where the 1954 gang threw it. The chisel mark on the roller seat is at two inches. Sheet 11 calls for three. There is no locomotive in this creek and there never was one. What the valley heard for forty-three winters was a bridge letting go of itself, one inch late.*
- Closing line: "My grandmother measured that bridge and was not believed. I measured it again sixty-five years later, with a better tape and worse handwriting, and she was right. That is all I have."

## 13. Consolidated list of conflicts and oddities

1. **Deaths in the wreck**: Vesta — crew killed, never brought up, still in the gorge. Pike — "Nobody killed. Not one man"; *Sentinel* confirms; engine on her side at the north end. Ruth — no locomotive in the creek. Pike and Ruth both document No. 9 sold 19 June 1901 "on her own wheels"; Ruth adds cut up May 1929 at Sixmile.
2. **Onset year**: 1897–98 (Vesta) / ~1908 (Pike) / 1907 or 1913 (Judd, self-contradicting) / 17 Jan 1912 (Ruth). Wexler record: nothing 1897–1909.
3. **Thermal rule**: 1 in. per **50°** (Pike) vs 1 in. per **40°** (Adela's book / Ruth).
4. **Sheet 11 travel**: **four** inches (Pike) vs **three** inches (Adela / Ruth). Built at **two**.
5. **Re-decking year**: **1908** (Pike) / **August 1909** (Judd's written note, Ruth) / "**summer of 1911**" (Judd spoken, same breath).
6. **Rebuild/rockers year**: **spring 1953** (Judd) vs **March 1954 / "the 1954 gang"** (Pike, Ruth). Vesta: "all that spring," no year.
7. **Judd's book start**: 1907 vs 1913 within r03; Ruth says Jan 1912.
8. **Family relationship**: Ruth calls Judd "my father's uncle"; Vesta says "grand-niece"; but Judd says Adela was his father's younger sister → Judd and Wendell are first cousins.
9. **Adela's dates of service**: Judd — "She worked for the same railroad **before I was born** [1888]"; Ruth/Pike — she was in the engineering party **while the viaduct was building (1896–97)**. Adela was born 1874, so she'd have been ≤14 before 1888.
10. **Adela's departure**: Pike — party reduced, not rehired "the August following," yet "gone inside four years" (two timescales in one paragraph); "I have nothing in writing on the point."
11. **Quarry grade before the Ashby extension**: Pike has a work train coming "down off the quarry grade" on 6 March 1898, but also says the Ashby extension work was what the party was rehired for "the August following" (1898). **[my inference]** Possibly construction track, but odd.
12. **Wager-day temperatures** (r03): 41 at noon, 38 at four, "falling four degrees an hour since one" — internally inconsistent.
13. **Judd's 39 years** at the depot vs. nights from 1907 and still agent in 1953.
14. **What is in the back of the book**: Judd — the 7/190 death figures; Ruth — the count of silent nights.
15. **Pedestal(s)**: Judd — "cast pedestals" thrown over (plural); Ruth recovers one, the south shoe.
16. **Custody of the night book**: Judd (1965) "let her have it"/"take it away"; Vesta — "he let her have it"; Ruth (1963) speaks of it coming to the Society "when Judd Rennick has done with it" and says he "lent" it.
17. **Pike's sourcing**: he gives exact 1897 figures (66°, Sheet 11) although the construction records burned 4 April 1911 and he admits nothing in writing on Adela; Ruth's figures come from a surviving primary document (Adela's duplicate).
18. **Ruth omits the 40 tons** from her summary of Judd's gang note; Pike's whole theory rests on it. Judd wrote it but "did not think it meant anything."
19. **Time of night**: Vesta "closer to two"; Judd "between midnight and four"; the one timed instance is **1:10 a.m.** (Jan 1937).
20. **Where Judd lives**: "up on the hill there" (Vesta) vs "three streets from the depot" (Ruth) — not necessarily contradictory.
21. **Naming the sound**: "the boom" (Ruth only); "she spoke / she may talk / she rang for deaths" (Vesta, Judd).
22. **Sound behavior**: Judd — "did not travel"; Vesta — "like a door shut in a cellar three towns off"; Ruth — "ran along the rails." Judd's sounder rattled before the floor moved; Vesta felt the floor before hearing.
23. **Wexler start date** hedged: "Miss Wexler puts the beginning of it at the first of January, 1894, and I have used her date."
24. **Vesta's mother's line** "that is only the train" is ironic against Ruth's "It was not a train."
25. **Kettle Bench low of −13 (Jan 1918)** — well below Ruth's 6° binding point; by her theory the bridge would have spoken that winter (consistent with Judd's book covering 1918, but not stated).
