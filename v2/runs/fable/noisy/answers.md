## Section A — Reconstruction

### A1. The building of the Sallow Creek Viaduct

The Cadder Valley Railroad was a 31-mile branch built to bring stone down from the Ashby quarry (milepost 31) to the county seat at Ninestone (milepost 0). Its one great structure was the viaduct over the Sallow Creek gorge at milepost 22, a mile short of the mill town of Cadder Falls (milepost 23). From the quarry the line fell toward it on a 2.2 percent grade held for nine miles — about 1,045 feet of descent.

Work began in April 1896. Three spans — 120, 300 and 120 feet, 540 feet in all — stood 97 feet above the water. The center span was a pin-connected truss, fixed at its north end and free at the south, where it rode on a nest of rollers seated in a cast-iron pedestal (the south shoe), so that all of the span's movement with temperature was taken there. Emil Rennick (1866–1931), a Cadder Falls carpenter, built the falsework the iron was assembled on. The resident engineer was Warren Tice, afterward the road's chief engineer at Ninestone. The engineering party's computer — and, when needed, its rodman — was Emil's younger sister Adela Rennick (1874–1959), who joined the road in 1895 and kept her own duplicate field book in black oilcloth alongside the company's.

The rule for the long span was one inch of movement in 300 feet for every 40 degrees; Sheet 11 of the contract drawings called for three inches of cold travel at the south nest. The span was closed at 3:40 p.m. on 12 September 1897 with the thermometer at 66 degrees — and the rollers were set at the two-inch mark. Adela reported the shortfall to Tice ("W.T."), who answered that the pedestal could not be recast before the fall haul and that two inches was more than the valley would ever ask for. She entered it in her book "so that it is somewhere." (Which gang hand physically set the nest — Anders Berg per Dorsey Tice, Cass Nolan per the school pamphlet — cannot be determined from the sources; the decision to leave it stood was Tice's, and by a story at two removes he later said "we set that nest a mark shy.")

The first train reached Cadder Falls on 14 November 1897. As built, the nest would not run out of travel until 14 below zero, a temperature the valley never reached, so for twelve winters a wrong bridge behaved like a right one. The party was reduced entire on 15 May 1901, Tice's recommendation entered on Adela's card; she married Josiah Frayne in 1902. On 4 April 1911 the roundhouse and office at Ninestone burned with the road's construction records, leaving Adela's book the only written account of the bridge's building. Tice retired in the spring of 1931 and died in 1937.

### A2. The night book, and what the valley believed

In August 1909 a bridge gang spent three weeks re-decking the viaduct — 85-lb rail for the 60, new ties, both shoes jacked and set again to the same two-inch mark, the north-pier thermometer reading 86° when the south nest was seated; foreman Coyle said the deck was forty tons heavier. The next two winters never went below nine above zero and nothing was heard. On 17 January 1912 — high 34, low −2, clear and still — the valley heard the bridge for the first time: one deep report in the small hours, felt in the floor before it was heard. Judd Rennick (b. 1888), Emil's son, night operator at the Cadder Falls depot since 1909 and agent from 1915 to June 1954, went out with a lantern expecting a runaway car, found nothing, and next morning ruled up a book: date, day's high, night's low, whether she spoke, the hour. No opinions.

He kept it 43 winters, 1911–12 through 1953–54: 61 nights she spoke, 34 winters with at least one, nine with none, the fullest 1917–18 with five. Always between midnight and four, never twice in a night; the telegraph sounder rattled before the floor moved. Two conditions never failed — a night low below six degrees, after a day at least thirty degrees warmer. Five nights met both and gave nothing, each inside a cold spell where she had already spoken with no day above forty between: "she would not speak twice inside one freeze." The Kettle Bench weather record, kept by Peter Wexler and then his daughter Lettie, agreed on every night. Against the old women's belief that she rang for a death, Judd counted: 190 deaths in those years, seven within a week of a night she spoke.

On 14 January 1936, with the store's porch thermometer falling four degrees an hour, Judd told Dorsey Tice over his counter that she would speak before morning; Dorsey bet five dollars she would not. The low was three below; she spoke at 1:10 a.m.; Dorsey paid the next day.

The valley believed the sound was locomotive No. 9, which had run away down the quarry grade on 6 March 1898 and left the rails at the north approach of the new bridge. Grandmothers who "were here for it" told that she went into the gorge with her crew, was never brought up, and came down the grade again on killing nights. Nobody had died and she was rerailed in nine days; but the wreck was real, the place was the same, the sound was iron, and memory kept the nights it wanted and dropped the rest.

The last night was 9 February 1954. In March 1954 the road fitted rocker bearings with four inches of travel each way for diesel power; three qualifying nights in the next two winters passed in silence. Judd's last entry, 2 March 1956: "I do not know now what it was I was listening to."

### A3. The locomotive's second life

No. 9 was the Cadder Valley's best engine: a 4-4-0 American type from the Rowan Locomotive Works, built 1889, works number 1142, $8,600 new. On 6 March 1898 she came down the quarry grade with a work train and brakes that would not hold her and went off the iron at the north approach of the viaduct at 7:20 a.m.; nobody was killed, Lidell broke an arm and Sherrod was scalded, and she was rerailed in nine days.

In 1901 Silas Tolliver (1849–1922), founder of the Tolliver Lumber Company at Sixmile in Bly County, two hundred miles north, wanted a road engine to bring made-up trains of logs down the main stem of his tram faster than a man could walk beside them. His daughter Pearl (b. 1881), the company's bookkeeper from the age of eighteen, found the Cadder Valley's advertisement for a twelve-year-old American type and put it on his desk. On 19 June 1901 the CVR stock book recorded No. 9 sold to Tolliver for $2,150 — exactly a quarter of her first cost — "delivered on her own wheels." A Pell County crew took her north under her own steam, eleven days and four railroads, handed her over at the interchange, drank coffee in the Tolliver kitchen, and went home as passengers.

The shop painted out the 9 and painted on a 4. Her first season on the grades, where the geared engines belonged, was a failure — "a racehorse to pull a stump" — after which she was kept on the main stem of the Sixmile tram, by the end nineteen miles of thirty-pound rail up three drainages. There she worked 27 years: retubed 1909, new tires 1913, frame welded 1919 and 1922, a full shopping in 1924, with a whistle you could hear at the schoolhouse and feel in the floor of the house. In September 1928 a second-hand eight-coupled engine replaced her; she stood behind the shop under a tarpaulin through the winter, and in May 1929, forty years old, she was cut up at Sixmile. Pearl's shop-book entry: "No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile this month. Scrap to Ravel Brothers, $470." The tires and brass went separately; the boiler shell became the mill's wash-water tank and was still there when the mill came down.

The cut ran out in 1936. In 1938 Pearl drove the stock book, shop book, timber ledgers and eleven boxes of correspondence to the Bly County Historical Rooms, where they were catalogued and consulted by nobody for twenty-four years — until Ruth Frayne wrote in 1962, and the schoolmaster Athey in 1966. Through every winter Cadder Falls listened for her, the Four was hauling logs two hundred miles away.

### A4. The 1962 survey and what it established

In September 1962 the state's flood-control cut took Sallow Creek out of its bed for eleven weeks. Pell County sent Ruth Frayne (b. 1934), three years a civil engineer in the highway department, to inspect the piers with a level and a camera. She walked more than 800 feet of dry gravel above and below the viaduct and found nothing of iron larger than a spike: no locomotive, no frame, no boiler, no wheel. Forty feet below the south abutment she found the old south shoe — a cast-iron pedestal the size of a kitchen stove, broken at one corner, thrown over by the 1954 gang — with a chisel setting mark on its roller seat. She measured it three times with a steel tape: two inches.

Her father Wendell put his mother Adela's field book into her hands; page 62 recorded the closing on 12 September 1897 at 66 degrees, rollers at the two-inch mark, Sheet 11 calling for three, and Tice overruling her. Judd Rennick lent her the night book; Lettie Wexler lent the Kettle Bench weather record in four cardboard boxes without asking why; the Bly County Historical Rooms sent photostats of the 1901 sale and the 1929 scrapping by return. Her arithmetic, at one inch per 40 degrees: three inches would have bound at 54 below; two inches at 14 below, never reached (the coldest of those years was 9 below, 4 February 1899); two inches re-seated at 86 degrees in August 1909 bound at 6 above — exactly where the night book's 61 nights sat and never once above. The Wexler record showed seven sub-six nights between 1897 and 1909 with no sound, two winters after the re-decking never below nine, and the first sub-six night thereafter, 17 January 1912, as the first night in Judd's book. Her conclusion: what the valley heard for 43 winters was the bridge letting go of itself, one inch late.

The report (typescript, linen tracing) was filed that month; the *Sentinel* ran "The Ghost of Sallow Creek Lies Down" on 14 October 1962; she read her findings to the Historical Society at Ninestone in 1963. The field book (deposited by Wendell), the pedestal, the report and the photostats went to the Society that year; the weather record followed in 1967 and the night book in 1969, from the two keepers' estates. In 1971 the county engineer's office put a memorandum on file recommending that the pedestal be kept as the only physical evidence of the 1897 setting and that memorandum, drawings, 1954 records and survey never again be read separately.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Brother and sister — Adela was Emil's younger sister. Four sources say so in as many words (Judd r03, Sable r07, Wendell r10, Athey r12); Emil b. 1866, Adela b. 1874.

**B2.** Judd was Adela's nephew (she was his aunt). Established by Judd himself — "my father's younger sister" (r03) — and independently by Wendell ("Emil's son, which made him my mother's nephew," r10), Sable (Emil and Adela siblings, Wendell and Judd first cousins, r07) and Athey ("son of Emil," r12). What points elsewhere: Ruth calls Judd "my father's uncle" (r04) and Vesta calls Ruth his "grand-niece" (r01), which would make Judd Adela's brother. Wendell resolves it: Ruth "used to call him uncle and he never corrected her" — a courtesy title for a cousin sixteen years older than her father. Parentage stated by the man himself and his cousin outranks a courtesy usage.

**B3.** First cousins once removed. Derivation: Emil and Adela were siblings (B1); Judd was Emil's son and Wendell was Adela's son, so Judd and Wendell were first cousins (Wendell r10, Sable r07); Ruth is Wendell's daughter (Ruth r04, Wendell, Sable), so Ruth is the child of Judd's first cousin — his first cousin once removed, which Wendell states outright.

**B4.** Warren Tice, the viaduct's resident engineer — the man who overruled Adela and left the nest at two inches. Sources: Dorsey's own first-person account ("My father… Warren Tice, his name was," r08) and Wendell ("His son Dorsey kept the store," r10) against "nephew" in Vesta (r01) and Athey (r12), which are the same town tradition; the man's own testimony plus a contemporary family wins. Why it matters to January 1936 **[inference]**: the storekeeper who bet five dollars that the bridge would not speak was the son of the man whose decision made it speak; the man who won was the nephew of the woman who had recorded the objection, and he won by a rule derived from the book she had, in effect, begun. Dorsey's denial on the same tape that his father "had no hand in that bridge," and his naming of Anders Berg, are a son's deflection; the wager was the Tice denial tested against the Rennick record, and it lost. By the story at two removes, Tice had said at his 1931 retirement dinner that he had "listened to it ever since"; five years later his son bet that there was nothing to listen for.

**B5.** All four came to Ruth Frayne in the autumn of 1962:
- *Field book*: Adela's own duplicate, kept 1895–1901 on the party; in her own keeping from 1901 on the shelf over her chair at Cadder Falls until her death in 1959; then her son Wendell's; in September 1962 Wendell "took my mother's black book down off the shelf and put it into her hands myself"; Ruth deposited it with the Society in 1963 on a slip in Wendell's hand.
- *Night book*: kept by Judd at the depot from January 1912 to March 1956; in 1962 Ruth read it through at his kitchen table and asked to take it away; he lent it (the Society's provenance record: lent personally in 1962, returned); it came to the Society only from his estate in 1969.
- *Weather record*: begun by Peter Wexler at Kettle Bench, kept by his daughter Lettie from 1926; in the fall of 1962 Lettie lent it to Ruth in four cardboard boxes "without asking what I wanted it for" and without a receipt; every box came back; the Society received it in 1967 after Lettie's death.
- *Bly County ledgers*: the Tolliver Lumber Company's books, kept by Pearl Tolliver Nace, deposited by her in 1938 at the Bly County Historical Rooms and unconsulted for twenty-four years; Ruth wrote in 1962 and received photostats of two pages (stock book, 19 June 1901; shop book, May 1929) by return; the photostats went to the Society with her report in 1963, the originals remaining at Bly County.
(To these she added the pedestal from the creek bed and the *Sentinel* of 8 March 1898 in the Society's file.)

## Section C — Math

**C1.** Rule: 1 inch in 300 feet per 40°, i.e. 1 inch per 40° for this 300-foot span (Adela's field book, inside cover, transcribed identically by Ruth r04 and Sable r07). Closed at 66° (all sources). Cold travel exhausted when the span has cooled by (inches of travel × 40°) below the temperature at which it was seated.

(a) As drawn, Sheet 11 = 3 inches: 3 × 40 = 120°; 66 − 120 = **−54 °F (54 below zero)**.
(b) As set in 1897, 2 inches: 2 × 40 = 80°; 66 − 80 = **−14 °F (14 below zero)**.
(c) After August 1909, re-seated to the same 2-inch mark at 86°: 86 − 80 = **+6 °F (6 above zero)**.
(d) Margin lost: from −14 to +6 = **20 degrees** (equivalently 86 − 66 = 20: the re-seat gave away the difference between the two seating temperatures).

Rejected alternative: Pike and Quarles use 1 inch per 50° and Sheet 11 = 4 inches, giving −134 / −34 / −14 (and the same 20° for (d)). I reject it because (i) the 40° rule and the 3 inches are in the contemporaneous field book, written the day of the closing by the party's computer; (ii) only the 40° rule lands the post-1909 binding point on the night book's observed threshold (heard below six, never above), and Quarles concedes his −14 "is not readily reconciled with the local record"; (iii) "four inches each way" is the specification of the 1954 rocker bearings, carried back onto Sheet 11.

**C2.** (a) Threshold six degrees. 38 − 6 = 32 degrees to fall; 32 ÷ 4 = 8 hours; 4 p.m. + 8 h = **about midnight** (Judd's own reckoning at the counter). She spoke at 1:10 a.m., about an hour after crossing.
(b) Day's high 41 (at noon, both eyewitnesses); night's low −3. Fall = 41 − (−3) = **44 degrees**. The second condition is a fall of at least 30 degrees: 44 ≥ 30, **satisfied**. (Note: the three afternoon figures both narrators give — 41 at noon, 38 at four, "falling four an hour since one" — cannot all be true: 41 − 3 × 4 = 29 at four, not 38. Whichever figure is off, the high-to-low fall exceeds 30 under any reading.)

**C3.** Counts from the book's own summary page as transcribed by Sable (r07) and confirmed by the keeper (r03), Ruth (r04) and Lettie's 61 (r06): 43 winters; 61 nights heard; 34 winters with at least one; 9 with none; 5 qualifying nights silent.

(a) 61 ÷ 43 = 1.418… → **1.4 nights per winter**.
(b) 61 + 5 = **66 nights** met both conditions (the summary page: "sixty-six nights that answered them in all").
(c) 61 ÷ 34 = 1.794… → **1.8 nights per winter heard**.

Which winters: 1911–12 through 1953–54 inclusive — 43 winters (1954 − 1911 = 43; 34 + 9 = 43 checks). First fixed by the first night in the book, 17 January 1912 (Ruth from the book and the Wexler record; Athey, whose "twenty-four years before" January 1936 also gives 1912). Last fixed by the last night heard, 9 February 1954 (Sable, *Sentinel*, Athey), with the bearings replaced in March 1954. Excluded: the three qualifying nights of the two winters after the rebuild (1954–55, 1955–56), which Judd's own last entry treats as "since the rockers went in" and which the summary of 43 winters was closed before — the bearing that produced the sound no longer existed. (Including them changes nothing at one decimal: 61/45 = 1.4; 69 qualifying; (c) unchanged.) Also excluded: the seven sub-six nights of 1897–1909 in the Wexler record, which predate the book. Rejected: Dorsey's and Athey's 57/30/13 (would give 1.3 and 1.9) and Lettie's/Quarles's 8 silent (69) — see E1.

**C4.** (a) Built 1889, cut up May 1929: **40 years** (39 if she left the works after May 1889; the build month is not in the sources).
(b) $2,150 ÷ $8,600 = 0.25 → **25%** (exactly one quarter; the transposed $2,510 would give 29.2%).
(c) Sold 19 June 1901; withdrawn September 1928 on the arrival of the eight-coupled engine: **27 years** (27 years 3 months; 27 years 11 months to the cutting-up), matching Alma's "twenty-seven years she worked for us."

**C5.** (a) 120 + 300 + 120 = **540 feet** (Pike, Quarles; the *Sentinel*'s and Athey's "six hundred" are rejected by this arithmetic).
(b) 2.2% over 9 miles (milepost 31 to 22): 9 × 5,280 = 47,520 ft; × 0.022 = **1,045 feet** (1,045.4). Quarles's "eight miles" would give 929 ft; the mileposts, given by two independent sources, settle nine.
(c) Record high 101° (July 1936); record low −11° (January 1918; the keeper and the record's own index sheet — Ruth's and the *Sentinel*'s −13 rejected, see E1). Range 112°. At 1 inch per 40°: 112 ÷ 40 = **2.8 inches**. (With −13: 2.85; with the 50° rule: 2.24.) Note the implication: from a 66° closing the cold side needs 77/40 = 1.9 in — inside the 2 inches set in 1897; from the 86° re-seat it needs 97/40 = 2.4 in — outside them, which is the whole story.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** Both were **necessary but not sufficient**. Necessary: all 61 heard nights had a low below six and followed a day at least thirty degrees warmer — "not an exception in the book" (Judd), checked night by night against an independent record (Ruth; Lettie's dozen random dates "agreed, every one, to the degree"). No night outside the conditions was ever heard; Dorsey's "three weeks at zero and never a thing" shows a low alone does not do it, and Judd's threshold rule shows a fall alone does not either. Not sufficient: 5 of the 66 qualifying nights gave nothing (the summary page), and after March 1954 three more did. The five silent nights share a third feature — each sits inside a cold spell in which she had already spoken with no day above forty between — so Judd's "not twice inside one freeze," or Quarles's mechanism (a released bearing sits at the end of its travel until a thaw walks it back), is the missing condition; with it, the three conditions were jointly sufficient throughout the 43 winters, and the bearing change of 1954 removed the mechanism altogether.

**D2.** (1) *The contemporaneous record of the wreck*: nobody died and the engine never entered the gorge — she lay on her side at the north approach in daylight and was rerailed in nine days (Adela's field book p. 71, written that week; the *Sentinel* of 8 March 1898; Emil, on the ground that morning, via Wendell; Pike). (2) *Another county's books and another family's memory*: the CVR stock book sold her to Tolliver on 19 June 1901 "on her own wheels," the Tolliver shop book cut her up at Sixmile in May 1929, and Alma Nace's family remembers her 27 working years in Bly County — she was two hundred miles away for every one of the 43 winters. (3) *The timing in two records kept by people with no theory*: the sound began on 17 January 1912, fourteen years after the wreck, on the first sub-six night after the 1909 re-seat, having stayed silent through seven earlier sub-six nights (the Wexler record), and it stopped permanently in March 1954 when only the bearings were changed — a thing in the creek bed does not switch on with a bearing reset and off with a bearing requisition. (4) *The sound's own physics*: it came up the rails first — the telegraph sounder rattled before the floor moved (Judd) — and struck once, never twice in a night, at an exact temperature; that is a structure releasing along continuous rail, not iron under water (Judd, Quarles). (5) *The survey*: 800 feet of dry bed with nothing of iron larger than a spike (Ruth; the *Sentinel*'s photographs). Independence: (1) rests on 1898 documents and eyewitnesses; (2) on ledgers kept for another purpose by strangers who never heard the sound; (3) on weather and station books kept by people who compared nothing; (4) on the sound's transmission; (5) on physical inspection — none borrows from another. Reasons (1), (2), (3) and (4) would all stand if the 1962 survey had never been made.

**D3.** No — not on any evidence the sources contain. The documents say the opposite of a dismissal: the letter of 30 April 1901 reduced the party "entire" on 15 May, paid her to that day, and had Tice himself asking that his recommendation be entered on her card; the slip of 20 July 1901 re-engaged a rodman and a chainman for field work on the Ashby extension, and she was the road's only computer, a desk post a field party does not carry. She stayed on the party three years and eight months after the entry, through the wreck and after it. The only claim of a connection is Pike's — "never forgave that entry, and she was gone inside four years" — which he admits rests on "nothing in writing," being office talk relayed by his father; and the dinner remark at two removes has Tice conceding she was right, not resenting her. Wendell, as her son, is an interested defender, but the letter is not his testimony. What cannot be excluded is an unrecorded motive behind a general reduction; nothing in the sources supports one, so the documented answer stands.

## Section E — Contradictions

Format: claims — narrators — resolution — method.

2. **Thermal rule**: 1 in/40° (Adela's book via Ruth r04 and Sable r07) vs 1 in/50° (Pike r02; Quarles r09). → 40°. Quoted document; arithmetic (only 40° yields the observed +6° threshold; Quarles concedes the mismatch).
3. **Sheet 11 travel**: 3 in (Adela's entry; Ruth; Sable) vs 4 in (Pike; Quarles, "each way"). → 3. Quoted document; "each way" is the 1954 rocker specification.
4. **Grade length**: nine miles (Judd; mileposts 31→22 in Pike and Judd) vs eight (Quarles). → nine. Arithmetic on mileposts.
5. **Who set the nest**: Anders Berg (Dorsey) / Cass Nolan (Athey) / "we set" (Tice via Emil–Adela–Wendell). → CBD as to the hand; the decision was Tice's (Adela's entry). One hearsay source each; no method breaks it.
6. **Warren Tice's role**: resident engineer (Pike; Wendell; Athey; "W.T." in the field book) vs "no hand in that bridge, in the Ninestone office the whole time" (Dorsey), who also says his father "closed the span himself in ninety-seven." → Resident engineer. Document + majority of independent sources + Dorsey's internal contradiction.
7. **Dorsey's relation to Warren**: son (Dorsey; Wendell) vs nephew (Vesta; Athey). → Son. First-person testimony and a contemporary family; the two "nephew" sources share one town tradition.
8. **Construction records**: burned 4 April 1911, "nothing left about that bridge but my book" (Pike; Adela via Wendell) vs "the contract drawings for the viaduct are held here" (Quarles, county office, 1971). → CBD whether the county holds a set the railroad lost; what is established is that the railroad's set burned, and Quarles's reading of Sheet 11 matches the 1954 rockers, which discredits his set as a source for 1897.
9. **Adela's birth year**: 1874 (Ruth; Athey) vs 1876 (Wendell; Sable). → 1874. Arithmetic: both Ruth and Wendell say she died in 1959 at eighty-five; 1959 − 85 = 1874. Sable's date came from Wendell's slip.
10. **Adela's dates of service**: 1895–1901 (Wendell) vs "before I was born [1888]" (Judd). → 1895–1901. The 1901 letter is a document; the viaduct (1896–97) falls inside Wendell's dates.
11. **Why Adela left**: dismissed over the entry (Pike) vs party reduced entire with Tice's recommendation (the letter; Wendell). → Reduction. Quoted document. (See D3.)
12. **Deaths in the 1898 wreck**: crew killed and never brought up (Vesta; the tradition in Sentinel and Athey ch. IV) vs nobody killed, two hurt (Adela p. 71; *Sentinel* 8 March 1898; Pike; Emil via Wendell; Athey ch. III). → Nobody. Contemporaneous documents.
13. **Sale price of No. 9**: $2,150 (stock book, quoted in words and figures by Pike, photostat transcribed by Sable) vs $2,510 (Alma, twice; *Sentinel*). → $2,150. Quoted document; it is exactly 25% of cost.
14. **Sale year**: 19 June 1901 (stock book; Athey's press note; *Sentinel*) vs 1902 (Alma). → 1901. Document; Alma's own "1901 my grandfather decided" and "twelve-year-old" (1889 + 12) agree.
15. **Re-decking year**: 1908 (Pike) / August 1909 (Judd's written note; Ruth; Sable; Quarles; Lettie's framing) / "summer of 1911" (Judd, spoken) → August 1909. The written note copied from the depot slate is a document; 1911 is the fire year, likely confused.
16. **Onset of the sound**: winter 1897–98 (Vesta; *Sentinel* "the winter after the wreck") / about 1908 (Pike) / 1907 or 1913 (Judd) / 17 January 1912 (Ruth from the night book and the Wexler record; Athey). → 17 January 1912. Two documents; the Wexler record has seven sub-six nights 1897–1909 with no sound and nobody at the Falls ever claimed one.
17. **Judd's night start**: 1907 (Judd) vs 1909 (Dorsey; Wendell). → 1909. Two independent sources against a self-contradicting memory (Judd also dates the book's start to 1913).
18. **Judd's book start**: 1907 / "winter of 1913" (Judd) vs 1912 (Ruth; Athey). → 1912. Documents; Judd's two dates cancel each other.
19. **Night-book totals**: 61 nights / 34 winters / 9 (Judd; Ruth; Lettie; Sable's verbatim transcription) vs 57 / 30 / 13 (Dorsey; Athey). → 61/34/9. Quoted document and the keeper.
20. **Silent qualifying nights**: 5 (66 total) (Judd; Sable's transcription; Ruth) vs 8 (69) (Lettie; Quarles "at second hand"). → 5/66. Quoted document; Quarles disclaims first-hand knowledge.
21. **Deaths within a week**: 7 of 190 (Judd; Athey) vs "better than a third" (Dorsey). → 7 of 190. The keeper's count; Dorsey's is a recollection of a conversation.
22. **Judd's silence on the death count**: "never said at the store" (Judd) vs "he told me once" (Dorsey). → CBD; immaterial.

### Two narrators carrying the same wrong value — and what breaks the tie

- **Pike and Quarles — 1 inch per 50°.** Broken by Adela's contemporaneous rule line (one document, read by two independent readers) and by arithmetic: only 40° yields the +6° binding point at which the book records every one of 61 nights and none above; Quarles himself says his 50° result does not fit the record.
- **Pike and Quarles — Sheet 11 = 4 inches.** Broken by Adela's entry written with the sheet in front of her, and by the phrase "four inches of travel each way," which is the 1954 rocker specification (Judd, Ruth, Quarles §4) — both men had the later figure and carried it back; the railroad's drawings burned in 1911 and Pike admits nothing in writing.
- **Pike and Quarles — the forty tons as cause.** Broken by Quarles's own 1954 record: nothing changed but the bearings, the heavy deck still stands, and the sound stopped; and by the two silent winters after the re-decking.
- ***Sentinel* and Athey — "six hundred feet."** Broken by the three span lengths (Pike from the office, Quarles from the drawings) summing to 540; Athey compiled from the newspaper's back files, so the two are one source.
- ***Sentinel* and Athey — the wager made in the depot, both men sitting up.** Broken by both eyewitnesses: Judd and Dorsey put it at the store counter, and Dorsey heard it in bed upstairs; again one derivative source.
- **Dorsey and Athey — 57 nights, 30 winters with, 13 without.** Broken by the book's summary page transcribed verbatim by the archivist and confirmed by the keeper and two readers (Ruth, Lettie); Athey's figures match Dorsey's exactly and came from "the town's memory."
- **Lettie and Quarles — 8 silent nights, 69 qualifying.** Broken by the same transcription and the keeper; Quarles states he has the book at second hand, and Lettie read it for an hour on her kitchen table.
- **Alma and the *Sentinel* — $2,510.** Broken by the stock-book entry in words and figures, which is exactly one quarter of the $8,600 cost; the reporter had the sale second-hand from the survey and Alma transposed two digits she repeated.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The sound was the center span's south bearing binding and releasing. The 300-foot truss was pinned at the north end and free at the south on a roller nest whose seat, set in September 1897 at 66°, gave two inches of cold travel — 80 degrees' worth at the span's rule of one inch per 40° — instead of the three inches (120°) on Sheet 11. In August 1909 the re-decking gang jacked the shoes and re-seated the nest to the same two-inch mark with the steel at 86°, so from then on the travel ran out at 86 − 80 = 6° above zero. *Character*: when the span cooled past that point the shoe hung up, contraction went on, strain accumulated in the truss and pins, and the bearing let go all at once — a single deep report, never twice in a night, carried along the continuous rail faster than through air, so the depot's telegraph sounder rattled and the floors moved before anyone heard it. *Threshold*: below six degrees — the arithmetic of the 1909 seating temperature, and the number the night book gives 61 times and never exceeds. *Second condition*: a fall of thirty degrees within the day, because a slow cold lets the nest creep in small friction-limited steps and store nothing, while a fast fall carries the bearing past its freedom before it can relieve itself. *Silent qualifying nights*: after a release the bearing sits at the cold end of its seat and cannot release again until a thaw above about forty degrees walks it back; all five silent nights fall inside a cold spell after a release with no such day between ("not twice inside one freeze"). *Onset*: before 1909 the binding point was −14°; the coldest night of 1897–1909 was −9 (4 February 1899), so seven sub-six nights passed in silence; the two winters after the re-seat never went below +9; the first night under six after August 1909 was 17 January 1912 (high 34, low −2, a 36-degree fall), and that was the first night in the book. *Cessation*: in March 1954 rocker bearings with four inches of travel each way (160°) replaced the nest; no valley temperature can exhaust them; the last night heard was 9 February 1954, and three qualifying nights in the next two winters gave nothing.

*Competing explanation for the onset*: the forty tons the 1909 deck added — Pike's "I say the weight did it," repeated as opinion by Quarles — the dead load, not the travel, bringing the rollers to bind. It fails on three pieces of evidence. First, the weight was there from August 1909, yet the winters of 1909–10 and 1910–11 passed in silence and the sound waited for the first sub-six night: it tracked temperature, not load. Second, a weight theory predicts no particular threshold, no thirty-degree condition and no reset by thaw; the travel arithmetic predicts all three and lands on the exact number in the book. Third, and decisively, the 1909 deck was not disturbed in 1954 — "nothing was altered in March 1954 but the bearings themselves" and the deck "stands over the span today" (the county's own rehabilitation record) — yet the sound stopped that month and never returned; had forty tons been the cause, the forty tons are still there. (The other competing account, the ghost of No. 9, is defeated in D2.)

*Physical evidence recovered in 1962*: the cast-iron south pedestal, found forty feet below the south abutment where the 1954 gang threw it, carrying on its roller seat the chisel setting mark at two inches against the drawing's three — measured three times, "the only physical evidence of the setting of 1897 now in existence," now on the floor of the Society's north store room at Ninestone.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** In 1897 a short-line railroad bridged Sallow Creek and set the south roller nest of its 300-foot span an inch short of the drawing; the party's computer, Adela Rennick, recorded the overruled objection. A runaway engine derailed there in 1898, hurting two men; sold north in 1901, it hauled logs for 27 years. When the deck was re-seated at 86 degrees in 1909, the bridge began, from January 1912, to let go with one report on nights that fell thirty degrees to below six — and the valley called it the engine's ghost. Judd Rennick logged 61 nights in 43 winters; new bearings in 1954 ended it. In 1962 a dry creek gave Ruth Frayne the marked pedestal, and four records — field book, night book, weather book, lumber ledgers — each held a piece none could prove alone.
