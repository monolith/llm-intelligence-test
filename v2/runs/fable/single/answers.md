# Answer sheet — Cadder Falls bundle (single read)

Conventions: r01–r12 = the twelve retellings in bundle order. "Document" = a text quoted verbatim from a physical record (field book, night book, ledger, newspaper, letter). Where I say "resolved," the method is named. Where the sources do not decide, I say so.

---

## Section A — Reconstruction

### A1. The building of the Sallow Creek Viaduct

The Cadder Valley Railroad was a 31-mile branch from Ninestone (milepost 0: county seat, shops, office) to the Ashby quarry (milepost 31), built to bring stone down. Its one great structure was the Sallow Creek Viaduct at milepost 22, a mile above the mill town of Cadder Falls (milepost 23). From the quarry the line fell nine miles at a steady 2.2 percent to the viaduct — a great deal of falling for a small road with second-hand brakes.

Work began in April 1896 under resident engineer Warren Tice, afterward the company's chief engineer at Ninestone (retired spring 1931, died 1937). Emil Rennick (1866–1931), a Cadder Falls carpenter, built the falsework. His younger sister Adela Rennick (1874–1959), with the railroad 1895–1901, was the party's computer and rodman; she kept her own black-oilcloth duplicate of the party's book. The finished bridge was three spans — 120, 300 and 120 feet, 540 feet in all — 97 feet above the water. The 300-foot center span was a pin-connected truss, pinned at its north end and free at its south, where it rode on a nest of rollers seated in a cast pedestal so the steel could lengthen in heat and shorten in cold. The working rule entered in the field book: one inch of movement per 300 feet per 40 degrees.

On 12 September 1897 at 3:40 p.m., thermometer at 66°, the span was closed. Sheet 11 of the contract drawings called for three inches of cold travel; the rollers were set at the two-inch mark. Adela reported it to Tice, who answered that the pedestal could not be recast before the fall haul and that two inches was more than the valley would ever ask for. Overruled, she wrote it down "so that it is somewhere." The line opened to Cadder Falls on 14 November 1897.

By the arithmetic Tice was nearly right: as built the span would bind only at −14°, and the valley's coldest reading in the next twelve years was −9 (4 February 1899). Nothing happened, and a wrong bridge behaved like a right one.

The party was reduced entire on 15 May 1901 (letter of 30 April 1901, carrying Tice's recommendation for her card). Two field men — a rodman and a chainman — were re-engaged 1 August 1901 for the Ashby extension; she was not. She married Josiah Frayne in 1902 and never worked for a railroad again. On 4 April 1911 the Ninestone roundhouse and office burned with the whole construction record, leaving her book the only written account of how the bridge was built.

Not determinable from the sources: who physically set the nest (Anders Berg per r08, Cass Nolan per r12), and whether Tice really said at his 1931 retirement dinner "we set that nest a mark shy" (r10, at two removes; denied by r08, who was present).

### A2. The night book and what was heard from the bridge

What the valley heard was one deep report — not a roll, not a rattle — felt in the floor before it was heard, always between midnight and four, never twice in a night, and only on "killing nights": a soft day followed by a hard, sudden cold. At the depot it came up the rails first: the telegraph sounder rattled, then the floor, then the sound.

Its precondition was set in August 1909, when a bridge gang under foreman Coyle spent three weeks re-decking the viaduct — 85-lb rail for 60, new ties, a deck forty tons heavier — and jacked both shoes and reset them to the same two-inch mark with the north-pier thermometer at 86° at four o'clock. Two mild winters followed (never below +9). The first night under six degrees after that was 17 January 1912 — high 34, low −2, clear and still — and the bridge spoke.

Judd Rennick (1888–1969), Emil's son, night operator at Cadder Falls depot from 1909 and agent 1915–June 1954, ruled a five-column book the next morning: date, day's high, night's low, whether she spoke, the hour. He kept no theory. Over 43 winters (1911–12 through 1953–54) the bridge spoke 61 nights; 34 winters had at least one, nine had none; the fullest was 1917–18 with five. From the columns he drew two conditions that never failed: night's low below 6°, and a fall of at least 30° from the day's high. Five nights met both and gave nothing; all five sat inside a cold spell where she had already spoken and no thaw above 40° had intervened — she would not speak twice in one freeze.

The town held that she rang for a death. He counted: 190 deaths in Cadder Falls in the years of the book, seven within a week of a night she spoke. On 14 January 1936, in Dorsey Tice's store — 41° at noon, 38° at four, falling 4° an hour, no thaw since mid-December — he said she would speak before morning; Dorsey bet five dollars she would not. The low was −3; she spoke at 1:10 a.m.; Dorsey paid next day and told it on himself for 25 years.

The valley's own explanation: on 6 March 1898 locomotive No. 9 ran away down the quarry grade and left the rails at the north approach of the new bridge. Nobody was killed; she was rerailed in nine days and sold in 1901. Tradition nonetheless buried her and a crew in the gorge, dated the sound from the winter after the wreck, and heard her "working the grade." The ghost was fourteen years older than the sound.

Last heard 9 February 1954. In March 1954 the road was rebuilt for diesels: roller nest out, rocker bearings with four inches of travel each way in, pedestal thrown into the creek. Three qualifying nights in the next two winters brought nothing. Last entry, 2 March 1956: "I do not know now what it was I was listening to." The Falls felt robbed.

### A3. The locomotive's second life

Cadder Valley No. 9 was a 4-4-0 American type from the Rowan Locomotive Works, built 1889, works number 1142, bought new for $8,600 — the best machine the little road owned. On the morning of 6 March 1898 she came down the 2.2 percent quarry grade with a work train and brakes that would not hold, and at 7:20 left the iron at the north approach of the new viaduct. Two of the gang were hurt (Lidell's arm broken, Sherrod scalded); nobody was killed; she lay on her side in plain view and was rerailed in nine days. The *Ninestone Sentinel* of 8 March 1898 said so, and Adela Rennick's field book, page 71, says the same.

On 19 June 1901 the company sold her to the Tolliver Lumber Company of Bly County, two hundred miles north, for $2,150 — a quarter of her cost, and a small personal wound to the chief clerk who wrote the entry. She went "delivered on her own wheels": eleven days and four railroads under her own steam, with a Pell County crew who handed her over at the interchange, drank coffee in the Tolliver kitchen, and went home as passengers.

Silas Tolliver (1849–1922) had wanted a road engine — big wheels, long wheelbase — to bring made-up trains of logs down the main stem of the Sixmile tram (by its end nineteen miles of 30-lb rail up three drainages) faster than a man could walk. His daughter Pearl Tolliver Nace (b. 1881), the company's bookkeeper for 39 years, found the advertisement and noted the $8,600 in the margin. The shop painted out the 9 and painted on a 4, the company numbering engines in the order bought. One bad season on grades she was never built for ("a racehorse to pull a stump"), then 27 years on the main stem: boiler retube 1909, new tires 1913, frame welded 1919 and again 1922, a heavy shopping 1924. Children at Sixmile felt her in the floor before they heard her whistle.

In September 1928 a second-hand eight-coupled engine displaced her; she sat out the winter under a tarpaulin. In May 1929 she was cut up at Sixmile, scrap to Ravel Brothers for $470; the boiler shell served the mill as a wash-water tank until the mill came down.

The cut ran out in 1936. In 1938 Pearl drove the stock book, shop book, timber ledgers and eleven boxes of correspondence to the Bly County Historical Rooms, where they drew no inquiry for 24 years — until Ruth Frayne's letter of 1962, answered by photostats by return, and Corwin Athey's in 1966. While the valley mourned her under Sallow Creek, she had hauled logs for 27 years and died in pieces two hundred miles away.

### A4. The 1962 survey and what it established

In September 1962 the state's flood-control cut took Sallow Creek out of its bed for eleven weeks. Ruth Frayne (b. 1934), three years a civil engineer with the Pell County highway department, went down for a routine pier inspection with a level and a camera; the *Sentinel* went along. It stopped being routine on the second afternoon.

**No locomotive.** She walked more than 800 feet of dry bed above and below the viaduct: no frame, no boiler, no wheel, nothing of iron larger than a spike. Photographed.

**The pedestal.** Forty feet below the south abutment lay the old cast-iron south shoe of the center span, thrown there by the 1954 gang. On its roller seat is the erection setting mark, chiselled at two inches; measured three times with a steel tape. The 1954 rockers, also measured, allow four inches each way.

**The field book.** Her father Wendell Frayne handed her his mother Adela's black book. Its front matter gives the rule (one inch per 300 feet per 40°); page 62 records the closing on 12 September 1897 at 66°, rollers set at two inches against Sheet 11's three, reported to W.T. and overruled.

**The night book.** Judd Rennick, her father's first cousin, lent it: 61 nights in 43 winters, every one below 6° at the bottom of a fall of 30° or more, plus his own summing of qualifying silent nights, and the copied note of the August 1909 gang seating the south nest at 86°.

**The weather.** Lettie Wexler of Kettle Bench, no relation, lent four boxes of a daily record kept by her father Peter and herself since 1926. It agrees with the night book on all 61 nights, to the degree. It also settles the onset: between November 1897 and August 1909, seven nights below 6°, four with 30° falls, nothing heard; coldest −9 on 4 February 1899; the winters of 1909–10 and 1910–11 never below +9; the first night under 6° after the re-decking, 17 January 1912 (high 34, low −2), is the first night in the night book.

**The arithmetic.** Three inches = 120° of cooling: bind at −54. Two inches = 80°: bind at −14 — never reached. Reset to two inches at 86°: bind at +6, the night book's threshold exactly.

**Bly County.** Photostats by return: No. 9 sold 19 June 1901, delivered on her own wheels; cut up May 1929 at Sixmile.

Conclusion, written on the tailgate: "What the valley heard for forty-three winters was a bridge letting go of itself, one inch late." Field book, pedestal and report went to the Historical Society in 1963, the weather record in 1967, the night book in 1969. The county engineer's 1971 memo confirmed the mechanism (while carrying a wrong coefficient and admitting its own numbers did not reconcile). Her grandmother had measured the bridge and was not believed; she measured it again 65 years later and her grandmother was right.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Brother and sister: Emil (b. 1866) the elder, Adela (b. 1874) his younger sister. Stated by Judd (r03, "my father had a younger sister, Adela"), Wendell (r10), the curator (r07) and the pamphlet (r12); no source disagrees.

**B2.** Judd was Adela's nephew. Judd himself: "My father was Emil Rennick" and Adela was "my father's younger sister" (r03); Wendell (r10): Judd "was Emil's son, which made him my mother's nephew"; r07 and r12 agree. What points elsewhere: (i) Ruth's "my father's uncle, Judd Rennick" (r04) — which would make Judd Adela's brother; Wendell explains she "used to call him uncle and he never corrected her" (r10), so this is a family courtesy title, not a relation; (ii) Vesta's "grand-niece" (r01) is the same error at second hand; (iii) the curator's swapped wrapper attributions (field book "J. Rennick", night book "A. Rennick", r07) — resolved by the "A.R." initials on the field-book entry, by the field book's provenance ("his mother's book") and the night book's (Judd's estate); the curator flagged them himself as a query.

**B3.** First cousin once removed. Chain: Judd is Emil's son (r03, r10, r12); Adela is Emil's sister (B1); Wendell is Adela's son (r10, r07); so Judd and Wendell are first cousins (r07, r10 both say so). Ruth is Wendell's daughter (r04 "my father, Wendell Frayne"; r10). A first cousin's child is a first cousin once removed — exactly Wendell's own correction (r10). Ruth's "uncle" and Vesta's "grand-niece" are the courtesy usage.

**B4.** Warren Tice — the viaduct's resident engineer, who overruled Adela's objection in 1897 and let the nest stay at two inches. Dorsey says so himself ("My father… Warren Tice, his name was," r08), and Wendell confirms "his son Dorsey" (r10); r01 and r12 say "nephew," but a man's own statement of who his father was outranks town memory. It matters because on 14 January 1936 the man who bet five dollars that the bridge would stay quiet was the son of the engineer whose decision made it speak, and he lost to the nephew of the woman who had warned his father — betting with a store thermometer against a book kept the way she kept hers. Warren had never told his son a word about the bridge (r08), so Dorsey was betting blind on his own father's work; and Dorsey's testy denial that his father "had no hand in that bridge" (contradicted a minute later by "he had closed the span himself") shows why the identity is sensitive.

**B5.** Chain of custody to Ruth Frayne, county highway engineer, autumn 1962:
- *Field book*: Adela kept it 1901–1959, on the shelf over her chair; on her death (1959) it passed to her son Wendell; in September 1962 he took it down and put it into Ruth's hands when she said she was going to the creek (r04, r10, r07 provenance). Deposited with the Society 1963.
- *Night book*: kept by Judd 1912–1956; in 1962 Ruth read it through at his kitchen table and asked to take it; he lent it to her personally (r03, r04); returned; came to the Society from his estate in 1969 (r07).
- *Weather record*: begun by Peter Wexler, kept by his daughter Lettie from 1926; in autumn 1962 Ruth "came up the hill" and Lettie lent all four boxes without a receipt (r06, r10, r04); every box came back; deposited 1967 (r07).
- *Bly County ledgers*: Pearl Tolliver Nace deposited the Tolliver stock and shop books at the Bly County Historical Rooms in 1938 (r05, r07); Ruth wrote to them and received photostats by return (r04); the two pages stayed with her report at the Society (r07); the originals never left Bly County.
Ruth was the single point of convergence: the field book through her father, the night book through her cousin, the weather through a neighbor's kindness, and the ledgers through the post.

## Section C — Math (show working)

**Inputs used (resolved in E1):** rule = 1 inch per 300 ft per 40 °F (field book, r04/r07 document; not r02/r09's 50°); span = 300 ft, so 1 inch = 40 °F; closed at 66 °F; Sheet 11 = 3 inches; as set = 2 inches; 1909 reset at 86 °F.

**C1.**
(a) 3 in × 40 °F/in = 120 °F of cooling; 66 − 120 = **−54 °F**.
(b) 2 in × 40 = 80 °F; 66 − 80 = **−14 °F**.
(c) 2 in from 86 °F: 86 − 80 = **+6 °F**.
(d) Margin lost = (−14) → (+6) = **20 °F** (equal to the 86 − 66 rise in seating temperature).
Check: +6 is exactly the night-book threshold ("below six, never at six and a half"), an independent record — this is what validates the 40° rule. With r02/r09's 50° and 4 inches the answers would be −134, −34, −14 and 20; but −14 would never have bound in a valley whose record low is −11 (or −13), so that pair cannot produce the 61 nights; Quarles himself admits his (c) "is not readily reconciled with the local record."

**C2.**
(a) Threshold 6 °F. 38 − 6 = 32 °F to fall; 32 ÷ 4 °F/hr = 8 hours; 4 p.m. + 8 h = **about midnight** (strictly "below six" is crossed just after midnight). Matches Judd's own sum at the counter.
(b) Day's high 41 (noon); night's low −3; fall = 41 − (−3) = **44 °F**; 44 ≥ 30, so **yes**, the second condition was satisfied (as was the first: −3 < 6; and Judd's third, a rest since the mid-December thaw).

**C3.**
Winters counted: the 43 winters of the phenomenon, **1911–12 through 1953–54** (1953 − 1911 + 1 = 43). First fixed by the first night heard, 17 January 1912 (Wexler record r06 document; Ruth r04; pamphlet r12 "24 years before" 1936), which is when Judd ruled up the book. Last fixed by the last night heard, 9 February 1954 (r07, r11, r12), followed by the March 1954 bearing replacement. Excluded: the two post-rocker winters 1954–55 and 1955–56, with their three qualifying silent nights — recorded two leaves after the summary, after the mechanism had been removed, and not in the book's own totals (Judd's five silent nights all sit "in a cold spell where she had already spoken," i.e. within the sound era). Counts used: 61 nights, 34 winters with ≥1, 9 with none, 5 silent qualifying (book's summary page, r07 verbatim; r03 author) — not r08/r12's 57/30/13 or r06/r09's 8.
(a) 61 ÷ 43 = 1.4186 → **1.4** nights per logged winter.
(b) 61 + 5 = **66** nights met both conditions.
(c) 61 ÷ 34 = 1.794 → **1.8** nights per winter with at least one.
(If Lettie's "eight" were right, (b) would be 69; I explain in E1 why 5 + the 3 post-1954 nights = 8 is the likely origin of that figure.)

**C4.**
(a) Built 1889; cut up May 1929: 1929 − 1889 = **40 years** (39 if built after May 1889 — the build month is not given).
(b) 2,150 ÷ 8,600 = 0.25 = **25%**. (The r05/r11 figure $2,510 would give 29.2%; rejected — see E1.)
(c) Sold and delivered June 1901; set out behind the shop September 1928: **27 years** working (27¼; r05 says "twenty-seven years she worked for us"). Owned until scrapped May 1929: just under 28.

**C5.**
(a) 120 + 300 + 120 = **540 ft** (r02, r09; not r11/r12's 600).
(b) Quarry MP 31 to viaduct MP 22 = 9 miles (r02, r03; r09's "eight" contradicts his own mileposts). 9 × 5,280 = 47,520 ft; × 0.022 = **1,045 ft** (1,045.4). (Over 8 miles it would be 929 ft.)
(c) Record high 101 °F (July 1936); record low −11 °F (January 1918; keeper r06 + index sheet r07 — see E1). Range 112 °F ÷ 40 °F/in = **2.8 inches**. (If the low were −13: 114/40 = 2.85 in; under the 50° rule 2.24 in.)

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** Necessary but not sufficient. Necessary: on all 61 nights the bridge was heard the low was below 6° and the day-to-night fall was 30° or more — "not an exception in the book" (r03), confirmed against an independent thermometer three miles off on every one of the 61 (r04) and on a random dozen "to the degree" (r06). Not sufficient: 66 nights met both conditions and five gave nothing (r03, r07); each condition alone is plainly not enough (three weeks at zero with no sound, r08; "a cold night on the end of a cold day was nothing to her," r03); and outside the 1912–54 window the conditions were met four times in 1897–1909 and three times in 1954–56 with silence (r04, r06, r09). The missing third condition is Judd's own: no release since the last thaw above about 40° (r03), which Quarles explains mechanically — a released bearing sits at the end of its travel with nothing left to release until warmed back (r09 §14). Add that, and within 1912–54 the conditions become jointly sufficient in the record: all five silent nights fall inside a freeze in which she had already spoken.

**D2.** Independent reasons it was not No. 9:
1. *The ledgers* (Pike r02, Nace r05, photostats r07, Athey's note r12): sold 19 June 1901, delivered north on her own wheels, worked 27 years as Tolliver No. 4, cut up May 1929. She was 200 miles away when the sound began in 1912 and scrap for the last 25 winters of it. Holds without the survey — the entries date from 1901 and 1929 and were deposited in 1938.
2. *The contemporary record of the wreck* (the *Sentinel* of 8 March 1898 quoted by r02; field book p. 71 quoted by r07; eyewitness Emil via r10): nobody killed, engine on her side at the north approach in daylight, rerailed in nine days. No crew and no engine ever went into the gorge. Holds without the survey.
3. *The onset* (Wexler record r06; night book r04/r12): the sound began 17 January 1912, fourteen years after the wreck, after seven cold nights (four fully qualifying) in 1897–1909 had passed in silence and nobody ever claimed otherwise. A wreck has no reason to wait for a re-decking. Holds without the survey — both records predate it.
4. *The cessation* (r02, r08, r09, r11, r12): it stopped for good in the month the bearings were changed, though nothing in the creek was touched. Holds without the survey.
5. *The physics of the sound* (Judd r03: sounder, floor, then noise, up the rails first; Quarles r09: a single stored-strain release carried by continuous rail, "none whatever" like an exhaust; exact thermal threshold): a structural event, not a machine.
6. *The dry creek* (Ruth r04, *Sentinel* r11): 800 feet of bed, nothing bigger than a spike.
They are independent because each rests on a different kind of evidence (company ledger; contemporary newspaper and eyewitness; a farm thermometer; a railroad work order; mechanics; physical inspection), from different narrators, in two counties, at different dates (1898, 1901–29, 1912, 1954, 1962); any one could be wrong without touching the others. Reasons 1–5 all stand if the survey had never been made.

**D3.** Cannot be determined from the sources — though the documents lean against a causal link. For a link: only Pike (r02), who says Tice "never forgave that entry, and she was gone inside four years," while conceding "I have nothing in writing" and that he was a boy reading "the temperature of a room." Against: the letter itself (r07, r10) reduces "the engineering party entire" and carries Tice's request that "his recommendation be entered upon your card" — an odd act from a man taking revenge; the two re-engaged in August were a rodman and a chainman for field work, and she was the road's only computer (r10); and Tice kept her on for four years after the entry, which is slow for a grudge. But Wendell is her son and is arguing a case; the recommendation could be a form of words; and she kept the re-engagement slip folded inside the letter, which at least suggests she felt the contrast — "why she kept it is not something the papers say" (r07). She herself never spoke of it (r03). What the sources establish: the whole party was let go, the letter was courteous, two field men were re-hired and she was not. What they do not establish: anyone's motive. That is not enough to answer either way.

## Section E — Contradictions

Format: claims — narrators — resolution — method. "Same wrong value" cases are marked **[SWV]** and the tie-breaker stated.

1. **Movement rule**: 1 in/300 ft per 40° (r04, r07 quoting the field book) vs per 50° (r02, r09). **[SWV: r02, r09]** Resolved 40°. Method: document (field book front matter, transcribed identically by two readers) plus arithmetic — 40° yields a binding point of +6, matching the night book's threshold, an independent record; 50° yields −14, below anything the valley ever recorded, so it cannot produce 61 nights. Tie broken by the document and the independent check; Pike writes from memory 45 years on, and Quarles concedes his figure does not reconcile.
2. **Sheet 11 travel**: three inches (r04, r07 quoting field book p. 62 and the report) vs four inches (r02, r09). **[SWV: r02, r09]** Resolved three. Method: contemporary document; corroborated by internal consistency — Ruth's "one inch late" and the reported "a mark shy" (r10) both describe a one-inch shortfall; Quarles's phrase "four inches of travel each way" is the 1954 rocker specification transposed onto the drawing; and the company's drawings burned in 1911 (r02, r10), so his claim to hold Sheet 11 is doubtful. (r11's "two inches shy" is a journalist's garble of "the mark is at two inches.") Moderate confidence.
3. **Viaduct length**: 540 ft (r02, r09, span by span) vs 600 ft (r11, r12). **[SWV: r11, r12]** Resolved 540. Method: arithmetic, 120 + 300 + 120; r12 was compiled from the newspaper's files, so the two are not independent.
4. **Grade length**: nine miles (r03; r02's mileposts) vs eight (r09). Resolved nine. Method: arithmetic, MP 31 − MP 22, using r09's own mileposts.
5. **Sale price**: $2,150 (r02, r07 ledger entry verbatim) vs $2,510 (r05, r11). **[SWV: r05, r11]** Resolved $2,150. Method: document; the two wrong values are a digit transposition arrived at separately (a memory of a margin note; a reporter's note), and the photostat plus Pike's father's own entry break the tie.
6. **Sale year**: 19 June 1901 (r02, r04, r07, r12) vs 1902 (r05). Resolved 1901. Method: document.
7. **Re-decking date**: August 1909 (r03's written note, r04, r07, r09) vs 1908 (r02) vs "summer of 1911" (r03 spoken, contradicting his own note). Resolved August 1909. Method: document (the note copied off the slate) outranks memory, Judd's own rule; corroborated by Wexler's "two winters after" being 1909–10 and 1910–11.
8. **Bearing rehabilitation**: March 1954 (r02, r07, r08, r09, r11, r12, r04) vs spring 1953 (r03). Resolved 1954. Method: majority plus arithmetic — the last entry dated 2 March 1956 is "the second winter since the rockers," which fits 1954, not 1953; and the last night heard was 9 February 1954.
9. **Wager date**: 14 January 1936 (r08, r12; r11 January 1936) vs January 1937 (r03). Resolved 1936. Method: arithmetic and internal consistency — r12's "twenty-four years" of records (1912 + 24), r08's "twenty-five years" to 1961, "five dollars was five dollars in 1936."
10. **Wager venue**: Tice's store, with Dorsey hearing it in bed upstairs (r03, r08 — both participants) vs the depot, both men sitting up (r11, r12). **[SWV: r11, r12]** Resolved store. Method: the two participants agree with each other on every figure; r12 follows the paper and town talk. Tie broken by first-person testimony of both parties.
11. **Bet direction**: Vesta (r01) has Dorsey betting she *would* speak; all others have him betting she would not. Resolved: Dorsey bet against. Method: participants (r03, r08).
12. **Night totals**: 61 nights / 34 winters heard / 9 silent (r03, r04, r06, r07 summary page verbatim) vs 57 / 30 / 13 (r08, r12). **[SWV: r08, r12]** Resolved 61/34/9. Method: document (the summary page, transcribed by the curator) plus three independent readers of the book; both sets sum to 43 so arithmetic cannot decide; r12 draws on "the town's memory," i.e. Dorsey.
13. **Silent qualifying nights**: five, total 66 (r03 author; r07 summary page verbatim) vs eight, total 69 (r06, r09 "at second hand"). **[SWV: r06, r09]** Resolved five/66. Method: document plus author; tie broken by arithmetic — the three qualifying silent nights of 1954–56, written two leaves after the summary, make 5 + 3 = 8, which is what a one-hour reader (Lettie) or a second-hand memo would naturally add together.
14. **Deaths**: 7 of 190 within a week (r03, r12) vs "better than a third" (r08). Resolved 7/190. Method: the counter himself, twice (to the interviewer and to Athey); Dorsey's is a garbled remark.
15. **Onset**: 17 January 1912 (r04, r06 document, r12) vs the winter after the wreck, 1897–98 (r01, r11) vs winter of 1913 (r03). **[SWV: r01, r11]** Resolved 17 January 1912. Method: document (Wexler entry) plus the night book's first entry plus the Wexler silence of 1897–1909; r01 was born 1920 and r11 repeats tradition, so they are one source, not two. Judd's "1913" is memory against his own book, and contradicts his own "started the book" in 1907.
16. **Judd's start at the depot**: 1907 (r03) vs 1909 (r08, r10). Resolved 1909. Method: majority plus r03's internal inconsistency (book started "that same winter" vs first sound 1913/1912).
17. **1898 casualties**: crew killed (r01; r12 ch. IV before its own correction) vs nobody killed (r02 quoting the *Sentinel*, r07 quoting field book p. 71, r10 eyewitness Emil, r12 ch. III). Resolved none killed. Method: two contemporary documents and an eyewitness.
18. **Where No. 9 ended**: in the creek (r01, r12 ch. IV) vs sold 1901, scrapped 1929 in Bly County (r02, r04, r05, r07, r12 note). Resolved Bly County. Method: documents; r12 corrects itself.
19. **Dorsey's relation to Warren Tice**: son (r08 himself, r10) vs nephew (r01, r12). **[SWV: r01, r12]** Resolved son. Method: the man's own statement plus an unrelated family historian; r12 is town memory.
20. **Within r08**: "my father had no hand in that bridge, he was in the Ninestone office the whole time" vs "he told me he had closed the span himself in ninety-seven… he was proud of it." Resolved: Tice was resident engineer and closed the span (r02, r10, r12, field book "reported to W.T."). Method: narrator's internal inconsistency plus document.
21. **Who set the nest**: Anders Berg (r08) vs Cass Nolan (r12). **Cannot be settled**: one memory each, no document, Dorsey has a motive to name someone other than his father, and the pamphlet's source is town memory. Method: none available.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The sound was the center span's south bearing binding and then letting go.

*Mechanism.* The 300-foot truss is pinned at the north and free at the south, where it rode on a roller nest in a cast pedestal. Steel shortens as it cools — one inch per 300 feet per 40°. In 1897 the nest was set at two inches of cold travel instead of the three Sheet 11 called for. When the span cooled far enough to use up that travel the shoe hung up; contraction continued; strain was stored in the members and pins until the shoe slipped all at once and the span dropped to where it should have been. That is why the sound was *one* deep report, never a roll, never twice in a night, felt before heard, travelling along the rail so that the depot's telegraph sounder rattled before the floor moved and the floor before the ear (r03, r09).

*The threshold and why six.* The span was closed at 66°; with two inches, binding lay at 66 − 80 = −14°, colder than the valley ever got (coldest 1897–1909: −9). In August 1909 the gang reset both shoes to the same two-inch mark with the steel at 86°. The binding point moved with it: 86 − 80 = +6°. The night book records 61 nights, every one below six and none above (r03, r04).

*The second condition.* A slow decline lets the nest creep, overcoming friction a little at a time with no stored strain; only a fall of 30° or more within the day carries the bearing past its freedom faster than it can relieve itself (r09 §13). Hence "killing nights" only, and three weeks at zero without a sound (r08).

*Silent qualifying nights.* Once released, the shoe sits at the end of its travel with nothing left to release until a thaw above about 40° pulls it back; every one of the five silent nights sits in a freeze in which she had already spoken (r03, r09 §14). The conditions were necessary, not sufficient.

*Onset in January 1912 and not before.* Before 1909 the binding point was −14°, never reached: seven nights below six and four fully qualifying nights in 1897–1909 passed silent. After the reseating the two winters of 1909–11 never went below +9. The first night under six after August 1909 was 17 January 1912 (high 34, low −2), and it is the first night in the book (r04, r06).

*Permanent cessation.* In March 1954 the nest and pedestal were replaced by rockers with four inches of travel each way, which no temperature on the Kettle Bench record can exhaust. Last night heard 9 February 1954; three qualifying nights in 1954–56, silent; nothing since (r07, r09, r11, r12).

*The competing onset explanation offered by the sources* is Pike's and Quarles's: that the forty extra tons of the 1909 deck overloaded the rollers and made them bind (r02 §"one opinion"; r09 §10). It fails on the evidence: (1) dead load gives no reason for a sharp threshold at any particular temperature, whereas the travel arithmetic predicts +6 exactly and the book shows it; (2) it gives no reason for the rate-of-fall and thaw conditions, which are about the position of the shoe in its seat, not the weight on it; (3) the deck of 1909 was left in place in 1954 and "nothing was altered… but the bearings themselves" (r09 §15) — the weight stayed, the sound went, and Quarles himself credits the cessation to travel, not to relief of load; (4) the sound did not begin with the heavier deck but two and a half years later, on the first night the new binding point was crossed. The folk explanation (No. 9 in the gorge from 1898) fails for the reasons in D2.

*Physical evidence recovered in 1962.* The cast-iron south pedestal, thrown into the creek by the 1954 gang, found forty feet below the south abutment, with the erection setting mark chiselled on the roller seat at two inches — measured three times — against Sheet 11's three; together with the negative evidence of 800 feet of dry bed holding nothing larger than a spike.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** In 1897 a railroad bridge over Sallow Creek was closed with its roller bearing set one inch short of the drawing; the computer who objected, Adela Rennick, was overruled and wrote it in her own book. Nothing happened until an 1909 re-decking reset the same mark on a hot afternoon, moving the binding point to +6°. From January 1912 to February 1954 the span seized and let go on sudden-cold nights with one deep report; the valley blamed a locomotive wrecked in 1898, which had in fact been sold north and scrapped. Her nephew's night book, a neighbor's thermometer, a lumber company's ledger, and her granddaughter's 1962 survey — which found the marked pedestal and no engine — each held one piece; only together did they explain it. (118 words)
