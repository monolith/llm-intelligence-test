## Section A — Reconstruction

### A1. The building of the Sallow Creek Viaduct

The Cadder Valley Railroad was a 31-mile branch built to bring stone down from the Ashby quarry (milepost 31) to the county seat at Ninestone (milepost 0), where the shops and office stood. Its one great structure was the Sallow Creek Viaduct at milepost 22, a mile above the mill town of Cadder Falls (milepost 23). Work began in April 1896. The bridge is three spans — 120 feet, a 300-foot pin-connected truss over the gorge, and 120 feet — 540 feet in all, 97 feet above the water. The grade from the quarry down to the viaduct is 2.2 percent held over nine miles.

Warren Tice was resident engineer (later the road's chief engineer at Ninestone; retired spring 1931, died 1937; father of Dorsey Tice, the Cadder Falls storekeeper). Emil Rennick (1866–1931), a Falls carpenter, built the falsework. His younger sister Adela Rennick (born 1874), with the road 1895–1901 as the party's computer and rodman, kept her own duplicate field book in black oilcloth, carrying in its front matter the working rule for the long span: one inch of movement in 300 feet for every 40 degrees.

The truss was pinned at its north end; the south end rode on a nest of rollers in a cast pedestal so the span could shorten in the cold. The span was closed on 12 September 1897 at 3:40 p.m. with the thermometer at 66°F. Sheet 11 of the contract drawings called for three inches of cold travel; the rollers were set at the two-inch chisel mark. Adela reported it to Tice, who answered that the pedestal could not be recast before the fall haul and that two inches was more than the valley would ever ask for. She entered it on page 62 "so that it is somewhere." (Who physically set the nest — Anders Berg per Dorsey Tice, Cass Nolan per the school pamphlet — the sources cannot settle.)

The line opened to Cadder Falls on 14 November 1897. On 6 March 1898 at 7:20 a.m. locomotive No. 9, running away down the quarry grade with a work train, left the rails at the north approach; nobody was killed, two of the gang (Lidell, Sherrod) were hurt, and she was rerailed in nine days. The engineering party was reduced entire on 15 May 1901 (letter of 30 April), Tice's recommendation entered on Adela's card; two field men, Corliss and Wain, were re-engaged 1 August 1901 for the Ashby extension.

In August 1909 a bridge gang under foreman Coyle spent three weeks laying 85-pound rail in place of 60, with new ties — a deck 40 tons heavier — and jacked both shoes and reseated them to the same two-inch mark, the south nest seated with the north-pier thermometer at 86°F at four o'clock. On 4 April 1911 the Ninestone roundhouse and office burned with the road's construction records; "nothing left about that bridge but my book," Adela said. In March 1954 the bridge was rehabilitated for diesels: roller nest and pedestal out, rocker bearings with four inches of travel each way in, and the old pedestal thrown over the abutment into the creek.

### A2. The night book and what was heard from the bridge

The sound was one deep report — not a roll, a rattle or a bell — felt in the floor before it was heard, always between midnight and four, never twice in a night, and only when a soft day collapsed into hard cold: the "killing nights." At the depot it came up the rails first: the telegraph sounder rattled in its bracket, then the floor moved, then the noise. It was heard at Cadder Falls, a mile below the bridge, and never at Ninestone.

It was first heard on 17 January 1912 (Kettle Bench that day: high 34, low −2, clear and still). Judd Rennick (born 1888, Emil's son), night operator at the Falls depot from 1909 and station agent from 1915 until June 1954, ruled up a book next morning with five columns: date, day's high, night's low, whether she spoke, the hour. He kept it 43 winters, 1911–12 through 1953–54, and wrote no opinions in it. From the pages he extracted two conditions that never failed: the night's low fell below 6°F, and the day's high had been at least 30 degrees warmer than that low. His summary at the back: 61 nights she spoke; 34 winters with at least one, nine with none; five nights met both conditions and gave nothing — 66 qualifying nights. His one guess: she never spoke twice in one freeze; every silent qualifying night sat in a cold spell after a speaking night with no daytime thaw above 40°F between. The fullest winter was 1917–18, five nights. Against the belief that she rang for deaths he counted: 190 deaths at Cadder Falls in those years, seven within a week of a speaking night.

On 14 January 1936, in Dorsey Tice's store about four in the afternoon, Judd did the sum aloud: 41 at noon, 38 at four, falling four degrees an hour since one — under six by about midnight, a fall of better than 30, no thaw since the second week of December. Dorsey bet five dollars she would not speak. The low was −3; she spoke at 1:10 a.m.; Dorsey paid next day and told the story on himself for the rest of his life.

The last night heard was 9 February 1954. The bearings were replaced that March; three qualifying nights in the next two winters were silent, and on 2 March 1956 Judd wrote his last line: "I do not know now what it was I was listening to."

What the valley believed: that No. 9 lay in the gorge with her crew and the sound was her working the grade. The belief came from the 6 March 1898 runaway at the north approach of the brand-new bridge — a wreck with no dead, an engine rerailed in nine days and sold in 1901 — fused with a sound that did not begin until fourteen years later and back-dated to "the winter after the wreck." Some said she rang for deaths, others for hard winters. When the 1954 gang silenced her, the Falls said the railroad had spoiled it.

### A3. The locomotive's second life

Cadder Valley No. 9 was an American-type 4-4-0 from the Rowan Locomotive Works, built 1889, works number 1142, bought new for $8,600 — the best engine the road owned. On 6 March 1898 she ran away down the quarry grade with a work train and went over on her side at the north approach of the new viaduct; nobody was killed, two men were hurt, and she was rerailed in nine days and returned to work.

On 19 June 1901 the company sold her to the Tolliver Lumber Company of Bly County, two hundred miles north, for $2,150, "delivered on her own wheels" — chief clerk Pike wrote the entry himself. Silas Tolliver (1849–1922), who ran the Sixmile tram (eventually nineteen miles of 30-pound rail up three drainages), wanted a road engine that could bring loads down the main stem faster than a man could walk; his daughter Pearl Tolliver Nace (born 1881, the company's bookkeeper for 39 years from 1899) found the advertisement and wrote the $8,600 figure in the margin. The engine came north under her own steam in eleven days over four railroads with a Pell County crew, who went home as passengers. The shop painted out the 9 and painted on a 4.

Her first season on the steep grades was a failure — "a racehorse to pull a stump" — then she was kept on the main stem and worked 27 years: boiler retubed 1909, new tires 1913, a cracked frame welded in 1919 and again in 1922, a full shopping in 1924. In September 1928 a second-hand eight-coupled engine displaced her; she sat under a tarpaulin through the winter and was cut up at Sixmile in May 1929 — "No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile this month. Scrap to Ravel Brothers, $470." Ravel took the tires and brass separately; her boiler shell served as a wash-water tank in the mill until the mill came down.

The cut ran out in 1936; in 1938 Pearl drove the stock book, shop book, timber ledgers and eleven boxes of correspondence to the Bly County Historical Rooms, where they drew no inquiry for 24 years — until Ruth Frayne wrote in 1962 and received photostats of both entries, and Corwin Athey wrote in 1966 and corrected his pamphlet at press. Alma Nace's 1970 memoir gives the price as $2,510 and the year as 1902; the ledger, quoted twice verbatim, says $2,150 and 1901, and her own "27 years" fits 1901–1928.

### A4. The 1962 survey and what it established

In September 1962 the state's flood-control cut took Sallow Creek out of its bed for eleven weeks, and the Pell County highway department sent Ruth Frayne (born 1934, a civil engineer three years in the department by 1963) to inspect the piers. She walked more than 800 feet of dry bed above and below the viaduct and found nothing of iron larger than a spike: no locomotive, no frame, no wheel. She photographed it. Forty feet below the south abutment lay the cast pedestal the 1954 gang had thrown over — the old south shoe of the center span — with the setting chisel mark on the roller seat at two inches, measured three times with a steel tape. The 1954 rocker bearings above measured four inches of travel each way.

Her father Wendell handed her his mother's black oilcloth field book, on the shelf 58 years: the rule of one inch in 300 feet for 40 degrees, and the page-62 entry of 12 September 1897 — span closed at 66°F, rollers at two inches, Sheet 11 calling for three, Tice's refusal to recast. Judd Rennick let her read the night book at his table and take it away. Lettie Wexler lent the Kettle Bench weather record in four cardboard boxes. She wrote to the Bly County Historical Rooms and got photostats: No. 9 sold 19 June 1901, cut up May 1929.

The arithmetic: as drawn, cold travel ran out at −54°F; as built, at −14°F, which the valley never reached (the coldest of 1897–1909 was −9 on 4 February 1899, and seven nights below 6°F in those years — four after 30-degree falls — passed silent); after the August 1909 reseat at 86°F, at +6°F. The Wexler record agreed with the night book on all 61 nights, showed the two winters after the re-decking never below +9 (coldest 3 February 1911), and put the first sub-6 night after 1909 on 17 January 1912 — the night the book begins.

Her report closed: "There is no locomotive in this creek and there never was one. What the valley heard for forty-three winters was a bridge letting go of itself, one inch late." The Sentinel ran the story on 14 October 1962; she read the talk to the Historical Society in 1963 and gave it the field book and the pedestal; the Wexler boxes came in 1967, the night book from Judd's estate in 1969. H. L. Quarles's 1971 memorandum confirmed the bearing arrangement, the bind-and-release mechanism, the rail transmission and the incidental cessation for the county file, while using a 1-inch-in-50-degrees coefficient he admitted he could not reconcile with the record.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Adela was Emil Rennick's younger sister. Judd (Emil's son) says "my father had a younger sister, Adela"; Wendell (Adela's son) says "my mother, Adela Rennick, was his younger sister"; the curator and Athey concur. Four sources, two of them the children of the siblings themselves.

**B2.** Judd was Adela's nephew — the son of her brother Emil. Established by Judd's own account of his parentage (father Emil; Emil's sister Adela), by Wendell ("Emil's son, which made him my mother's nephew"), and by the curator ("Judd Rennick's father, Emil, and Adela ... were brother and sister"). What points elsewhere: Ruth Frayne calls Judd "my father's uncle" and Vesta Cobb calls Ruth his "grand-niece," which would make Judd Adela's brother; and the curator's wrappers cross-attribute the two books (field book labelled "J. Rennick," night book "A. Rennick"). Resolution: Wendell explains the first — Ruth "used to call him uncle and he never corrected her," a courtesy title from a girl to a cousin sixteen years older than her own father; the wrappers are contradicted by the initials inside (the field book is signed "A.R."; the night book is in the agent's hand) and the curator himself flags them for correction. Judd's first-hand statement of who his father was outranks a courtesy usage.

**B3.** Ruth Frayne was Judd's first cousin once removed. Derivation: Adela → Wendell (son) → Ruth (daughter), so Ruth is Adela's granddaughter. Emil → Judd (son). Adela and Emil were siblings, so their children Wendell and Judd were first cousins. Ruth, being the child of Judd's first cousin, is one generation down from that cousinship: first cousin once removed. Wendell states this explicitly; Ruth's "uncle" and Vesta's "grand-niece" are the courtesy usage, not the kinship.

**B4.** Warren Tice — resident engineer on the viaduct, later chief engineer, retired spring 1931, died 1937. Dorsey says so himself ("My father ... Warren Tice"), Wendell confirms ("His son Dorsey kept the store"); Vesta and Athey's "nephew" is town memory and loses to the son's own word. Why it matters to January 1936: the man who bet five dollars that the bridge would stay silent was the son of the engineer who had overruled Adela Rennick and accepted the two-inch setting that made it speak — and he bet against Adela's nephew, whose book held the rule her setting had created. Dorsey insists his father "had no hand in that bridge" and that the retirement-dinner remark ("we set that nest a mark shy") was never said; his skepticism had a family motive, and his losing the bet was, without his knowing it, the thermometer proving that the sound was a property of his father's bridge.

**B5.** Chain of custody, all converging on Ruth Frayne in autumn 1962:
- *Field book*: kept by Adela Rennick from her railroad years (1895–1901) on the shelf over her chair until her death in 1959 → her son Wendell Frayne (the only child; "the family's filing cabinet") → handed by Wendell to his daughter Ruth in September 1962 when she said she was going to the creek. (Afterward: given by Wendell to the Historical Society, 1963.)
- *Night book*: ruled up by Judd Rennick at the Cadder Falls depot in January 1912, kept by him through 2 March 1956, in his own house after retirement → lent personally by Judd to Ruth in 1962 (she read it through at his table and he let her take it) → returned to him → to the Society from his estate, 1969.
- *Weather record*: begun by Peter Wexler at Kettle Bench (1884 by Lettie's account; see E1) → kept by his daughter Lettie from 1926 → lent by Lettie to Ruth in four cardboard boxes in fall 1962, no receipt asked → all four boxes returned → to the Society, 1967, the year Lettie died.
- *Bly County ledgers*: Tolliver Lumber Company stock book and shop book kept by Pearl Tolliver Nace (1899–1938) → deposited by Pearl in 1938 at the Bly County Historical Rooms → first inquiry in 24 years was Ruth's letter in 1962 → the Rooms sent photostats of the 19 June 1901 and May 1929 entries, which she left with her report (the originals never left Bly County).
- (Plus the pedestal, which she recovered herself from the creek bed.)

## Section C — Math

**C1.** Rule used: one inch in 300 feet per 40°F — Adela Rennick's field book, front matter, quoted verbatim by Frayne and by the curator with the book on his table. Closing temperature: 66°F (field book p. 62; Pike agrees). Travel per the drawing: three inches (field book p. 62). I reject Pike's and Quarles's 1-in-50 / four inches — see E1 — because those values put the post-1909 bind at −14°F, colder than the valley's record low, and cannot produce the 61 nights logged at or below +6°F; Quarles himself admits the irreconcilability.
- (a) 3 in × 40°/in = 120° of cooling; 66 − 120 = **−54°F**.
- (b) 2 in × 40°/in = 80°; 66 − 80 = **−14°F**.
- (c) Reseated at the same two-inch mark with the steel at 86°F: 86 − 80 = **+6°F**.
- (d) (+6) − (−14) = **20°F** of margin lost — exactly the 86 − 66 = 20° difference between the seating temperatures. (Measured against the drawing as specified, the total shortfall was 60°, of which 40° was the 1897 error and 20° the 1909 work.)
- For transparency, under Pike/Quarles values (1 in/50°, 4 in): (a) 66 − 200 = −134°F; (b) 66 − 100 = −34°F; (c) 86 − 100 = −14°F; (d) still 20°.

**C2.** (a) Threshold is "below six." 38 − 6 = 32° to fall; 32 ÷ 4°/h = 8 h; 4 p.m. + 8 h = **about midnight**. (Judd's own sum at the counter; the bridge spoke at 1:10 a.m., after the crossing.)
(b) Day's high 41 (noon); night's low −3. 41 − (−3) = **44°** of fall; 44 ≥ 30, so the second condition **was satisfied** (and Judd noted a third: no thaw above 40 since the second week of December, so the bearing was "rested").

**C3.** Counts from the night book's own summary page (quoted verbatim by the curator; recited by Judd): 61 nights heard, 34 winters with at least one, 9 with none, 5 qualifying-but-silent, 66 qualifying.
- (a) 61 ÷ 43 = 1.419 → **1.4** nights per logged winter.
- (b) 61 + 5 = **66** nights met both conditions.
- (c) 61 ÷ 34 = 1.794 → **1.8** nights per speaking winter.
- Winters: 43, running from the winter of 1911–12 through the winter of 1953–54 (1954 − 1911 = 43). First fixed by the first entry, 17 January 1912 (Wexler daybook line, Frayne, Athey; Judd's own "the first time she spoke" — his "1907" and "winter of 1913" are inconsistent with each other and with the book). Last fixed by the last night heard, 9 February 1954, and the March 1954 bearing change; the summary itself says forty-three winters.
- Excluded: (i) the three nights of 1954–55 and 1955–56 that met both conditions and were silent — recorded in the closing note of 2 March 1956 but after the summary and outside the 43 winters (including them gives 69 qualifying nights over 45 winters, 61 ÷ 45 = 1.4); (ii) the four nights of 1897–1909 that met both conditions with no sound — they are in the Wexler record, not the night book; (iii) Lettie Wexler's and Quarles's "eight silent, 69 total" and Dorsey/Athey's "57 nights, 30/13 winters" — rejected in favor of the book's own page (E1).

**C4.** (a) Built 1889, cut up May 1929: 1929 − 1889 = **40 years** (39–40; the sources give only the build year).
(b) $2,150 ÷ $8,600 = 0.25 = **25%** of original cost (ledger entry, quoted verbatim by Pike and the curator; Alma's $2,510 is a transposition, see E1).
(c) Sold 19 June 1901, set out behind the shop September 1928: **27 years** working — Alma's own figure. (To the cut-up in May 1929 it is 27 years 11 months, i.e. 28 if you count to scrapping.)

**C5.** (a) 120 + 300 + 120 = **540 feet** (Pike, Quarles; the Sentinel's and Athey's "six hundred" fails the addition).
(b) Quarry milepost 31 to viaduct milepost 22 = 9 miles (Judd: "nine miles off the quarry"; Quarles's "eight" contradicts the mileposts he himself cites). 9 × 5,280 = 47,520 ft; × 0.022 = 1,045.44 → **≈1,045 feet**.
(c) Record high 101°F (July 1936; all sources); record low −11°F (January 1918; the keeper, Lettie Wexler, and the depositor's index sheet — Frayne's and the Sentinel's −13 rejected, E1). Range = 101 − (−11) = 112°F. At 1 in per 40°: 112 ÷ 40 = **2.8 inches**. (With −13: 2.85 in; under the 1-in-50 rule: 2.2 in.)

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** Necessary, not sufficient. Necessity: on all 61 heard nights in 43 winters the low was below 6°F and stood at the bottom of a fall of at least 30° — "not an exception in the book," and Judd went through it looking for one; the Wexler thermometer three miles off, kept by people who never wrote down a sound, confirms every one of the 61 (Frayne) and a dozen taken at random "to the degree" (Lettie). No sound was ever logged above the threshold. Not sufficient: five nights inside the book met both conditions and were silent (66 qualifying, 61 heard); three more in 1954–56 and four in 1897–1909 met both and were silent. The silent five all sit in cold spells after a speaking night with no thaw above 40° between, so a third condition (a re-warming since the last release) plus the bearing's 1909–1954 setting are needed to complete sufficiency. Quarles states the same: necessary conditions, not sufficient ones.

**D2.** Independent reasons the sound cannot have been No. 9:
1. *The company ledger and the 1898 newspaper (Pike; Adela's p. 71 entry; Emil via Wendell).* Nobody died on 6 March 1898; the engine lay on her side at the north end in daylight, was rerailed in nine days, and on 19 June 1901 was sold and "delivered on her own wheels" — she steamed away three years after she was supposed to be under the water.
2. *The Bly County shop book and Alma Nace's memoir.* A different company's records, two hundred miles north, deposited in 1938 and unread until 1962: she worked 27 years at Sixmile as No. 4, was cut up in May 1929, scrap $470, boiler shell a wash-water tank. Nace knew nothing of the Pell County story and disclaimed it.
3. *The night book and the Wexler record (Judd, Lettie, Frayne).* The sound began 17 January 1912 — fourteen years after the wreck. Seven nights below 6°F between 1897 and 1909 passed in silence and nobody at the Falls ever claimed otherwise. A ghost born of the wreck would have started in 1898.
4. *The physics of the sound as logged (Judd, Dorsey, Quarles).* It came up the rails before the floor before the air; once a night, never twice; only after a 30° fall, never in a slow cold; predictable from a thermometer in the afternoon (the wager). That is the signature of steel releasing strain, not of an engine — and the sound was never heard at Ninestone, 22 miles off, only a mile down the line.
5. *The 1962 survey (Frayne, Sentinel).* No locomotive, nothing bigger than a spike, in 800 feet of dry bed; and the sound stopped for good in the very month (March 1954) the bearings alone were replaced, the 1909 deck left in place and the creek untouched.
Independence: each reason rests on a different record system kept by different people who never compared notes — the CVR office at Ninestone, the Tolliver books in Bly County, the depot at the Falls and a milk-house thermometer at Kettle Bench, and the physical inspection of the bed. Any one could be wrong without disturbing the others. Reasons 1–4 hold with no 1962 survey at all: the 1901 ledger entry, the 1929 shop book, the 1912 first entry, and the rails-first character of the sound all predate it. Reason 1 alone is sufficient.

**D3.** The sources cannot establish that the objection had anything to do with her leaving, and the direct evidence leans against it. What the documents show: the letter of 30 April 1901 reduced "the engineering party entire" on 15 May, and "Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card" — a man nursing a four-year grudge does not add a recommendation. The slip of 20 July 1901 re-engaging Corliss (rodman) and Wain (chainman) for the Ashby extension shows two field men taken back for field work; she was the road's only computer, and it never had another, so her non-re-engagement is what a party reduction looks like, not a punishment. Against this: Pike says "Tice never forgave that entry, and she was gone inside four years," but admits he has nothing in writing and is reading "the temperature of a room" as a boy; and she kept the re-engagement slip folded inside the letter, which the curator notes without explaining. That is suggestive, not probative. Answer: no link is shown; the whole-party layoff with a recommendation is the best-evidenced account, and Pike's is an admitted inference. Cannot be determined beyond that.

## Section E — Contradictions

Format: claims — who — resolution — method.

1. **Viaduct length.** 540 ft (Pike, Quarles: 120 + 300 + 120) vs "six hundred feet" (Sentinel, Athey). → 540. Arithmetic on the spans.
2. **Movement rule.** 1 in per 40° (Adela's field book, via Frayne and curator) vs 1 in per 50° (Pike, Quarles). → 40°. Contemporaneous document; empirical fit (40° gives a +6°F bind matching 61 nights never above 6); Quarles concedes his figure "is not readily reconciled with the local record."
3. **Sheet 11 travel.** Three inches (Adela's p. 62 entry) vs four inches (Pike; Quarles "four inches each way"). → Three. Document written on closing day by the computer working from the sheet; Quarles's "each way" is the language of the 1954 rocker spec, not a roller nest with a setting mark; Pike's father was a clerk, not an engineer, and the drawings burned in 1911 by Pike's own account. This is my least certain resolution, because Quarles claims to hold the drawings; I note the residual doubt.
4. **Re-decking year.** 1908 (Pike) vs August 1909 (Judd's written slate note, Frayne, Quarles, curator) vs "the summer of 1911" (Judd, spoken). → August 1909. The written note outranks both memories; Judd contradicts his own hand; Lettie's twelve-year window runs "through to the summer of 1909."
5. **Bearing replacement.** Spring 1953 (Judd) vs March 1954 (Pike, curator, Quarles, Sentinel, Athey, Dorsey, Frayne). → March 1954. The book's last heard night is 9 February 1954 (curator) — it cannot post-date the rebuild; document and arithmetic.
6. **Onset of the sound.** Winter 1897–98, "on the heels of the wreck" (Vesta; Sentinel "from the winter after the wreck") vs 17 January 1912 (Wexler daybook line, Frayne, Athey, the book). → 1912. Document (daybook) plus the seven silent sub-6 nights of 1897–1909; Vesta (born 1920) is repeating her grandmother.
7. **Duration.** "Seventy-odd years" (Vesta) vs 43 winters, 1912–1954. → 43 winters. The book.
8. **When Judd went on nights.** 1907 (Judd) vs 1909 (Dorsey, Wendell). → 1909, moderately. Two independent sources vs one self-report from a narrator who also mis-years the first sound (1913), the gang (1911) and the rockers (1953); his "started the book that same winter" is false whichever year he began.
9. **Count of nights and winters.** 61 nights / 34 speaking winters / 9 silent (Judd; the summary page via curator; Frayne; Lettie) vs 57 / 30 / 13 (Dorsey, Athey). → 61/34/9. Document. Both splits sum to 43, so arithmetic cannot break it; the page can.
10. **Silent qualifying nights.** 5 (66 total) (Judd; summary page) vs 8 (69 total) (Lettie, Quarles). → 5/66. Document; Lettie read the book for one hour three years earlier; Quarles has it "at second hand."
11. **Deaths within a week.** 7 of 190 (Judd; Athey) vs "better than a third" (Dorsey, "he told me once"). → 7 of 190. Judd's count in the back of the book; Judd also says he never gave the numbers at the store — that side-conflict is immaterial and unsettled.
12. **Wager year.** January 1937 (Judd) vs 14 January 1936 (Dorsey "without hesitation"; Sentinel; Athey). → 1936. Internal consistency: Athey's "written things down for twenty-four years before that evening" (1912 + 24 = 1936) and Dorsey's "never brought it up again in twenty-five years" (1936 + 25 = 1961, the year of his tape); Judd's years are unreliable (item 8). The book entry would settle it and is not quoted.
13. **Wager scene.** In the store, Dorsey hearing it in bed upstairs (Judd, Dorsey) vs in the depot, both sitting up (Sentinel, Athey). → Store. Both participants agree, first-hand. Payment "next morning before I opened" (Dorsey) vs "in the store next day in front of everybody" (Judd): cannot be settled, immaterial.
14. **Dorsey's relation to Warren Tice.** Son (Dorsey, Wendell) vs nephew (Vesta, Athey). → Son. A man's own statement of his father, plus the deck-at-six story.
15. **Warren Tice during construction.** "In the Ninestone office the whole time" (Dorsey) vs resident engineer on the work (Pike, Wendell, Athey; the "W.T." Adela reported to on closing day) and Dorsey's own "he had closed the span himself in ninety-seven." → On site as resident engineer. Dorsey's internal inconsistency; his motive is stated ("the last thing he would have wanted his name hung on").
16. **Who set the nest.** Anders Berg (Dorsey) vs Cass Nolan (Athey). → **Cannot be determined from the sources.** One memory each, no document, and Athey's compilation may or may not derive from Dorsey.
17. **Tice's retirement-dinner remark.** "We set that nest a mark shy and I have listened to it ever since" (Emil → Adela → Wendell, two removes) vs "he said nothing of the kind" (Dorsey, present at the dinner, interested). → **Cannot be determined.** Hearsay against an interested denial; the pedestal shows the nest *was* a mark shy whether or not he said so.
18. **Adela's birth year.** 1874 (Frayne, Athey) vs 1876 (Wendell, curator). → 1874. Arithmetic: died 1959 at 85 (Wendell's and Frayne's own figure) → 1874; the curator took his date from Wendell's slips and conversation.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The sound was the south bearing of the 300-foot center span binding as the steel contracted and then releasing all at once.

*Setup.* The truss is pinned at the north end; the south end rides a roller nest in a cast pedestal with a chisel setting mark, and the steel moves about one inch per 40°F. On 12 September 1897, at 66°F, the rollers were set at the two-inch mark although Sheet 11 called for three; Tice would not recast the pedestal before the fall haul. As set, the nest ran out of cold travel at 66 − 80 = −14°F. The valley's record low is −11, and the coldest night of 1897–1909 was −9 (4 February 1899), so the error was invisible: seven nights below 6°F in those twelve years, four after 30-degree falls, passed in silence. In August 1909 a gang re-laid the deck (85-pound rail, 40 tons heavier) and reseated both shoes to the same two-inch mark with the steel at 86°F. That moved the bind point to 86 − 80 = **+6°F** — a temperature the valley reaches most winters.

*Character of the sound.* When the span cools past +6 the shoe hits the end of its seat and binds; contraction continues and strain is stored in the truss members and pins; the shoe lets go entirely and the span drops into position in one movement. Stored energy discharged at one stroke gives a single deep, short report — no roll, no repeat — transmitted far better along continuous rail than through air, hence the sounder rattling before the floor before the ear, hence felt in the soles before heard, hence registered a mile down the line at the Falls and never 22 miles off at Ninestone.

*The threshold and why that number.* Two inches of travel at 40° per inch is 80°; 86 − 80 = 6. The night book's 61 nights, all below 6 and never above, are that arithmetic observed.

*The second condition.* A slow cold lets the bearing creep — friction overcome repeatedly in small increments at low stored strain, nothing accumulates. A fall of 30° or more within a day carries the shoe past its freedom faster than it can relieve itself; the strain is stored and released whole. Three weeks at zero: nothing. A soft day and the bottom out of it: the bridge speaks.

*Silent qualifying nights.* After a release the shoe stands at the far end of its travel with nothing left to give; only a daytime thaw above about 40°F re-warms the span and draws it back. Every one of the five silent qualifying nights in the book sits inside a cold spell after a speaking night with no such thaw between (Judd's "she would not speak twice inside one freeze"). The conditions were necessary, not sufficient.

*Onset.* Nothing before August 1909 because −14°F never came. Nothing in 1909–10 or 1910–11 because those winters never went below +9. The first night under 6 after the reseat was 17 January 1912 (high 34, low −2): the first entry in the book.

*Cessation.* In March 1954 the roller nest and pedestal were replaced by rocker bearings with four inches of travel each way — 160°F of movement at 1 in/40°, against a valley range of 112°F — and nothing else was touched; the 1909 deck still stands. Last heard 9 February 1954; three qualifying nights in the next two winters silent; never since. The cure was incidental to a diesel rehabilitation and unnoticed by the men who effected it.

*Competing explanations for the onset, and why they fail.* (1) The valley's: the ghost of No. 9 and her crew, beginning "the winter after the wreck" of March 1898. Defeated by the record of the wreck (nobody killed, engine rerailed in nine days — Sentinel of 8 March 1898, Adela p. 71), by the ledger of 19 June 1901 (sold, delivered on her own wheels), by the Tolliver shop book of May 1929 (cut up at Sixmile), by the Wexler daybook (seven silent sub-6 nights 1897–1909, first sound 1912, fourteen years after the wreck), and by the dry creek of 1962. (2) The office's: Pike's and Quarles's forty tons — the heavier 1909 deck overloaded the rollers and made them bind. This correctly ties the onset to the 1909 work but fails as the mechanism: added load predicts no particular temperature, yet the book shows a threshold at exactly +6°F, never above, which only the reseat arithmetic produces; Quarles's own coefficient with the load theory yields a −14°F bind he cannot reconcile; and after March 1954 the same 40-ton deck sat on the same span and the sound stopped, because the only thing changed was the travel. The load may have stiffened the nest's friction and helped it bind rather than creep, but the number came from the mark.

*Physical evidence of 1962.* The cast south pedestal, forty feet below the abutment where the 1954 gang threw it, with the chisel setting mark on the roller seat at two inches — the one surviving witness to the 1897 setting — and no locomotive in 800 feet of dry bed.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** In 1897 the Cadder Valley Railroad seated the 300-foot span of its Sallow Creek viaduct one inch short of its drawing; the party's computer, Adela Rennick, objected, was overruled, and wrote it down. The error was harmless until August 1909, when a re-decking gang reset the bearing to the same mark at 86°F, so that whenever a sudden cold snap took the steel below 6°F the bearing bound and then let go with one deep report. From 1912 to 1954 station agent Judd Rennick logged 61 such nights; the valley blamed a locomotive that had derailed harmlessly in 1898, been sold in 1901 and scrapped in Bly County in 1929. New bearings in 1954 ended it. In 1962 Ruth Frayne, with a dry creek, the pedestal, the field book, the night book, the Wexler weather record and the Bly County ledgers, proved all of it.
