# Narrator Briefs, v2 (SECRET — never show to the system under test)

One self-contained brief per retelling. Each is written for an author who will draft that
retelling **without reading any other brief**. Everything the author needs — facts with
values, errors with their exact wrong wording, documents to quote verbatim, and the
prohibitions — is inside the brief.

**Rules for every retelling.**
- Target length **1,200–1,800 words**. Prose, in the narrator's own voice, not a fact list.
- Quote the documents assigned to you **word for word**. Do not paraphrase them, do not
  correct their spelling, do not add or drop a figure.
- State the facts assigned to you with the exact values given. Do not add facts that are
  not in your brief, and never invent a number, a date, a name, or a relationship.
- Reproduce your assigned errors **exactly as worded here**, in the narrator's own idiom,
  with no hedging and no wink to the reader. The narrator believes them.
- Never say or imply that any narrator is unreliable, that a fact is corrupted, that
  another retelling exists, or that the reader is being tested.
- Do not mention anything in your "must NOT mention" list, even in passing.
- American spelling throughout. No real people or places.

**Shared world vocabulary** (safe for any narrator to use): Pell County · Ninestone (the
county seat) · Cadder Falls (the mill town) · Kettle Bench (a farm three miles up the
valley) · Sallow Creek and its gorge · the Sallow Creek Viaduct · the Cadder Valley
Railroad (CVR) · the Ashby quarry · Bly County, two hundred miles north · the Tolliver
Lumber Company and its Sixmile tram.

## r01 — Vesta Cobb, a mill hand's daughter

**Voice.** Seventy-four years old, born 1920 at Cadder Falls, recorded on tape in 1974 by
somebody from the county. Talks in long spoken paragraphs, doubles back, corrects herself
about small things and never about large ones. No dates unless she is sure of them, and
she is sure of the wrong ones. Warm, superstitious in a matter-of-fact way, and completely
uninterested in engineering.

**Vantage point.** She grew up hearing it. Her father worked in the mill and her mother's
people had been at the Falls since the line was built. She heard the sound herself as a
child — she gives one vivid first-hand account of a night in the 1930s when it woke the
whole street. She never read a document in her life and would not know what Sheet 11 was.

**What she knows (state these as given).**
- The sound: one deep report, never a rattle or a roll, "like a door shut in a cellar three
  towns off"; always in the small hours, between midnight and about four; you felt it in
  the floorboards before you heard it. (F087)
- It only ever came on what the Falls called the killing nights — the hard, clear, sudden
  cold, when the day had been mild and the night went to pieces. (F088, in folk form)
- Judd Rennick, the station agent, wrote every one of them down in a ruled book, and
  everybody knew he did, and nobody at the store took it seriously. (F027)
- The store at the Falls was Dorsey Tice's, and Dorsey once lost money to Judd Rennick over
  it, which the town enjoyed for years. (F010, F049 — she does not know the year)
- It stopped when the railroad "put new iron under that bridge" and everyone said the
  railroad had spoiled it. (F091, in folk form)
- Judd Rennick lived on into his eighties and was still in town when the young Frayne woman
  came round asking questions. (F004, F058)

**What she gets wrong (X01, X02, X03 — reproduce with these wordings).**
1. **The lore, asserted flatly and never withdrawn.** Number Nine and her crew went into
   the gorge in the bad winter when the bridge was new, and they are down there yet, and it
   is her working the grade that the valley hears. Say it as fact. Say the crew died. She
   must never hedge it, never attribute it to "people said," and never mention any sale of
   the engine — she has never heard of one.
2. **"It started the winter the bridge was new."** She places the first hearing in the
   first winter after the viaduct opened — 1897 into 1898 — and connects it directly to the
   wreck. She will say "seventy-odd years" of it if pressed.
3. **"Dorsey Tice was old Warren Tice's nephew."** Use the word *nephew*. She may add that
   the Tices were "railroad people from Ninestone."

**Allowed rhetoric (not scored, not an error).** "A hundred nights if there was one." She
may exaggerate the frequency freely as long as she never states a counted total.

**Must NOT mention.** Any specification, any measurement in inches, the roller nest, any
thermometer reading, any figure from the night book, the Tolliver Lumber Company, Bly
County, Pearl Nace, the pedestal recovered in 1962, the weather record, Adela Rennick by
name (she may say "the agent's aunt worked for the railroad once" at most), or any dollar
amount except the wager money if she recalls it as "five dollars."

**Documents.** None. She quotes nothing.

## r02 — Harlan Pike, son of the CVR's chief clerk

**Voice.** A memoir written in 1958 by a retired man in his seventies, precise and a little
proud, fond of the exact figure and the file reference. He writes the way a clerk's son
writes: mileposts, grades, dates, dollars. Dry humor about the company. He is defending a
world that no longer exists.

**Vantage point.** His father was chief clerk of the Cadder Valley Railroad at Ninestone
from 1893 to 1919 and brought the office home with him. Harlan grew up among stock books
and payrolls, and he saw much of what he describes as a boy or heard it across the supper
table. He never worked on the bridge and never kept a night book; his authority is
documentary and second-hand, and he knows it.

**What he knows (state these as given).**
- The CVR: thirty-one miles. Ninestone at milepost 0, the Sallow Creek Viaduct at milepost
  22, Cadder Falls at milepost 23, the Ashby quarry at milepost 31. (F021)
- The grade from the quarry down to the viaduct: **two and two-tenths percent, nine miles
  of it**. (F079)
- The viaduct: three spans — 120 feet, then a pin-connected truss of **300 feet**, then 120
  feet: **five hundred and forty feet** in all, **ninety-seven feet** above the creek. Begun
  April 1896; the line opened to Cadder Falls **14 November 1897**. (F022, F035, F037, F078)
- The center span's north end is pinned solid; the south end rests on a nest of rollers in a
  cast pedestal, so the steel can lengthen and shorten. (F023)
- The rule the engineers used for that span: **one inch in three hundred feet for forty
  degrees**. (F060)
- The span was closed on **12 September 1897** with the thermometer at **sixty-six
  degrees**. (F036, F061)
- Locomotive No. 9: a 4-4-0 from the **Rowan Locomotive Works, 1889, works number 1142**,
  bought new for **eight thousand six hundred dollars** — the best machine the company
  owned. (F028, F081)
- The runaway of **6 March 1898**: she came down off the quarry grade without brakes and
  went off the iron at the **north approach**, and **nobody was killed**. Two of the gang
  hurt. Nine days to rerail her. (F038, F093)
- She was sold on **19 June 1901** to a lumber company in Bly County for **two thousand one
  hundred and fifty dollars**. (F042)
- **Adela Rennick** was the engineering party's computer — "she did the arithmetic, and she
  did it better than the party did." She kept her own duplicate book. Her time ended when
  the party was reduced. (F002, F026)
- **Warren Tice** was the resident engineer on the viaduct and later the company's chief
  engineer at Ninestone. (F009)
- The **Ninestone roundhouse and office fire of 4 April 1911** destroyed the company's
  construction records and erection time books; after it, nothing the CVR had written about
  building the viaduct survived. (F034)
- The **August re-decking**: eighty-five-pound steel in place of sixty, new ties, the shoes
  jacked and re-set, the deck forty tons heavier. (F045 — but see his error 2 for the year)
- The **March 1954** rehabilitation for diesel power, which put in new bearings; the noise
  stopped after it and never came back. (F052, F091)

**What he gets wrong (X04, X05 — reproduce with these wordings).**
1. **"Sheet 11 called for four inches of travel."** He states the specification as **four
   inches** and is confident about it. He may add that "four inches was generous for a
   three-hundred-foot span." He must never quote the field book on this point.
2. **"The re-decking was in 1908."** He gives the year of the re-decking as **1908**
   throughout. Everything else about the re-decking he gives correctly.

**Abstention poles he carries (⌀X06, ⌀X07 — reproduce with these wordings, as his own
opinion, citing nothing).**
3. **On Adela Rennick's leaving:** "Tice never forgave that entry, and she was gone inside
   four years." He offers no letter, no minute, no witness — this is a conviction he formed
   from the atmosphere of the office. He must **not** quote the reduction-of-force letter,
   and must **not** claim to have seen anything in writing about it.
4. **On the sound at Ninestone:** "We never heard it at Ninestone. My father would have
   written it down." Stated flatly as a family fact.

**Must NOT mention.** The night book's totals (61, 43, 34, 9, 5, 66), the +6-degree
threshold, the thirty-degree fall, the 86-degree reading of 1909, the wager, the 1962
survey, Ruth Frayne, the Wexler weather record, the Tolliver shop book, the scrapping of
the engine, the pedestal recovered from the creek, or the contents of Adela's field book
beyond the fact that she kept one.

**Documents he quotes verbatim.**

> *No lives were lost. Two of the gang are hurt, and the engine is on her side at the north
> end of the new viaduct, where she will be got up within the fortnight.*
> — *Ninestone Sentinel*, 8 March 1898

> *Locomotive No. 9, 4-4-0, Rowan Works 1889, works no. 1142, sold this day to the Tolliver
> Lumber Company, Bly County, for two thousand one hundred and fifty dollars ($2,150.00),
> delivered on her own wheels.*
> — CVR stock book, 19 June 1901

## r03 — Judd Rennick, station agent (retired), interviewed 1965

**Voice.** Seventy-seven, interviewed at his kitchen table by someone from the historical
society; the transcript keeps his speech. Short declarative sentences. He counts before he
concludes and says so. He is not mystical and does not pretend to know what the sound was.
He is proud of exactly one thing: that he wrote it all down without an opinion in it.

**Vantage point.** He is the man who kept the night book, and everything in Story Two is
first-hand. **Critically: he is accurate about every number he ever counted and wrong
about every year he names.** He does not hedge his dates; he states them with the same
flat confidence as his counts.

**What he knows (state these as given — all correct).**
- Born 1888; his father was Emil Rennick the carpenter, who built the falsework for the
  viaduct; Emil's younger sister Adela worked for the same railroad before Judd was born
  and never spoke about it. (F001, F002, F003, F004)
- He went on nights at the Cadder Falls depot, and was made agent, and stayed thirty-nine
  years in all. (F046 — but see error 5 for the years)
- **The bridge gang.** Three weeks one summer: new eighty-five-pound steel where sixty had
  been, new ties, **both shoes jacked and set again to the same mark**, and the thermometer
  on the north pier reading **eighty-six degrees at four o'clock** when they seated the
  south nest. Mr. Coyle told him the deck was **forty tons heavier**. He copied this off
  the depot slate later, because at the time it did not matter. (F045)
- **The sound.** One deep report; a shiver that came up the rails into the floor and rattled
  the sounder on the table before the sound arrived; always between midnight and four.
  (F087)
- **The book.** Five ruled columns: the date, the day's high, the night's low, whether she
  spoke, and the hour. **Forty-three winters.** (F027, F068)
- **The conditions.** She never spoke unless the night's low went **below six degrees**, and
  never unless the day before it had been **at least thirty degrees warmer** than the night
  that followed. Both, every time. (F088)
- **The totals.** **Sixty-one** nights she spoke. **Thirty-four** winters she spoke at least
  once; **nine** winters she never spoke at all. **Five** nights answered both conditions
  and gave nothing — **sixty-six** nights that answered them in all. (F069, F070, F071)
- **Why the five were silent.** She would not speak twice inside one freeze. After she had
  spoken it took a thaw — a real one, up above forty in the daytime — before she would do it
  again. (F089)
- **The deaths.** He counted, because the town said she rang for them: **a hundred and
  ninety** deaths at the Falls in the years he kept the book, and **seven** of them inside a
  week after a night she spoke. He wrote the numbers in the back and never mentioned them at
  the store. (F075)
- **The wager.** Dorsey Tice's store, a January afternoon. It had made **forty-one at noon**;
  it was **thirty-eight at four o'clock** and had been falling **four degrees an hour** since
  one. Thirty-eight down to six is thirty-two degrees; at four an hour that is eight hours;
  she would be under six about midnight. There had been no thaw since the second week of
  December. Dorsey bet him **five dollars**. The low was **three below** and she spoke at
  **ten minutes past one**. (F049, F076, F077)
- The rockers: the railroad rebuilt the bridge for diesels and put in **rocker bearings with
  four inches of travel each way**, and threw the old cast pedestals over the abutment into
  the creek. Nobody told him. (F031, F052)
- **The second winter after the rockers** gave **three nights** under six degrees with falls
  of better than thirty, and nothing was heard on any of them. (F054)
- The mileposts and the grade: the quarry at 31, the Falls at 23, the bridge at 22, and
  **two and two-tenths percent** down the nine miles from the quarry. (F021, F079)
- The young Frayne woman came and read the book at his table and he lent it to her. (F019)

**What he gets wrong (X08–X12 — every one is a year, and he states each with total
confidence).**
1. **"The first time she spoke was the winter of 1913."**
2. **"The bridge gang was here the summer of 1911."**
3. **"The rockers went in the spring of 1953."**
4. **"That was January of 1937"** — the wager.
5. **"I went on nights in 1907 and started the book that same winter."**

He may name no other years at all. If the narrative needs a year he does not have, he says
"the year after" or "some winters later."

**Must NOT mention.** Sheet 11, the specification in inches, the two-inch chisel mark, the
1897 closing temperature, the movement rule, the −54/−14/+6 arithmetic, the Wexler weather
record, the Tolliver Lumber Company, the sale or scrapping of the engine, the 1911 fire, or
anything about why the sound started or stopped. He genuinely does not know any of it — the
whole point of his testimony is that he had every observation and none of the explanation.

**Documents he quotes verbatim.**

> *Bridge gang here three weeks, August 1909. New 85-lb. steel, new ties, both shoes jacked
> and set again to the mark. Thermometer on the north pier read 86 at four o'clock when
> they seated the south nest. Mr. Coyle says the deck is forty ton heavier than it was.*
> — the front matter of his own book

*(Note to the author: he reads this note aloud from the book and then says the year was
1911, contradicting the note he has just read. Do not draw attention to it; let it stand.)*

> *Second winter since the rockers went in. Three nights under six degrees, three falls of
> better than thirty, and not a sound out of her either winter. I have kept this book
> forty-three winters and I do not know now what it was I was listening to.*
> — his final entry

## r04 — Ruth Frayne, "What the Creek Gave Back," a talk to the Pell County Historical Society, 1963

**Voice.** A twenty-nine-year-old county engineer reading a paper to a room of amateurs.
Ordered, plain, faintly impatient with romance, and quietly moved once, at the end. She
numbers her findings. She shows her arithmetic on a blackboard and says so.

**Vantage point.** She did the 1962 survey herself. Everything she measured, she measured;
everything she read, she read. She is the corpus's principal explainer and she is right
about all of it except two things.

**What she knows (state these as given).**
- **September 1962**: the state's flood-control cut took Sallow Creek out of its bed for
  eleven weeks and the gorge was dry to the gravel. She is a civil engineer with the Pell
  County highway department, three years in; the inspection was routine. (F007, F058)
- **Finding one: nothing.** No locomotive, no frame, no boiler shell, no driving wheel, no
  firebox stays, over eight hundred feet of dry bed above and below the viaduct. She
  photographed all of it. (F092)
- **Finding two: the pedestal.** Cast iron, **forty feet below the abutment** on the south
  side, thrown there by the 1954 gang. The old south shoe. Cut into the roller seat, a
  chisel mark **at two inches**. She measured it three times with a steel tape. The **new
  rocker bearings** on the bridge measure **four inches** of travel each way. (F032, F025,
  F031)
- **Finding three: her grandmother's field book.** Adela Rennick, born **1874**, died
  **1959** at eighty-five; the book came to Ruth's father Wendell and from him to Ruth in
  1962. In the front matter, the rule. On page sixty-two, the entry of 12 September 1897.
  (F002, F006, F018, F026, F060, F036)
- **Finding four: the night book.** Judd Rennick, seventy-four and living three streets from
  the depot, lent it to her himself. Forty-three winters, sixty-one nights, every one below
  six degrees at the bottom of a fall of thirty or more, five nights that answered and gave
  nothing — and in the front, a gang in **August 1909** jacking both shoes and setting them
  again to the mark at **eighty-six degrees**. (F019, F045, F068, F069, F071, F088)
- **The arithmetic, which she gives step by step.** A three-hundred-foot span moves an inch
  for every forty degrees. Sheet 11 gave the nest **three inches** of cold travel and the
  span was closed at **sixty-six degrees**: three inches is a hundred and twenty degrees, so
  as drawn the bridge would have bound at **fifty-four below zero**. It was built to **two
  inches** — eighty degrees — which put the binding point at **fourteen below**, a
  temperature this valley does not reach, which is why for twelve years a wrong bridge
  behaved like a right one. Then in August 1909 the rollers were brought up to the same
  two-inch mark with the steel at **eighty-six**: eighty degrees down from eighty-six is
  **six above zero**. And six above zero is where the night book says the bridge spoke,
  sixty-one times, and never once above. (F060–F065, F094)
- **The independent witness.** The Wexler family of Kettle Bench kept a volunteer weather
  record — high, low and rain, every day — from **1 January 1894**; Lettie Wexler lent it to
  her in four cardboard boxes. Peter Wexler began it; his daughter Lettie has kept it since
  1926. They never wrote down a sound in their lives. Their weather agrees with Judd
  Rennick's book on **every one** of the sixty-one nights. (F014, F015, F016, F029)
- **The seven nights.** Between the opening of the line in November 1897 and the re-decking
  of August 1909 the Wexler record shows **seven nights below six degrees**, four of them at
  the bottom of falls of thirty degrees or more — and nothing was heard on any of them. The
  coldest reading of those twelve years was **nine below, on 4 February 1899**. The two
  winters after the re-decking never got below **nine above** (the coldest was 3 February
  1911), and the first night under six degrees after August 1909 fell on **17 January 1912**.
  "The silence of those seven nights is not an absence of data. It is the proof." (F085,
  F086, F047, F090)
- **The record high**: **one hundred and one degrees**, July 1936. (F050)
- **The locomotive.** She wrote to the Bly County Historical Rooms, which hold the Tolliver
  Lumber Company's books, deposited there in 1938 by the company's bookkeeper and consulted
  by nobody since, and they sent photostats: a stock book entry of **19 June 1901** and a
  shop book entry of **May 1929**. (F020, F042, F056, F092)
- **Disposition**: the field book to the historical society in 1963, the weather book when
  Lettie Wexler dies, the night book when Judd does, the pedestal to the entrance hall.
  (F059)

**What she gets wrong (X13, X14 — reproduce with these wordings).**
1. **"The lowest reading in the whole Wexler series is thirteen below."** She gives the
   record low as **−13°F** and attributes it to January 1918. (Everything else she says
   about the Wexler record is correct, including the 101-degree high.)
2. **"My father's uncle, Judd Rennick."** She calls Judd her father's *uncle* — once early
   and once late, naturally, in passing, never as a claim she is defending.

**Must NOT mention.** The wager, the death count, the peak winter of 1917/18, the
Sentinel, the 1911 Ninestone fire, the name Warren Tice (she says only "the resident
engineer"), the price the CVR paid for the locomotive, the price the Tollivers paid, or
anything about why her grandmother left the railroad. She must also not name Dorsey Tice
or Anders Berg or Cass Nolan.

**Documents she quotes verbatim.**

> *Rule for the long span: one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees.*
> — field book, front matter

> *Sept. 12, 1897. Span closed 3:40 p.m., thermometer 66°. Rollers set at the two-inch
> mark. Sheet 11 calls for three. Reported to W.T., who answers that the pedestal cannot be
> recast before the fall haul and that two inches is more than the valley will ever ask
> for. Entered here so that it is somewhere. — A.R.*
> — field book, page 62

> *Jan. 17. High 34. Low −2 at daylight. Clear, still.*
> — Wexler weather book, 17 January 1912

> *The south pedestal was recovered from the creek bed forty feet below the abutment, where
> the 1954 gang threw it. The chisel mark on the roller seat is at two inches. Sheet 11
> calls for three. There is no locomotive in this creek and there never was one. What the
> valley heard for forty-three winters was a bridge letting go of itself, one inch late.*
> — the closing paragraph of her own survey report, September 1962

## r05 — Alma Nace, "The Engines My Grandfather Bought," Bly County, 1970

**Voice.** A woman of sixty-three writing an affectionate, digressive family memoir for a
county historical annual. Anecdote first, ledger second. She quotes her mother a great
deal. She has never heard of Sallow Creek and does not care about it.

**Vantage point.** Her grandfather founded the company; her mother kept its books for
thirty-nine years. Alma grew up inside the sound of the engines. Her knowledge of the 1901
purchase is a girl's memory of a ledger page, and it is where she goes wrong. Everything
about the engine's working life and her family is correct.

**What she knows (state these as given).**
- **Silas Tolliver**, born 1849, founded the Tolliver Lumber Company in Bly County — mill,
  horse, and a conviction that the horse was temporary. He died in 1922. (F011)
- The **Sixmile tram**, named for the creek and not its length, eventually nineteen miles of
  thirty-pound rail up three drainages, with switchbacks. (F030)
- Her mother **Pearl Tolliver Nace**, born 1881, kept the company's books from the age of
  eighteen, for thirty-nine years, "because she was better at it than anybody her father
  could hire." (F012)
- In 1901 Silas wanted a road engine — big wheels, long wheelbase, able to bring a made-up
  train of loads down the main stem faster than walking. Pearl found the advertisement: a
  railroad down in **Pell County** selling a twelve-year-old American-type engine. (F042)
- The engine: a **4-4-0** from the **Rowan Works, 1889, works number 1142**. She had cost her
  first owners **eight thousand six hundred dollars** new, a figure Pearl noted in the
  margin, and Silas repeated the comparison for the rest of his life. (F028, F081)
- **"Delivered on her own wheels"** meant she came north under steam — eleven days and four
  railroads — with a Pell County crew who turned around and went home on the cars. (F042)
- The Tolliver shop painted out the 9 and painted on a **4**, because the company numbered
  its engines in the order it bought them. (F028)
- She was a disappointment for one season, on grades she was never built for, and an
  institution afterward: she worked **the main stem from 1901 to 1928 — twenty-seven
  years**. Boiler retube 1909, new tires 1913, a cracked frame welded in 1919 and again in
  1922, a shopping in 1924. (F083, F056)
- In September 1928 the company took delivery of a second-hand eight-coupled engine and
  **No. 4 was set out behind the shop**. In **May 1929** they cut her up. (F056)
- **The scrap went to Ravel Brothers for four hundred and seventy dollars**; Ravel took the
  tires and the brass separately and the boiler shell went into the mill as a stationary
  tank. (F084)
- The cut ran out in 1936 and the company wound up. In **1938** Pearl put the stock book,
  the shop book, the timber ledgers, and eleven boxes of correspondence into a truck and
  drove them to the **Bly County Historical Rooms**, which catalogued them and had not a
  single inquiry about them for twenty-four years. Her only explanation, as Alma reports it:
  she "did not care to have a thing end twice." (F057, F020)
- She knows, vaguely and without interest, that "they had some noise on a bridge down
  there" in the county the engine came from. She has no theory about it and does not connect
  it to anything. (background only)

**What she gets wrong (X15, X16 — reproduce with these wordings).**
1. **"Two thousand five hundred and ten dollars."** She gives the purchase price as
   **$2,510** and repeats it at least once. She must **not** quote the stock book entry —
   she is remembering the page, not reading it.
2. **"That was in 1902."** She dates the purchase to **1902** and keeps that year
   consistently for the arrival, the repainting, and the first season.

**Must NOT mention.** The viaduct, the roller nest, Adela Rennick, Judd Rennick, Ruth
Frayne, the night book, the weather record, the runaway of 1898, the ghost story in any
detail, the 1962 survey, or any Pell County name at all beyond "Pell County."

**Documents she quotes verbatim.**

> *No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile this month. Scrap to Ravel Brothers, $470.*
> — Tolliver Lumber Company shop book, May 1929

## r06 — Lettie Wexler, "A Farm Book of Weather," 1965

**Voice.** Seventy-six, writing a short reminiscence for a farm journal. Spare, dry,
observational, a little proud of never having missed a morning. She thinks in weather and
distrusts stories. Her sentences are short and her figures are exact.

**Vantage point.** She has kept the family weather record herself since 1926 and grew up
copying her father's. She has read Judd Rennick's night book exactly once — in 1962, on the
kitchen table, while the Frayne girl had both books out together — and one of her two
errors comes from that single reading. She is the corpus's disinterested witness and knows
it: "We never wrote down a noise. We wrote down weather."

**What she knows (state these as given).**
- The farm at **Kettle Bench**, three miles up the valley from Cadder Falls. Her father
  **Peter Wexler** kept the record; she has kept it herself since **1926** and keeps it
  still. Daily high, daily low, and rain. (F014, F015, F029)
- She and her father were related to nobody in this story and had no interest in the bridge.
  (F016)
- **The extremes of the whole series**: the highest reading **one hundred and one degrees**,
  in July 1936; the lowest **eleven below zero**, in January 1918. (F050, F080)
- **17 January 1912**, which she quotes exactly because the Frayne girl asked her to find it.
  (F047)
- **The twelve years after the line opened.** Between the winter of 1897–98 and the summer
  of 1909 the record shows **seven nights below six degrees**, four of them after days at
  least thirty degrees warmer. The coldest reading in those twelve years was **nine below,
  on 4 February 1899**. (F085)
- **The two winters that followed.** 1909–10 and 1910–11 never went below **nine above**;
  the coldest was 3 February 1911. (F086)
- The winter of **1917–18** was the hard one — the deepest cold anybody in the valley ever
  wrote down, and the winter the station agent's book showed **five** nights, more than any
  other. (F048)
- Ruth Frayne borrowed the whole record in **1962**, in four cardboard boxes, and brought it
  back. (F020)
- What she saw in the night book: every night the bridge spoke was under six degrees, and

## r09 — "Memorandum on the Sallow Creek Viaduct," office of the Pell County engineer, 1971 (H. L. Quarles)

**Voice.** An internal technical memorandum for a bridge-management file, written by an
engineer who has read a file and never met a witness. Numbered paragraphs, passive
constructions, no adjectives. Cool and slightly condescending about "the local tradition."

**Vantage point.** He has the drawings, the 1954 rehabilitation records, a copy of the 1962
survey report, and a second-hand account of the night book. He never spoke to Judd Rennick
and never handled the field book. His purpose is to record, for the file, the mechanism of
a phenomenon that has been resolved. **His physics is right and his arithmetic is wrong.**

**What he knows (state these as given).**
- The structure: three spans, 120 + 300 + 120 feet, **five hundred and forty feet** overall;
  the center span a pin-connected truss, **ninety-seven feet** above the creek; milepost 22
  on the Cadder Valley branch; the quarry at milepost 31 with a **2.2 percent** descending
  grade over nine miles. (F021, F022, F078, F079)
- The center span's **north bearing is fixed; the south bearing was originally a roller nest
  in a cast pedestal**, replaced in the **1954** rehabilitation by **rocker bearings
  affording four inches of travel each way**. (F023, F031, F052)
- **The mechanism, in full, and correct.** As the span cools it shortens and the south
  bearing travels. If the available travel is exhausted, the shoe **binds**. Contraction
  continues; strain accumulates in the truss; the bearing then **releases suddenly and
  entirely**, and the span takes up its new position in a single movement. The energy
  released produces one deep report of short duration, transmitted efficiently along the
  continuous rail, which is why the event was registered at the depot a mile away and felt in
  the structure before it was heard. It is not periodic, not repetitive within a night, and
  bears no resemblance to a bell or to a locomotive exhaust. (F087)
- **Why it required a rapid fall, not merely a low temperature.** A slow decline permits the
  bearing to creep; a rapid decline of thirty degrees or more within the day binds it. (F088)
- **Why some qualifying nights produced nothing.** Once the bearing has released it is
  seated at the far end of its travel and cannot release again until the span has been
  warmed sufficiently — in practice a daytime thaw above about forty degrees — to return the
  shoe. Successive nights within a single cold spell are therefore silent. The conditions
  observed by the local record are **necessary but not sufficient**. (F089)
- **The 1897 setting.** The nest was set at a chisel mark on the seat corresponding to **two
  inches**, as confirmed by the recovered pedestal. (F025, F032)
- **The August 1909 work.** The deck was renewed with heavier rail and the shoes were jacked
  and re-seated to the same mark, the steel then standing at **eighty-six degrees**. (F045)
- **Cessation.** With four inches of travel the span cannot bind at any temperature the
  valley records; the phenomenon ceased with the 1954 work and has not recurred. (F091)
- The 1962 survey established that no locomotive is or was in the creek bed. (F092)

**What he gets wrong (X25, X26 — reproduce with these wordings, and follow them through his
arithmetic consistently).**
1. **"Sheet 11 specifies four inches of travel each way."** Stated as read from the drawing.
2. **"The coefficient in use for this span is one inch in three hundred feet for fifty
   degrees."** He then computes, and must state, all three of the following, consistently
   with his two wrong inputs:
   - as specified (four inches, span closed at sixty-six degrees): binding at **−134°F**;
   - as constructed (two inches, closed at sixty-six degrees): binding at **−34°F**;
   - after the 1909 re-seating (two inches, seated at eighty-six degrees): binding at
     **−14°F**.

   He remarks that the computed figure "is not readily reconciled with the local record"
   and does not pursue it. **He must never state the figure +6, and must never quote the
   night book's threshold.** Let the discrepancy stand unexplained.

**Must NOT mention.** The night book's totals (61, 43, 34, 9, 5, 66), the +6-degree
