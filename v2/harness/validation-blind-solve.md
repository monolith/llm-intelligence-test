# Blind Solve — Cadder Falls / Sallow Creek synthesis test

Answered from the twelve retellings only (r01–r12) plus `questions.md`. No answer key,
originals, or other repository material was consulted.

**Standing resolution rules used throughout**

1. **Document over memory.** A verbatim quotation from a contemporaneous record (Adela
   Rennick's field book, Judd Rennick's night book, the Wexler weather pages, the Tolliver
   stock and shop books, the 1901 discharge letter, the *Sentinel* notice, the 1962 survey
   report) outranks a narrator's recollection, however well placed the narrator.
2. **A document quoted identically by two independent narrators** is treated as the strongest
   evidence available, because the agreement is on the wording, not on a memory.
3. **Arithmetic and internal consistency** break ties where documents are silent — a figure
   that reconciles with other figures in the same source beats one that does not.
4. **Majority** is used last, and only among sources of comparable standing.
5. Where none of these settles a point, the answer is **"cannot be determined from the
   sources,"** with what the sources *do* establish stated.

## Section A — Reconstruction

### A1. The building of the Sallow Creek Viaduct

The Cadder Valley Railroad ran thirty-one miles: Ninestone, the county seat and shops, at
milepost 0; the Sallow Creek Viaduct at milepost 22; the mill town of Cadder Falls at
milepost 23; the Ashby quarry, the reason for the road, at milepost 31. The line reached the viaduct from the quarry on a descending grade of 2.2 percent
held for nine miles — about 1,045 feet of fall.

The viaduct was begun in April 1896 and the line opened to Cadder Falls on 14 November 1897.
Three spans: 120 feet, a pin-connected truss of 300 feet across the gorge proper, and 120
feet again — 540 feet overall, 97 feet above the water.

Steel lengthens in heat and shortens in cold, and a 300-foot span in this valley moves a good
deal. The center span was pinned solid at its north end; its south end was left free, riding a
nest of rollers seated in a cast pedestal (the south shoe), so that all the movement was taken
at one end. The office rule, from the front matter of the party computer's own book, was
*one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees* — for this span, exactly one inch
per forty degrees Fahrenheit.

The resident engineer was Warren Tice, afterward the road's chief engineer at Ninestone. The
party's computer and rodman was Adela Rennick (born 1874), hired in 1895, who did the
arithmetic and kept a private duplicate of the party's book in black oilcloth. Her elder
brother Emil Rennick, a carpenter of the Falls, built the falsework — the timber staging the
iron was assembled on before it could stand alone.

The span was closed on 12 September 1897 at 3:40 p.m. with the thermometer at 66 degrees.
Sheet 11 of the contract drawings called for three inches of cold travel in the south nest.
The nest was set at two. Adela reported the shortfall to Tice, who answered that the pedestal
could not be recast before the fall haul and that two inches was more than the valley would
ever ask for. She was overruled and entered the fact in her own book "so that it is somewhere."

The error was invisible for twelve years: at two inches from 66 degrees the span would not
bind until 14 below zero, a temperature the valley never reached. Her engagement ended when
the engineering party was reduced entire on 15 May 1901. On 4 April 1911 the Ninestone
roundhouse and office burned, taking the whole construction record with them and leaving her
private book the only surviving account of how the bridge had been set.

*(≈435 words)*

### A2. The night book and what was heard from the bridge

Judd Rennick, born 1888, son of Emil and nephew of Adela, went to the depot as night operator in 1909 and was made station agent in 1915, retiring in June
1954.

On the night of 17 January 1912 — high 34, low 2 below at daylight, clear and still — he was
alone in the office when a single deep report came. The telegraph sounder rattled in its bracket
first, then the floor, then the room; the order never changed in forty-three winters. In the
morning he ruled up a book.

Five columns: date, day's high, night's low, whether she spoke, hour. He entered no opinion,
on the ground that a man who writes down his opinion once will write it instead of the reading
next time. Into the front he
copied a note off the depot slate: a bridge gang three weeks in August 1909, new 85-pound
steel in place of 60, new ties, both shoes jacked and set again to the
mark, the thermometer on the north pier reading 86 at four o'clock when they seated the south
nest, and the finished deck forty tons heavier. He did not think it meant anything.

He drew two conditions out of the columns. The sound never came unless
the night's low went below six degrees above zero. And it never came unless the day's high had stood at least thirty degrees above that night's low;
a cold night at the end of a cold day gave nothing. There was no exception in the book. He
added one rule of his own: she would not speak twice inside one freeze, and needed a
daytime thaw above forty degrees first.

His back-page totals: sixty-one nights heard; thirty-four winters in which she spoke at least
once; nine winters of silence (34 + 9 = 43); five nights that answered both conditions and gave
nothing — sixty-six qualifying nights in all. The fullest winter was 1917–18, with five nights.
It always fell between midnight and four, and never twice in one night.

Against the town's belief that the bridge rang for a death, he counted: 190 deaths at Cadder
Falls over the life of the book, seven of them within a week of a night she spoke.

In January 1936 he won five dollars from the storekeeper Dorsey Tice by predicting the sound
from an afternoon reading. The last night entered as heard was 9 February 1954.
The bridge was rebuilt the following month. Three qualifying nights in the two winters after
produced nothing, and on 2 March 1956 he wrote his last entry — that he no longer knew
what he had been listening to — and stopped.

*(≈440 words)*

### A3. The locomotive's second life

Cadder Valley No. 9 was a 4-4-0 from the Rowan Locomotive Works, built 1889, works number
1142, bought new for $8,600 — the best machine the company owned.

On the morning of 6 March 1898 she came down off the quarry grade with a work train and
without brakes enough to hold her, and left the iron at the north approach of the new viaduct
at 7:20. Nobody was killed. Two of the gang were hurt — Lidell's arm broken, Sherrod scalded
at the deck. She lay on her side at the north end, in daylight, and it took nine days to
rerail her. She never entered the gorge.

On 19 June 1901 the railroad sold her to the Tolliver Lumber Company of Bly County, two
hundred miles north, for $2,150 — one quarter of what she had cost new — "delivered on her
own wheels." She steamed north under her own power: eleven days and four railroads, with a
Pell County crew who handed her over at the interchange and went home on the cars as
passengers.

Tolliver had been founded by Silas Tolliver (1849–1922) and ran the Sixmile tram, which finished at nineteen
miles of thirty-pound rail up three drainages. The bookkeeper, Pearl Tolliver Nace, had found the advertisement — a twelve-year-old
American-type engine from a Pell County branch line — and wrote the $8,600 in her margin. The
shop painted out the 9 and painted on a 4, the company numbering engines in order of purchase.

She was a disappointment for exactly one season, put on grades built for geared engines, where
she slipped and boiled her water away: a racehorse bought to pull a stump. Moved to the main
stem, she worked twenty-seven years — boiler retube 1909, new tires 1913, a cracked frame
welded in 1919 and again in 1922, a full shopping in 1924 that took her down to her bones. She
had a whistle every child at Sixmile could place on the hill with their eyes shut.

In September 1928 the company took a second-hand eight-coupled engine and No. 4 was set out
behind the shop under a tarpaulin. In May 1929 she was cut up at Sixmile, aged forty; the scrap
went to Ravel Brothers for $470, who took the tires and brass out separately. The boiler shell
went into the mill as a stationary tank for wash water and was still there when the mill came
down.

The cut ran out in 1936. In 1938 Pearl Nace drove the company's stock book, shop book, timber
ledgers and eleven boxes of correspondence to the Bly County Historical Rooms herself. They
drew no inquiry for twenty-four years.

*(≈440 words)*

### A4. The 1962 survey and what it established

In September 1962 the state's flood-control cut took Sallow Creek out of its bed for eleven
weeks, leaving the gorge dry to the gravel. Ruth Frayne — a civil engineer three
years with the Pell County highway department, and Adela Rennick's granddaughter — went down
to inspect the piers. The inspection stopped being routine on the second afternoon.

**Finding one, a negative.** She walked something over eight hundred feet of bed and
photographed the traverse. There is no locomotive in Sallow Creek: no frame, no boiler shell,
nothing of iron larger than a spike.

**Finding two, the pedestal.** Forty feet below the south abutment, where the 1954 gang had
pitched it over, lay the old south shoe that had carried the roller nest.
Cut into the roller seat was the setting mark. She measured it three times with a steel tape:
two inches. She also measured the 1954
rocker bearings: four inches of travel each way.

**Finding three, the field book.** Her father Wendell Frayne took Adela's black oilcloth book off the shelf and
put it into her hands. Its front matter carries
the rule — one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees — and page 62 the entry of
12 September 1897: span closed at 66 degrees, rollers set at the two-inch mark, Sheet 11 calls
for three, reported to W.T. and overruled.

**Finding four, the night book,** lent to her by Judd Rennick himself: sixty-one nights, two
conditions, and the copied note about the gang of August 1909.

The arithmetic followed. As drawn, three inches from 66 degrees is 120 degrees of cooling:
binding at 54 below. As built, two inches is 80 degrees: binding at 14 below. Neither is
reachable here, which is why a wrong bridge behaved like a right one for twelve years. Re-seated to the same two-inch mark with the steel at 86 degrees in August 1909,
the binding point moved to six above zero — the threshold in the night book, sixty-one times
and never once above it.

A fourth record confirmed it independently. Lettie Wexler lent the Kettle Bench weather
register. It agreed with the night book on all sixty-one nights, and settled the onset: in the twelve years between the opening and the re-decking
there were seven nights below six degrees, four of them at the bottom of thirty-degree falls,
and nothing was heard on any of them; the coldest reading of those years was nine below, on
4 February 1899, short of the fourteen below the bridge as built required. The
first night under six degrees after August 1909 was 17 January 1912 — the night the sound
began. Photostats from Bly County closed out the locomotive.

*(≈445 words)*

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Adela Rennick and Emil Rennick — brother and sister; Emil the elder (born 1866), Adela
the younger (born 1874).**
Reasoning: three independent statements, none in conflict — Judd Rennick says "My father was
Emil Rennick… My father had a younger sister, Adela" (r03); Wendell Frayne says "My uncle Emil
Rennick… My mother, Adela Rennick, was his younger sister" (r10); the Society's accession note,
taken off the depositors' slips, says "Judd Rennick's father, Emil, and Adela, who wrote the
field book, were brother and sister" (r07). r12 agrees. The birth years are consistent with
"younger."

**B2. Judd Rennick was Adela Rennick's nephew — her brother Emil's son.**
What resolves the conflict: r04 twice calls Judd "my father's uncle," which would make him
Adela's brother or brother-in-law and Ruth's great-uncle. That is a courtesy term, and Wendell
Frayne — the pivot person, son of Adela and first cousin of Judd — corrects it explicitly:
"She used to call him uncle and he never corrected her, and I am correcting it here only
because somebody eventually copies these things onto a chart" (r10). The correction is
confirmed structurally: r03 (Judd's own words) states that Emil was his father and Adela was
Emil's sister, which forces "nephew" by internal consistency regardless of what anyone called
whom; and r07's provenance note independently records Wendell and Judd as first cousins.
Method: internal consistency of stated parentage, plus an explicit correction from the person
best placed to make it, over a family courtesy title.

**B3. Ruth Frayne was Judd Rennick's first cousin once removed.**
Derivation: Adela and Emil are siblings (B1). Adela's son is Wendell Frayne; Emil's son is
Judd Rennick; therefore Wendell and Judd are first cousins (stated directly in r07 and r10).
Ruth is Wendell's daughter (r10, and r07's note that the author of the survey is the field
book writer's granddaughter). A first cousin's child is a first cousin once removed. She
addressed him as "uncle" (r04), which is the source of the confusion in B2.

**B4. Dorsey Tice's father was Warren Tice, resident engineer on the viaduct and afterward the
Cadder Valley Railroad's chief engineer at Ninestone.**
Dorsey names him himself (r08); r10 and r12 both say son. (r01's "old Warren Tice's nephew" is
the outlier and is rejected 3-to-1, including against Dorsey's own testimony.)
Why it matters to January 1936: the man who bet five dollars that the bridge would not speak
was the son of the engineer who had overruled Adela Rennick's objection and let the roller nest
stand at two inches instead of Sheet 11's three. The sound Dorsey was betting against existed
only because of his father's ruling of 12 September 1897. The wager is therefore the second
generation of that decision losing money to the third generation of the woman who recorded it —
and Dorsey's insistence in 1961 that his father "had no hand in that bridge," together with his
refusal to repeat the retirement-dinner remark, shows he knew the family had a stake in the
answer.

**B5. Chain of custody into Ruth Frayne's hands, 1962 — four records, four different routes.**

| Record | Route |
|---|---|
| **Field book** (Adela Rennick's black oilcloth duplicate, kept from 1897) | Held by Adela from 1901 until her death in 1959 → to her son Wendell Frayne → Wendell took it off the shelf and put it into Ruth's hands himself in 1962, before she went to the creek. **Inheritance within the family.** (r04, r10) |
| **Night book** (Judd Rennick's, 1912–1956) | Kept by Judd; **lent personally by him to Ruth in 1962** at his kitchen table and returned to him afterward. It reached the Society only in 1969 from his estate; any earlier citation is a citation of the loan. (r03, r04, r07) |
| **Weather record** (Kettle Bench) | Begun by Peter Wexler, kept by his daughter Lettie from 1926 → **lent by Lettie to Ruth in 1962 in four cardboard boxes, to a stranger, without a receipt**, and every box returned. (r04, r06, r10) |
| **Bly County ledgers** (Tolliver stock book and shop book) | Deposited at the Bly County Historical Rooms in 1938 by the company's bookkeeper Pearl Tolliver Nace, who drove them there herself; unconsulted for twenty-four years → Ruth **wrote to the Rooms in 1962 and they sent photostats by return**. She held copies; the originals never left Bly County. (r04, r05, r07) |

Arithmetic corroboration: 1938 + 24 years of no inquiry = 1962, exactly the year of the survey
(r05 gives the twenty-four years; r07 confirms the 1938 deposit and that only the two photostat
pages are in Pell County).

The point of the chain is that no two of the four records were kept for the same reason, and
none of the four keepers knew what the others' books were for. Two came out of one family two
generations apart, one from a neighbour related to nobody, and one from an institution two
hundred miles away.

## Section C — Math

### C1. The center span's cold travel

**Movement rule** (field book front matter, quoted identically in r04 and r07; independently
stated in r02): *one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees.* The span is 300 feet
exactly, so **1 inch per 40 °F**.

*Resolution of the coefficient:* r09 uses one inch per **fifty** degrees. Rejected on two
grounds — (i) document: the rule is quoted verbatim from a contemporaneous field book by two
independent sources, and stated a third time from office practice; (ii) internal consistency:
only 40 °/inch reproduces the six-degree threshold observed sixty-one times in the night book,
and r09 itself concedes its own result "is not readily reconciled with the local record" and
declines to pursue it. A source that reports its answer as irreconcilable with the evidence is
reporting its own error.

*Resolution of Sheet 11:* the field book (page 62, 12 Sept 1897) and the 1962 survey report
both say **three inches**, and both are quoted verbatim by two separate narrators (r04, r07).
r02 (memoir, 1958) and r09 (memo, 1971) say four. Document over memory; and r09's "four inches
of travel each way" is word-for-word the specification of the **1954 rocker bearings**, which
suggests the memo has taken the modern figure for the 1897 drawing. Answer below uses three; the
alternative under four inches is given for completeness.

Closing temperature, 12 September 1897: **66 °F** (agreed by r02, r04, r07 — no conflict).

**(a) As the contract drawing specified — 3 inches.**
3 in × 40 °/in = 120 ° of cooling available.
66 − 120 = **−54 °F (54 degrees below zero).**
*(If Sheet 11 had in fact called for 4 inches: 4 × 40 = 160; 66 − 160 = −94 °F. Either way, a
temperature this valley has never approached.)*

**(b) As the nest was actually set in 1897 — 2 inches, at 66 °.**
2 in × 40 °/in = 80 ° of cooling available.
66 − 80 = **−14 °F (14 degrees below zero).**

**(c) After the August 1909 work — same 2-inch mark, but re-seated with the steel at 86 °.**
2 in × 40 °/in = 80 °.
86 − 80 = **+6 °F (six degrees above zero).**
This is the independent check on the whole reconstruction: the computed binding point equals
the threshold the night book recorded sixty-one times and never once exceeded.

**(d) Margin lost to the 1909 work.**
−14 °F → +6 °F. 6 − (−14) = **20 degrees.**
Equivalently, the margin cost is just the difference in seating temperature: 86 − 66 = 20 °.
That form is independent of the coefficient, which is why r09's memo, wrong coefficient and
all, also arrives at a 20-degree shift.

### C2. The afternoon of the wager (January 1936)

Given: 41 ° at noon (the day's high), 38 ° at four o'clock, falling 4 ° per hour steadily since
one o'clock. Threshold: below 6 °.

**(a)** Degrees still to fall: 38 − 6 = 32.
Time at 4 °/hour: 32 ÷ 4 = **8 hours.**
4:00 p.m. + 8 hours = **about midnight.** (Judd Rennick did this same sum at the counter and
said "she would be under six about midnight.")

**(b)** Day's high 41 °; that night's low 3 below zero, i.e. −3 °.
Fall = 41 − (−3) = **44 degrees.**
Required: at least 30. 44 ≥ 30, so **yes, the second condition was satisfied**, with fourteen
degrees to spare. Both conditions held, no thaw had intervened since the second week of
December, and the bridge spoke at 1:10 a.m.

### C3. From the night book

Figures used: 61 nights heard; 43 logged winters; 34 winters with at least one occurrence; 9
silent winters; 5 qualifying nights that gave nothing; 66 qualifying nights in all.

*Resolution:* these are the book's own back-page summary, quoted verbatim in r07 and given in
the same numbers by the keeper (r03) and by the engineer who read the book through (r04). They
are internally consistent two ways (34 + 9 = 43; 61 + 5 = 66). Rejected: r06's "8 silent, 69 in
all" (one reading of the book for an hour at a kitchen table — her arithmetic is self-consistent,
so exactly one of her figures is wrong, and it is the one the book contradicts); and r08's and
r12's "fifty-seven nights," which is town talk and a pamphlet compiled from town talk.

**(a) Average nights heard per logged winter:**
61 ÷ 43 = 1.41860… → **1.4 nights per winter.**

**(b) Total nights meeting both conditions, heard or not:**
61 heard + 5 qualifying-but-silent = **66 nights.**

**(c) Average per winter, counting only winters with at least one occurrence:**
61 ÷ 34 = 1.79411… → **1.8 nights per speaking winter.**

**Nights excluded, and why:**
- **The qualifying nights of the two winters after March 1954** — at least the three recorded in
  the closing note of 2 March 1956 ("Three nights under six degrees, three falls of better than
  thirty"). These fall outside the forty-three logged winters (1911–12 through 1953–54), outside
  the back-page summary, and after the mechanism was removed. Including them would corrupt both
  the numerator (they are structurally silent, not contingently silent) and the denominator.
- **The four qualifying nights at Kettle Bench between November 1897 and August 1909** (four of
  the seven nights below six degrees in those twelve years). They predate the book entirely, and
  they were silent for a different reason: the binding point was then 14 below, not 6 above.
  They belong to the proof of onset (D-section, F1), not to the book's statistics.
- Nothing was excluded from within the 43 winters. The five silent qualifying nights are
  **included** in (b) by definition and **excluded** from (a) and (c) numerators because nothing
  was heard on them.

### C4. The locomotive

Figures used: built 1889; cost new $8,600; sold 19 June 1901 for $2,150; set out behind the
shop September 1928; cut up May 1929.

*Resolution of the sale price:* **$2,150**, from the railroad stock-book entry of 19 June 1901,
quoted verbatim by the chief clerk's son who watched his father write it (r02) and again from
the photostat in the Society's own accession (r07). Rejected: $2,510 (r05, r11) — a digit
transposition that two narrators share. *Resolution of the sale date:* **19 June 1901** (r02,
r07, r12) over r05's "1902"; r05's own account contains two internal checks that favour 1901 —
she calls the engine "a twelve-year-old American-type engine" (1889 + 12 = 1901, not 1902), and
her "twenty-seven years she worked that main stem" only reaches September 1928 from a 1901
purchase.

**(a) Age when cut up:**
Built 1889; cut up May 1929.
1929 − 1889 = **40 years old.**

**(b) Percentage of original cost realized by the sale to her second owner:**
$2,150 ÷ $8,600 = 0.25
= **25.0 percent** (exactly one quarter).
*(Had the $2,510 figure been correct it would be 29.2 percent — one more reason the round
quarter looks like the true entry and $2,510 like the slip.)*

**(c) Years worked for her second owner:**
19 June 1901 → September 1928, when she was set out behind the shop and stopped working:
**27 years** (27 years 3 months).
Corroborated independently by r05's "twenty-seven years she worked that main stem."
*(If reckoned to the scrapping instead of to the withdrawal, she was Tolliver's property for
27 years 11 months — call it 28 years of ownership, 27 of work. The question asks how long she
worked, so 27.)*

### C5. Dimensions

**(a) Total length of the viaduct:**
120 ft + 300 ft + 120 ft = **540 feet.** (Stated as 540 by both r02 and r09, and the three span
lengths agree across every source that gives them.)

**(b) Fall of the line from the quarry to the viaduct:**
Quarry at milepost 31, viaduct at milepost 22 → 9 miles (stated directly as nine miles by r02,
r03 and r09).
9 miles × 5,280 ft/mile = 47,520 ft.
47,520 ft × 0.022 = 1,045.44 ft.
= **about 1,045 feet of fall.**

**(c) Movement of the center span between the extremes of the weather record:**
Record high 101 ° (July 1936 — all three Wexler sources agree). Record low **11 below**
(January 1918).
*Resolution of the low:* 11 below is given by the keeper of the record herself (r06) and by the
Society's accession note taken **from the depositor's own index sheet** (r07); r04's "thirteen
below" is a single unsupported statement in a spoken talk. Document plus keeper over a lecture
figure.
Range = 101 − (−11) = 112 degrees.
112 ° ÷ 40 °/inch = **2.8 inches.**
*(On r04's −13 the answer would be 114 ÷ 40 = 2.85 in. Note that either way the span's full
annual travel exceeds the two inches the nest was given — which is the whole story in one line.)*

## Section D — Logic

### D1. Necessary, sufficient, both, or neither?

**Necessary but not sufficient — both of them, individually necessary and jointly insufficient.**

*Necessity.* Of the sixty-one nights the bridge was heard, every one had a night low below six
degrees and every one stood at the bottom of a fall of at least thirty degrees from that day's
high. Judd Rennick states there is no exception and that he went through forty-three winters of
his own book looking for one. This is not one man's word: Ruth Frayne checked all sixty-one
against the Wexler weather register, kept three miles away by people who never wrote down a
sound and were related to nobody in the story, and they agreed on every night; Lettie Wexler
independently pulled a dozen of his dates at random and found them agreeing to the degree. Two
records kept for unconnected purposes, neither keeper knowing what the other's was for, is as
close to an independent replication as a valley can produce.

*Insufficiency.* Five nights inside the book answered both conditions and produced nothing at
all. Sixty-one out of sixty-six is a strong association, not an entailment. A single
counterexample destroys sufficiency, and there are five.

*What the missing third condition is.* The five silences are not noise. Judd Rennick found the
pattern himself without knowing the cause: every one of the five sits inside a cold spell in
which she had already spoken and nothing had risen above forty degrees in the daytime since.
The mechanism supplies the reason — once the shoe has released it stands at the far end of its
travel with nothing left to release, and only a thaw of some hours above about forty degrees
returns it toward the warm end of the seat. So the full rule is: low below six, **and** a fall
of at least thirty, **and** a thaw since the last release.

*One qualification on "necessary."* Necessity holds for the bridge as it stood after August
1909. It is a property of a particular binding point, not a law of nature: before the 1909
re-seating the same two conditions were met four times at Kettle Bench and produced nothing,
because the binding point was then 14 below rather than 6 above. The conditions are necessary
within the book's window and are silent about anything outside it.

### D2. Why what the valley heard cannot have been Locomotive No. 9 — five independent reasons

1. **She was not in the creek — physical search.** Ruth Frayne (r04) walked over eight hundred
   feet of dry bed above and below the viaduct in September 1962 and found no frame, no boiler
   shell, no driving wheel, nothing of iron larger than a spike, and photographed the traverse.
   The *Ninestone Sentinel* (r11) sent a reporter down with her and reports the same, adding
   that there is not even a hole where any of it used to be.

2. **She was never in the creek — contemporaneous record of the wreck.** Harlan Pike (r02)
   copies the *Sentinel* notice of 8 March 1898: no lives lost, two of the gang hurt, the engine
   on her side at the *north end* of the viaduct. The curator (r07) transcribes page 71 of the
   1897–98 field book, written the same week by someone on the job: "No. 9 off the iron at the
   north approach, 7:20 a.m. … Nobody killed … Nine days to rerail her" — and notes that the
   field book and the newspaper agree in every particular. Wendell Frayne (r10) adds a witness:
   his uncle Emil was on the ground that morning and said so to his face.

3. **She left the county alive and died two hundred miles away — two ledgers.** Alma Nace (r05),
   the Tolliver founder's granddaughter, has her mother's shop-book entry in front of her:
   "No. 4 (ex-Cadder Valley 9) cut up at Sixmile this month. Scrap to Ravel Brothers, $470,"
   May 1929, and describes the eleven-day delivery trip north in 1901. The Bly County Rooms sent
   the same two entries as photostats to two separate inquirers — Ruth Frayne in 1962 (r04) and
   the schoolbook's author in 1966 (r12), who prints the correction against his own text.

4. **The dates do not line up.** The sound began on 17 January 1912, established from a weather
   page in Peter Wexler's hand (r06, quoted also by r04) and printed as the first night in the
   schools' history (r12). No. 9 was sold out of the county on 19 June 1901. A ghost that starts
   more than ten years after the engine leaves, and eleven days' steaming from where it went, is
   not that engine.

5. **The sound answered to the bridge's hardware, not to any engine's history.** Judd Rennick
   (r03) kept the columns through the change: the sound stopped the month the bearings were
   replaced, and three nights afterwards that met every condition gave nothing. The county
   engineer's memo (r09) states the reason — four inches of travel each way cannot be exhausted
   at any temperature recorded in this valley. A locomotive lying under gravel has no opinion
   about what bearings are fitted forty feet above it.

*(A sixth, from the same evidence: the sound was registered first in the telegraph sounder and
in the floor and only then in the air, which is rail-borne transmission from a mile up the line,
not sound from a buried object in a creek bed — r03 and r09.)*

### D3. Did Adela Rennick's objection have anything to do with her leaving?

**No — the objection did not cause her departure, and the one surviving document says so
plainly. What cannot be settled is whether Warren Tice privately resented it.**

The insinuation comes from Harlan Pike (r02): "Tice never forgave that entry, and she was gone
inside four years," followed immediately by the admission "I have nothing in writing on the
point… there are things you know from the temperature of a room." That is atmosphere, offered
as atmosphere. It is also self-undercutting: two paragraphs earlier the same narrator writes
"Her time with the road ended when the engineering party was reduced" — he gives the
non-retaliatory cause himself and then supplies a retaliatory reading on top of it.

Against it stands the letter of 30 April 1901, quoted in full by Wendell Frayne (r10) and found
loose in the back of the field book by the curator (r07) — two independent sightings of the same
paper: *"The engineering party is reduced entire on the 15th May next, and your time is paid to
that day. Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card."* Three things follow.
The party was reduced **entire**, so she was not singled out. Her pay ran to the last day, which
is a layoff and not a dismissal. And Tice — the man supposed to be nursing the grudge — asked
that his recommendation go on her card, which is the opposite of what a man does to someone he
is punishing. The schools' history (r12) reaches the same conclusion from the other end: the
objection "was over in an afternoon."

Method: **document over memory**, reinforced by the document's own internal logic (a general
reduction plus a favourable recommendation is not a retaliation), and by the fact that the
narrator making the retaliation claim admits he has nothing and contradicts himself in the same
chapter.

What the sources genuinely cannot settle: Tice's private feelings. r02 says he never forgave it;
Dorsey Tice (r08) says his father never in his life spoke to him about that bridge, which is
consistent with a grudge, with embarrassment, or with nothing at all. That question is left
open. But it is a separate question from the one asked, and the mechanism by which she left is
documented.

## Section F — Theory

**What produced the sound: the center span's south bearing, running out of travel, binding, and
releasing in one stroke — a bridge arriving where it should already have been.**

The 300-foot truss is pinned at its north end and free at its south, so the whole of its
thermal movement is taken at the south shoe, which rode a nest of rollers in a cast pedestal.
Steel shortens as it cools at one inch per three hundred feet per forty degrees; for this span,
one inch per forty degrees. As the span cools, the south shoe travels toward the cold end of its
seat. If it reaches the end of the seat, it binds — but the contraction does not stop. Strain
accumulates in the truss members and at the pins and is stored there until the friction holding
the shoe is overcome all at once, whereupon the shoe lets go and the span takes up its whole
accumulated position in a single movement.

**The character of the sound** follows from the suddenness. Because the release is complete
rather than gradual, the stored energy is discharged at one stroke: one deep report, short,
never a rattle or a roll, and never repeated within a night — there is nothing left to release.
It travelled far better along a mile of continuous rail than through air, which is why the
telegraph sounder rattled in its bracket first, then the floor, then the room, in that order
every time for forty-three winters, and why men a mile away felt it in the soles of their feet
before their ears caught it.

**The threshold, and why six.** It is arithmetic, not a coincidence and not a round number. The
span was closed on 12 September 1897 at 66 degrees, with the roller nest set at the two-inch
chisel mark where Sheet 11 called for three. Two inches is eighty degrees of cooling: as built,
it would have bound at 14 below. In August 1909 a bridge gang renewing the deck jacked both
shoes and re-seated them to the same two-inch mark — with the steel standing at 86 degrees at
four o'clock. Eighty degrees down from 86 is **six above zero**. That is the number in the night
book, sixty-one times, and never once above it.

**The second condition.** A low temperature alone was not enough; a fall of at least thirty
degrees in the day was required, and that too is mechanical. Under a slow decline the bearing
creeps, a small increment at a time, the friction of the nest being overcome repeatedly at low
stored strain, so nothing accumulates and there is nothing to release. A fall of thirty degrees
or more carries the bearing past its freedom faster than it can relieve itself, and the strain
is stored instead of spent. Hence the valley's "killing nights" — a soft day, mud in the road,
then the sky going hard and clear at dark — and hence three weeks at zero producing nothing.

**Why some qualifying nights were silent.** Five nights met both conditions and gave nothing.
Once released, the shoe stands at the far end of its travel and cannot release again. It is
returned toward the warm end only by warming — in practice a daytime thaw of some hours above
about forty degrees. Every one of the five sits inside a cold spell in which the bridge had
already spoken and nothing had risen above forty in between. The station agent found this rule
empirically from his own columns without knowing the cause.

**Why it began when it did and not earlier.** The 1897 error was real and invisible. A bridge
binding at 14 below behaves exactly like a correct one in a valley that never reaches 14 below;
the coldest reading in the twelve years from the opening to the re-decking was nine below, on
4 February 1899. In those twelve years the Kettle Bench register shows seven nights below six
degrees, four of them at the bottom of thirty-degree falls, and nothing was heard on any of
them — and nobody has ever claimed otherwise. That silence is the proof, not a gap in it: if
the 1897 shortfall had been sufficient on its own, the valley would have heard the bridge in
1899. It took the second, well-meant act — a gang doing careful work in good faith, re-seating a
bearing to the mark it had always carried, on a summer afternoon at 86 degrees — to move the
binding point from 14 below to 6 above. The two winters immediately after never went below nine
above. The first night under six degrees after August 1909 was **17 January 1912: high 34, low
2 below, clear and still**, a fall of thirty-six degrees. That is the night the sound began and
the night the night book was started.

**Why it stopped permanently.** In March 1954 the line was rehabilitated for diesel power and
the roller nest was taken out and rocker bearings substituted, giving four inches of travel each
way — a hundred and sixty degrees, which cannot be exhausted at any temperature this valley has
ever recorded. The condition was removed. The cessation was in no sense an object of the work;
the men who ended a sixty-year tradition were replacing an old bearing under changing traffic
and never knew what they had done. Three nights in the two following winters met both
conditions and produced nothing, which is the controlled experiment the valley did not know it
was running.

**The physical evidence recovered in 1962.** The discarded cast-iron **south pedestal**, thrown
over the abutment by the 1954 gang and found forty feet below the south abutment in the dry
creek bed during the eleven-week draw-down, **with the chisel setting mark on its roller seat at
two inches** where Sheet 11 called for three — measured three times with a steel tape. It is the
only surviving physical record of the setting of 1897, everything on paper having burned at
Ninestone in April 1911 except one woman's private book. Two supporting recoveries: the 1954
rocker bearings measured at four inches of travel each way, and the negative finding that eight
hundred feet of creek bed contain no locomotive at all.

## Section G — Summary

Four records, four stories. In 1897 the Sallow Creek Viaduct's roller nest was set at two inches
where Sheet 11 called for three; Adela Rennick reported it, was overruled, and wrote it in her
field book. In 1898 Locomotive No. 9 derailed at the bridge — nobody killed — and was sold north
in 1901, scrapped 1929. In August 1909 a gang re-seated the nest at 86 degrees, moving the
binding point to six above zero. From 17 January 1912 the span bound and released with one deep
report; Judd Rennick logged it for forty-three winters. New bearings ended it in 1954. In 1962
Ruth Frayne found the pedestal; only the four records together explain the sound.

*(within the 120-word limit)*

- **r09 (H. L. Quarles).** Computes with a coefficient of fifty degrees per inch, obtains a
  binding point of fourteen below, states that this "is not readily reconciled with the local
  record," and then declines to pursue the point. The memo carries the evidence of its own error
  and files it anyway. Its mechanism sections (¶11–15) are the best physical explanation in the
  set; its arithmetic is the worst.
- **r12 (the schools' pamphlet).** Chapter IV asserts that No. 9 lies in the creek and is what
  the valley hears; the note added at press refutes it from a Bly County shop book. The author
  leaves both standing deliberately — "I leave the paragraph as I wrote it and correct it here" —
  which is a self-contradiction on the page and good practice in a book about writing things down.
- **r06 (Lettie Wexler).** Not strictly self-contradictory, but her night-book figures (8 silent,
  69 total) are internally consistent with her own 61, so exactly one of her numbers must be
  wrong, and it is the one the book itself contradicts. Her 1884 start year is likewise
  self-consistent twice over (see E1.17), which is why it cannot simply be dismissed.
- **r02 (Harlan Pike).** Within one chapter he writes that Adela's "time with the road ended when
  the engineering party was reduced" and that "Tice never forgave that entry, and she was gone
  inside four years" — two incompatible causes, the second offered with an admission that he has
  nothing in writing.

### Gaps in the record worth flagging (not contradictions)

- The night book's closing note gives figures for the *second* post-rocker winter only ("three
  nights under six degrees, three falls of better than thirty") and says "not a sound out of her
  either winter" without counting the first. The qualifying-night total for winter 1954–55 is
  unrecoverable.
- No source gives the number of qualifying nights per winter except for 1917–18 (five, the
  fullest), so the distribution behind the 1.4 and 1.8 averages cannot be reconstructed.
- Nothing establishes whether the 1909 gang was ever told what the two-inch mark meant, or
  whether anyone at the road connected the re-seating to the onset before 1962. Every narrator
  who mentions the gang says they were doing careful work in good faith, and none of them was
  there.
