## Section A — Reconstruction

### A1. The building of the Sallow Creek Viaduct

The Cadder Valley Railroad ran thirty-one miles: Ninestone (milepost 0, county seat, shops and
office) to the Ashby quarry (milepost 31), with the Sallow Creek Viaduct at milepost 22 and the
mill town of Cadder Falls at milepost 23. The road existed to bring stone down from the quarry,
and the nine miles from quarry to viaduct fell at a steady 2.2 percent, about 1,045 feet of
drop, a great deal for a small road with second-hand brakes.

The viaduct was begun in April 1896. Three spans: 120 feet, a pin-connected truss of 300 feet
across the gorge, then 120 feet again; 540 feet in all, standing 97 feet above the creek. The
resident engineer was Warren Tice, later the company's chief engineer. Emil Rennick of the
Falls, a carpenter, built the falsework. The party's computer, the person who did the
arithmetic, was his younger sister Adela Rennick, born 1874, who also ran a rod in the field
and kept a duplicate book of her own in black oilcloth alongside the company's. She had gone to
the road in 1895.

Steel lengthens in heat and shortens in cold. The center span was therefore pinned at its north
end and left free at the south, where it rode on a nest of rollers seated in a cast pedestal.
The working rule in her book was one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees; for this
span, one inch per forty degrees.

The span was closed at 3:40 p.m. on 12 September 1897 with the thermometer at 66 degrees.
Sheet 11 of the contract drawings called for three inches of travel in the south nest. The
rollers were set at the two-inch mark. Adela Rennick reported it; Tice answered that the
pedestal could not be recast before the fall haul and that two inches was more than the valley
would ever ask for. She entered the objection in her own book "so that it is somewhere." The
line opened to Cadder Falls on 14 November 1897.

Her employment ended on 15 May 1901 when the engineering party was reduced entire. She married
Josiah Frayne in 1902.

Two later events fixed the bridge's history. On 4 April 1911 the Ninestone roundhouse and
office burned, taking the company's whole construction record; nothing then survived in writing
about the building of the bridge but her book. And in August 1909 a bridge gang spent three
weeks on the structure: 85-pound steel for 60, new ties throughout, both shoes jacked and
re-seated to the same two-inch mark, the north-pier thermometer at 86 degrees when they seated
the south nest, and the finished deck forty tons heavier.

### A2. The night book and what was heard from the bridge

Judd Rennick, born 1888, son of Emil the carpenter and therefore nephew to Adela, went to the
Cadder Falls depot as night operator in 1909, was made station agent in 1915, and stayed until
he retired in June 1954.

On the night of 17 January 1912 (high 34, low 2 below, clear and still) he heard it for the
first time and thought a car had got away in the quarry. In the morning he ruled up a book:
five columns, the date, the day's high, the night's low, whether she spoke, and the hour. He
kept it forty-three winters, through the winter of 1953-54.

The sound was one deep report and then nothing. Not a rattle, not a roll, never repeated inside
a night, always between midnight and four. It arrived up the rails first, the telegraph sounder
rattling in its bracket, then through the floor, then in the air.

Out of the book he drew two conditions. She never spoke unless the night's low went below six
degrees, and never unless the day's high stood at least thirty degrees above the night that
followed: a soft day with the bottom out of it, not a cold night at the end of a cold day.
There was no exception in forty-three winters. The back page: sixty-one nights she spoke;
thirty-four winters she spoke at least once and nine she never spoke at all; five further
nights answered both conditions and gave nothing, making sixty-six qualifying nights. His rule
for the five: she would not speak twice inside one freeze until a daytime thaw above forty
degrees had intervened.

The valley believed it was Locomotive No. 9, which ran away off the quarry grade on 6 March
1898 and left the rails at the north approach, with her crew supposedly drowned in the gorge
and never brought up. Nobody was killed in that wreck and the engine never entered the water;
the belief was fourteen years older than the sound and was fastened onto it afterward. The
grandmothers' corollary was that the bridge rang for a death within the week. Judd counted
instead: 190 deaths at Cadder Falls in his years, seven inside a week of a night she spoke.

On 14 January 1936 he did the sum at Dorsey Tice's counter and said she would speak before
morning. Tice bet five dollars against it. The low was 3 below; she spoke at 1:10 a.m.

The last night entered as heard was 9 February 1954. In March the bearings were replaced. Three
qualifying nights in the two following winters gave nothing, and on 2 March 1956 he wrote his
last note and stopped.

### A3. The locomotive's second life

Cadder Valley No. 9 was a 4-4-0 from the Rowan Locomotive Works, built 1889, works number 1142,
and cost the company $8,600 new. On the morning of 6 March 1898 she came down off the Ashby
quarry grade with a work train and brakes that would not hold, and went off the iron at the
north approach of the new viaduct at 7:20 a.m. Nobody was killed; Lidell's arm was broken and
Sherrod was scalded at the deck; nine days to rerail her.

On 19 June 1901 the road sold her to the Tolliver Lumber Company of Bly County, two hundred
miles north, for $2,150, a quarter of what she had cost, delivered on her own wheels. She
steamed north under her own power, eleven days and four railroads, with a Pell County crew who
handed her over at the interchange and went home on the cars as passengers.

Tolliver had been built by Silas Tolliver (1849-1922) around the Sixmile tram, named for the
creek and not its length: by the end nineteen miles of thirty-pound rail up three drainages.
His daughter Pearl Tolliver Nace, born 1881, kept the books from the age of eighteen for
thirty-nine years, and it was she who found the advertisement for the twelve-year-old
American-type engine and put it on her father's desk. He wanted a road engine that would bring
a made-up train down the main stem faster than a man could walk.

The shop painted out the 9 and painted on a 4, the company numbering engines in order of
purchase. She was a disappointment for exactly one season, put on grades built for geared
machines, where she slipped and boiled her water away: "a racehorse to pull a stump." Kept to
the main stem afterward, she became an institution, with a whistle the county could tell apart
and a tread the children felt in the floor before they heard her. Boiler retube 1909, new tires
1913, a cracked frame welded in 1919 and again in 1922, a full shopping in 1924.

In September 1928 the company took delivery of a second-hand eight-coupled engine and set No. 4
out behind the shop under a tarpaulin, after twenty-seven years of work. In May 1929 they cut
her up at Sixmile, aged forty; the scrap went to Ravel Brothers for $470, and the boiler shell
served out its days as the mill's wash-water tank.

The cut ran out in 1936. In 1938 Pearl Nace drove the stock book, the shop book, the timber
ledgers and eleven boxes of correspondence to the Bly County Historical Rooms herself. Nobody
asked to see them for twenty-four years.

### A4. The 1962 survey and what it established

Ruth Frayne, born 1934, was a civil engineer with the Pell County highway department,
daughter of Wendell Frayne and granddaughter of Adela Rennick. In September 1962 the state's
flood-control cut took Sallow Creek out of its bed for eleven weeks and left the gorge dry to
the gravel. She went out to inspect the piers, and it stopped being routine on the second
afternoon.

She reported four findings. First, nothing: she walked over eight hundred feet of bed above and
below the viaduct and found nothing of iron larger than a spike. There is no locomotive in
Sallow Creek and there never was one.

Second, the pedestal: forty feet below the south abutment, where the 1954 gang had thrown it, a
casting the size of a kitchen stove, the old south shoe that had carried the roller nest. Cut
into the roller seat was the setting mark, which she measured three times with a steel tape:
two inches. The 1954 rocker bearings measure four inches of travel each way.

Third, her grandmother's field book, kept until her death in 1959 and handed to Ruth by her
father: the front-matter rule ("one inch in three hundred feet for forty degrees") and the
entry of 12 September 1897 recording the span closed at 66 degrees, the rollers set at two
inches where Sheet 11 called for three, and the objection overruled.

Fourth, Judd Rennick's night book, lent to her by him personally.

The arithmetic: three inches is 120 degrees of cooling, so as drawn the span would have bound
at 54 below. As built, two inches from 66 degrees gives 14 below, also never reached, which is
why a wrong bridge behaved like a right one for twelve years. But the 1909 gang re-seated the
nest to the same mark at 86 degrees, and 86 less 80 is 6 above zero: the exact threshold in the
night book.

Her second witness was the Wexler weather record from Kettle Bench, lent in four boxes by
Lettie Wexler, who was related to nobody in the story and never wrote down a sound. It agreed
with the night book on every one of the sixty-one nights, and it settled the onset: in the
twelve years between the opening and the re-decking there were seven nights below six degrees,
four at the bottom of thirty-degree falls, all silent, the coldest reading being 9 below on
4 February 1899. The two winters after the re-decking never went below 9 above. The first night
under six degrees after August 1909 was 17 January 1912, the night the sound began.

She also wrote to Bly County and got photostats of the 1901 sale and the 1929 scrapping.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Brother and sister — Emil the elder, Adela the younger. Judd states it in his own
words ("My father had a younger sister, Adela"), Wendell Frayne's family notes state it
("My uncle Emil Rennick... My mother, Adela Rennick, was his younger sister"), and the
curator's provenance note states it independently of both. No source disputes it.

**B2.** Judd Rennick was Adela's nephew — her brother Emil's son. Established by three
independent lines: Judd's own account of his parentage; Wendell Frayne's notes ("He was
Emil's son, which made him my mother's nephew and my first cousin"); and the Society's
accession note ("Judd Rennick's father, Emil, and Adela... were brother and sister").
Two things point elsewhere and both are answered. Ruth Frayne calls him "my father's uncle"
and Vesta Cobb calls Ruth "his grand-niece" — courtesy kinship, which Wendell corrects
explicitly and by name ("She used to call him uncle and he never corrected her"). And the
Society's wrappers attribute the field book to "J. Rennick" and the night book to
"A. Rennick," which would reverse the two writers; the curator himself flags this as
inconsistent with the initials inside, and the internal evidence settles it — the field book
is signed "— A.R." and came down through Adela's son, the night book was kept at the depot
and came from Judd's estate.

**B3.** First cousins once removed. Derivation: Adela and Emil were siblings; Wendell is
Adela's son and Judd is Emil's son, so Wendell and Judd are first cousins; Ruth is Wendell's
daughter, so she stands to Judd as the child of a first cousin — first cousin once removed.
Wendell sets out exactly this chain and warns that the "uncle" usage will otherwise end up on
a family chart.

**B4.** Warren Tice — resident engineer on the viaduct and afterward the Cadder Valley's chief
engineer at Ninestone (retired spring 1931, died 1937). Dorsey says so in the first person and
Wendell Frayne's notes agree; the two sources that make Dorsey his *nephew* (Vesta Cobb, and
the school pamphlet compiled from town memory) are the same piece of town talk twice.
Why it matters to January 1936: the man who bet five dollars that the bridge would not speak
was the son of the engineer who overruled Adela Rennick's objection and let the nest be set
a mark shy — so the sound Dorsey was betting against was his father's error. It also explains
the shape of his testimony: he opens the tape insisting his father "had no hand in that
bridge" and "was in the Ninestone office the whole time it was building," then later on the
same tape recalls his father taking him out on the deck at six and telling him he had closed
the span himself in ninety-seven; and he refuses even to repeat the story that Tice said at
his retirement dinner, "we set that nest a mark shy and I have listened to it ever since."

**B5.** Chain of custody into Ruth Frayne's hands in 1962:
- *Field book* — written by Adela Rennick 1895-1901, kept by her from 1901 until her death in
  1959, then to her son Wendell Frayne, who took it down off the shelf and put it into Ruth's
  hands himself when she said she was going out to the creek. (To the Society, 1963.)
- *Night book* — kept by Judd Rennick at the depot 1912-1956, lent by him personally to Ruth
  in 1962 at his kitchen table and returned to him afterward. (To the Society from his estate,
  1969.)
- *Weather record* — begun by Peter Wexler, continued by his daughter Lettie from 1926; Lettie
  gave Ruth the whole of it in four cardboard boxes in 1962 without asking what it was for or
  taking a receipt, and Ruth brought every box back. (To the Society 1967, after Lettie's
  death.)
- *Bly County ledgers* — the Tolliver Lumber Company's books, kept by Pearl Tolliver Nace and
  deposited by her at the Bly County Historical Rooms in 1938, where they sat uncatalogued by
  any reader for twenty-four years; Ruth wrote in 1962 and they sent photostats of the two
  relevant pages by return. (Arithmetic check: 1938 + 24 = 1962, the year of her letter.)
So three came to her through her own family and the neighbours' kindness, and the fourth by
post; the connecting person is Ruth, and the connecting fact is that she was Adela's
granddaughter and Judd's first cousin once removed.

## Section C — Math

### C1. The center span

Movement rule: **one inch in 300 feet for 40 degrees** (Adela Rennick's field book, front
matter). The span is 300 feet exactly, so one inch per 40 degrees of cooling. Span closed
12 September 1897 at **66 degrees**.

*Why 40 and not 50:* the field-book line is a quoted document, transcribed independently by
the 1962 surveyor and by the Society's curator. The 50-degree figure appears only in a memoir
written from office recollection and in a county memo. Decisive check: 2 inches at 40 degrees
per inch, taken from the 86 degrees of the 1909 re-seating, gives exactly +6 — the threshold
in the night book. The 50-degree rule gives −14, and the memo that uses it admits its answer
"is not readily reconciled with the local record."

*Why Sheet 11 called for three inches and not four:* the field-book entry of the day of
closing says "Sheet 11 calls for three," and the 1962 survey report repeats it. The four-inch
figure is a memoir and a memo; the likely contamination is that the 1954 *rocker* bearings do
give four inches each way.

**(a) As Sheet 11 specified (3 inches):**
3 in. x 40 deg/in. = 120 degrees of cooling; 66 − 120 = **−54 degrees F**.

**(b) As actually set in 1897 (2 inches):**
2 in. x 40 = 80 degrees; 66 − 80 = **−14 degrees F**.

**(c) After the August 1909 work (2 inches, re-seated at 86 degrees):**
86 − 80 = **+6 degrees F**.

**(d) Margin the 1909 work cost:**
(+6) − (−14) = **20 degrees**.

### C2. The afternoon of the wager (14 January 1936)

**(a)** Threshold is a low below 6 degrees. 38 − 6 = 32 degrees to fall; at 4 degrees an hour,
32 / 4 = 8 hours; 4:00 p.m. + 8 hours = **about midnight**. (She spoke at 1:10 a.m., about an
hour past the crossing — consistent.)

**(b)** Day's high 41 at noon; that night's low 3 below. 41 − (−3) = **44 degrees**. The second
condition requires a fall of at least 30, so **yes** — satisfied with 14 degrees to spare.

### C3. From the night book

Counts taken from the summary on the book's back page as transcribed by the curator and stated
by Judd Rennick himself: 61 nights heard, 34 winters with at least one, 9 silent winters,
5 qualifying nights that gave nothing, 66 qualifying nights in all.

**(a)** 61 / 43 = 1.4186... = **1.4 nights per logged winter**.

**(b)** 61 heard + 5 silent-but-qualifying = **66 nights**.

**(c)** 61 / 34 = 1.7941... = **1.8 nights per speaking winter**.

**Which winters, and how the ends were fixed.** Forty-three winters, from the winter of
1911-12 through the winter of 1953-54.
- *First:* the first entry is the night of 17 January 1912 (high 34, low −2, clear and still) —
  the night the sound was first heard and, on the surveyor's account and the school pamphlet's,
  the night the book was opened. The Wexler page for that date is quoted verbatim by two
  narrators.
- *Last:* the last night entered as heard is 9 February 1954, and the bearings were replaced
  the following month.
- *Check:* counting winters by their January year, 1912 through 1954 inclusive is 43 — the
  number the book itself claims. A start in 1907 (Judd's own recollection) would give 48, and
  a first sound in 1913 would put the first entry a year after the documented 17 January 1912
  page.

**Nights excluded.**
- The **three qualifying nights of the winters of 1954-55 and 1955-56** — below six degrees,
  falls of better than thirty, no sound. They are excluded because they fall after the March
  1954 bearing replacement removed the mechanism, and because they lie outside the forty-three
  winters the book's own summary is drawn over. Including them would make (b) 69 and would
  wrongly load the sufficiency test in Section D.
- No night is excluded from the 61.
- Note the rejected alternative: one narrator recalls eight silent qualifying nights and a
  total of 69. That is a single reading of the book for an hour at a kitchen table, and the
  county memo that repeats it says explicitly that it has the figure at second hand — so the
  two are one source, not two, and the transcribed back page governs.

### C4. The locomotive

**(a)** Built 1889 (Rowan works no. 1142), cut up at Sixmile in May 1929: **40 years old**.
(The month of building is not recorded; 40 is the year difference.)

**(b)** 2,150 / 8,600 = 0.25 = **25 percent** exactly.
*Working on the price:* the stock-book entry of 19 June 1901 reads $2,150.00, and is quoted
verbatim from two separate holdings (the chief clerk's own copy of the entry, and the
photostat lodged with the Society). The $2,510 figure appears in a granddaughter's memoir of
her mother's ledgers and in a newspaper feature that attributes its figure to the county
engineer — whose own report quotes $2,150, so the paper is a misquotation and not an
independent vote. It is a digit transposition. The exact quarter is a further check.

**(c)** Bought 19 June 1901, set out behind the shop in September 1928 when the eight-coupled
engine arrived: **27 years** (27 years 3 months). Her own family's account says "twenty-seven
years she worked for us," which is itself the check that rejects that account's stray "That
was in 1902" — 1902 + 27 = 1929, but she was already under a tarpaulin in the winter of
1928-29. She was also advertised as twelve years old, and 1889 + 12 = 1901.

### C5. Distances

**(a) Total length of the viaduct: 540 feet.**
120 + 300 + 120 = 540. Two sources give the component spans and both sum to 540; the two that
say "six hundred feet" give no components, and one of them was compiled from the other's back
files. Arithmetic settles it.

**(b) Fall of the line from quarry to viaduct: about 1,045 feet.**
Ashby quarry is milepost 31, the viaduct milepost 22, so 9 miles (the station agent says "the
nine miles off the quarry"; a county memo's "eight miles" contradicts the mileposts the same
memo gives). 9 x 5,280 = 47,520 feet; 47,520 x 0.022 = **1,045.44 feet**.

**(c) Movement of the center span between the record extremes: 2.8 inches.**
Record high 101 degrees (July 1936, agreed by all three sources that give it); record low
11 below (January 1918). Range = 101 − (−11) = 112 degrees. 112 / 40 = **2.8 inches**.
*Resolution of the low:* the keeper of the weather record states 11 below in her own signed
account, and the record's own index sheet, accessioned with the boxes, says 11 below. The
13-below figure appears in the surveyor's talk and in a newspaper feature that got its
material from the surveyor — one chain, not two. This is my least secure resolution; if 13
below is preferred the answer is 114 / 40 = 2.85 inches.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. Necessary, sufficient, both, or neither

**Necessary but not sufficient.** Necessity: every one of the sixty-one logged nights had a
low below six degrees and stood at the bottom of a fall of thirty degrees or more from that
day's high, with no exception in forty-three winters — and the keeper says he went through the
book specifically looking for one. This is not merely his own bookkeeping: the Kettle Bench
weather record, kept three miles off by a family related to nobody in the story and containing
not one word about the bridge, agrees with his book on every one of the sixty-one nights, and
its keeper independently spot-checked a dozen of his dates in her own pages and found them
right to the degree. Two records kept for unrelated purposes cannot both be wrong in the same
direction sixty-one times.

Insufficiency: five nights in the same book met both conditions and produced nothing at all,
by the book's own back-page count. Five counterexamples out of sixty-six qualifying nights
kill sufficiency outright. (The three qualifying-but-silent nights of the two winters after
March 1954 also fail sufficiency, but trivially — the mechanism had been removed by then, so
they are not evidence about the 1912-1954 regime.)

Add a third condition — that a daytime thaw above about forty degrees must have intervened
since the last report — and the set becomes jointly sufficient within that regime: all five
silent nights sit inside cold spells in which she had already spoken and nothing had got above
forty in between.

### D2. Why it cannot have been Locomotive No. 9

**Reason 1 — she was sold out of the county alive and scrapped two hundred miles away
(clerk's son; corroborated by the lumber company's granddaughter and by the Society's
photostats).** The stock book of 19 June 1901 records No. 9 sold to the Tolliver Lumber
Company, "delivered on her own wheels" — that is, she steamed north under her own power three
years after she is supposed to have been lying under the gravel. Tolliver renumbered her 4 and
cut her up at Sixmile in May 1929. The sound went on for twenty-five years after she was
scrap.

**Reason 2 — nobody died in the 1898 wreck and the engine never left the deck level (field
book page 71; the *Ninestone Sentinel* of 8 March 1898; Emil Rennick, who was on the ground
that morning).** She went off the iron at the *north approach* at 7:20 in the morning, in
daylight, with half the county looking at her; Lidell's arm was broken and Sherrod was
scalded; nine days to rerail her. The ghost's whole premise — a drowned crew under the gravel —
never happened.

**Reason 3 — the chronology is wrong by fourteen years (Wexler weather record; night book).**
The sound did not begin the winter after the wreck. In the twelve years between the opening
and the 1909 re-decking there were seven nights below six degrees at Kettle Bench, four of
them at the bottom of thirty-degree falls, and nobody heard anything on any of them. The first
report was 17 January 1912 — eleven years after the engine left the county.

**Reason 4 — the behaviour of the sound is wrong for an engine (station agent; storekeeper;
county engineer's memo).** One report, never repeating within a night, always between midnight
and four, never in summer, arriving up the continuous rail before it arrived in the air and
rattling the telegraph sounder first. A locomotive "working the grade" would be periodic. A
locomotive under water would not work a grade at all.

**Reason 5 — it stopped the month the bearings were changed and nothing else was changed
(storekeeper; newspaper; county memo).** The 1909 deck was left in place in March 1954;
only the bearings came out. Ghosts do not answer to a requisition.

*Why these are independent:* Reason 1 rests on two companies' ledgers; Reason 2 on a
contemporaneous newspaper, a contemporaneous field-book page and an eyewitness; Reason 3 on a
farm weather record kept by non-relatives for agricultural reasons; Reason 4 on the physics of
what people heard, using no document at all; Reason 5 on the dated coincidence of the
rebuilding. Strike any four and the fifth still stands.

*Reasons that survive without the 1962 survey:* all of them. The survey added only the absence
of iron in the bed and the recovered pedestal. Reason 1 is the cleanest — the sale entry was
written in 1901 and was quoted in a memoir composed in 1958, four years before anyone walked
the dry creek. Reason 2 is next: the newspaper notice is from 1898.

### D3. Did the objection have anything to do with her leaving?

**Cannot be determined from the sources** — and what documentation exists leans against it.

What is established: the objection was entered on 12 September 1897 and her last day was
15 May 1901 (three years and eight months later, not four years — the "four years" is a
memoirist's rounding). The letter of 30 April 1901 is short and survives in two independent
transcriptions: "The engineering party is reduced entire on the 15th May next, and your time
is paid to that day. Mr. Tice asks that his recommendation be entered upon your card." A
printed office slip of 20 July 1901 re-engaged two of that party from 1 August for the Ashby
extension — J. Corliss, rodman, and F. Wain, chainman. She was not re-engaged. She kept the
letter and the slip folded together for fifty-eight years.

Why that is not enough. The affirmative case is one memoirist's inference — Tice "never
forgave that entry" — offered with the explicit admission "I have nothing in writing on the
point and I do not pretend to... there are things you know from the temperature of a room."
Against it: the party was reduced entire, so she needed no special reason to go; the two men
recalled were a rodman and a chainman and the extension work was field work, while she was the
only computer the road ever employed and it never employed another; and Tice asked that his
own recommendation be entered on her card, which is not what a man does to someone he is
punishing. Her keeping the two papers together shows that *she* connected them, which is
evidence about her mind and none about the company's reason. The construction files burned in
1911, so no personnel record can now be produced either way. No document connects the entry to
the discharge, and no living witness ever claimed to have seen the connection made.

## Section E — Contradictions

### E1. Every direct factual contradiction found

Format: claim A vs claim B — who — resolution — method. **CBD** = the sources cannot settle it.

**E-1. Movement rule: 1 in./300 ft per 40 degrees vs per 50 degrees.** Field book (quoted by
Ruth Frayne and by the curator) vs Harlan Pike and H. L. Quarles. → **40 degrees.** Method:
quoted document, plus the decisive check that only 40 reproduces the six-degree threshold in
the night book; Quarles's own memo concedes his answer will not reconcile with the record.
*Same wrong value in two narrators (Pike, Quarles).*

**E-2. Sheet 11: three inches vs four inches.** Field book page 62 and the 1962 survey report
vs Pike and Quarles. → **three inches.** Method: a document written on the day of the closing
by the person doing the arithmetic, quoted verbatim in two independent places, over a memoir
written sixty years later and a memo whose other numbers for this bridge (the coefficient, the
eight-mile grade) are demonstrably wrong. *Same wrong value in two narrators.* What breaks the
tie beyond the document: the likely source of the error is visible — the 1954 rocker bearings
really do give four inches each way, and both men were writing after 1954. Caveat recorded:
Quarles claims his office holds the contract drawings, which is the one documentary claim on
the four-inch side; Pike's own memoir says the construction files burned in 1911, so his figure
cannot be from the drawings.

**E-3. Nights the bridge spoke: 61 vs 57.** Night book back page (Judd Rennick; curator's
transcription; Ruth Frayne; Lettie Wexler) vs Dorsey Tice and the school pamphlet. → **61.**
Method: the document. *Same wrong value in two narrators (Tice, pamphlet)* — broken by the
transcribed back page, and by non-independence: the pamphlet was compiled from "the town's
memory," and the store was where the town's memory lived.

**E-4. Winter split: 34 spoke / 9 silent vs 30 / 13.** Same pairing as E-3. → **34 / 9.**
Method: the document. Arithmetic cannot break this one — both pairs sum to 43 — which is
exactly why the back page has to.

**E-5. Silent qualifying nights: 5 (total 66) vs 8 (total 69).** Night book back page and
Judd Rennick vs Lettie Wexler and Quarles. → **5 and 66.** *Same wrong value in two narrators*
— broken by the transcribed back page, and by the fact that Quarles states in his own memo
that he has the figure "at second hand" and has not examined the book, so he is not an
independent second voice; Lettie Wexler read the book once, for an hour, thirty-odd years ago.

**E-6. Total length: 540 feet vs 600 feet.** Pike and Quarles (both giving 120 + 300 + 120)
vs the *Sentinel* and the school pamphlet. → **540.** Method: arithmetic on the components.
*Same wrong value in two narrators (newspaper, pamphlet)* — broken by the sum, and by the
pamphlet's own statement that it was compiled partly from the newspaper's back files, so the
600 is one error printed twice.

**E-7. Sale price: $2,150 vs $2,510.** Stock-book entry (Pike; curator's photostat; Ruth
Frayne) vs Alma Nace and the *Sentinel*. → **$2,150.** *Same wrong value in two narrators* —
broken by the document in two holdings; by the ratio (2,150 is exactly 25 percent of the
$8,600 first cost, 2,510 is 29.2 percent); and by the newspaper's own attribution, which

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What actually produced the sound

**What produced the sound.** The 300-foot center span was pinned at its north end and free at
the south, where it rode on a nest of rollers in a cast pedestal, so that the whole of its
thermal movement was taken at one end. Steel shortens as it cools at one inch in three hundred
feet for forty degrees — for this span, one inch per forty degrees. When the span cooled past
the point at which the nest ran out of travel, the shoe hung up. Contraction did not stop; the
strain went into the members of the truss and into the pins and was stored there until it
overcame the friction of the seized nest, at which point the bearing released completely and
the span took up its new position in one movement.

**The character of the sound** follows from that release being sudden and total: the stored
energy is discharged at a stroke instead of spent by degrees, so it is one deep report of short
duration, not a ring, not a roll, and never repeated within a night. It travels far better
along a mile of continuous rail than through air, which is why the telegraph sounder at the
depot rattled in its bracket first, then the floor moved, then the noise arrived — and why men
in bed at the Falls felt it in their feet before they heard it.

**The threshold, and why six.** The nest was set to a two-inch mark. Two inches is eighty
degrees of cooling. In August 1909 a bridge gang jacked both shoes and re-seated them to that
same mark with the steel standing at eighty-six degrees on the north-pier thermometer at four
o'clock. Eighty-six less eighty is six above zero. That is not an approximation fitted to the
data; it is the arithmetic of the metal, and it lands exactly on the figure the night book
gives — below six she spoke, at six and a half or seven never.

**The second condition.** A slow decline lets the shoe creep: friction is overcome repeatedly
in small increments at low stored strain, so nothing accumulates and nothing releases. A fall
of thirty degrees or more inside a day carries the bearing past its freedom faster than it can
relieve itself, so the strain is stored instead of spent. Hence the valley's "killing nights" —
a soft day, mud in the road, and then the bottom out of it — and hence three weeks at zero
with never a sound.

**Why some qualifying nights were silent.** Once released, the shoe stands at the far end of
its travel with nothing left to release. It is returned toward the warm end only by warming —
in practice a daytime thaw of some hours above about forty degrees. Successive nights inside a
single cold spell are therefore silent however cold they are. All five of the book's silent
qualifying nights sit in freezes where she had already spoken and nothing had got above forty
in between; the keeper worked this rule out himself and called it the only guess he would
allow.

**Why it began when it did.** As built in 1897 the span was closed at sixty-six degrees on a
two-inch mark, so it would have bound at fourteen below — a temperature this valley does not
reach. The error was therefore invisible: a wrong bridge behaved exactly like a right one,
because the difference between them lay in weather that never came. In the twelve years to
1909 the Kettle Bench record shows seven nights below six degrees, four of them at the bottom
of thirty-degree falls, all silent, and the coldest reading of those years was nine below on
4 February 1899 — cold, but well short of fourteen below. The August 1909 re-seating at
eighty-six degrees moved the binding point from fourteen below to six above, a loss of twenty
degrees. Two mild winters followed, neither going below nine above (coldest 3 February 1911).
The first night under six degrees after the gang went home was 17 January 1912 — high 34, low
2 below, clear and still, a fall of thirty-six degrees — and that is the night the sound began
and the night the night book was opened.

**Why it stopped permanently.** In March 1954 the road was rehabilitated for diesel power and
the roller nest was replaced with rocker bearings giving four inches of travel each way, which
cannot be exhausted at any temperature this valley records. The last night heard was 9 February
1954, a month before. Three nights in the two following winters answered both conditions and
gave nothing. Nothing else was altered: the 1909 deck was not disturbed and stands over the
span yet.

**The competing explanation the sources offer, and why it fails.** It is the added dead load.
The 1909 re-decking put eighty-five-pound steel and new ties on the bridge and left the deck
forty tons heavier, and a roller nest takes that increase directly on the rollers. Harlan Pike
puts it flatly — "Forty tons is forty tons... I say the weight did it" — and H. L. Quarles
calls it "at least as probable" and notes that the onset date "is consistent with the
re-decking and with nothing else in the record."

It fails on four counts. First, weight predicts no temperature at all. It cannot generate a
threshold, still less the particular number six, whereas the re-seating generates it to the
degree from 86 − (2 x 40). Second, the load was untouched in March 1954 — Quarles's own memo
records that the 1909 deck was not disturbed and that "nothing was altered in March 1954 but
the bearings themselves" — yet the sound stopped dead and never returned. If forty tons had
caused it, changing the bearing while leaving the forty tons in place would not have cured it.
Third, the onset is not merely "consistent with" 1909: the sound did not begin in 1909, or in
1910, or in 1911. It waited two mild winters and began on the first night the valley went below
six degrees. That is the signature of a threshold, not of a crushing load. Fourth, over
forty-three winters the phenomenon tracks temperature and rate of fall exactly, in two records
kept independently — behaviour proper to a travel limit, not to bearing pressure. (The two
folk alternatives fail on the same ground: the locomotive is disposed of in Section D2, and the
store's "ice in the gorge" gives no reason for the sound to keep to killing nights, as Vesta
Cobb herself pointed out to the man who proposed it.)

**The physical evidence recovered in 1962.** The discarded cast-iron south pedestal, found in
the gravel forty feet below the south abutment where the 1954 gang had thrown it over, with the
chisel setting mark cut into the roller seat — measured three times with a steel tape at two
inches, against the three inches Sheet 11 called for. It is the only surviving physical record
of the setting of 1897, the construction files having burned in 1911. With it goes the negative
evidence: eight hundred feet of dry creek bed containing no locomotive and nothing of iron
larger than a spike.

## Section G — Summary

### G1.

The Cadder Valley Railroad's Sallow Creek viaduct opened in 1897. Its center span closed at 66
degrees on a roller nest set two inches short of the drawing; Adela Rennick, the party's
computer, objected, was overruled, wrote it down. Harmless until August 1909, when a gang
re-seated the nest at 86 degrees, leaving six degrees of cold travel. From 17 January 1912 the
span bound and let go with one report on fast-falling nights below six degrees; the valley
blamed a locomotive it believed lay in the gorge. New bearings ended it in 1954. Four records
(field book, night book, weather record, lumber ledgers) held the error, the effect, the
weather, the engine's alibi. Ruth Frayne joined them, 1962.

*(119 words.)*
